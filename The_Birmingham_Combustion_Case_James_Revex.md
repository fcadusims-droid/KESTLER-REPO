# THE BIRMINGHAM COMBUSTION CASE

## Unsealed Industrial-Medical Records Concerning James Revex
### Birmingham and the Western Canal Districts, 1952–1958

> There are men who survive a war.
> There are men who carry it home.
> Birmingham made room for a third kind.

---

## I. The City That Kept Burning

Birmingham did not heal after the war.

It resumed production.

The furnaces came back before the widows stopped wearing black. The foundries reopened before the men returning from Europe and North Africa learned how to sleep without flinching. Women left the aircraft sheds at Castle Bromwich with ruined nerves and yellowed fingernails. Children grew tall in streets where silence still sounded like an air raid waiting to happen.

The public language was reconstruction.

The private language was exhaustion.

By 1952 the city had become very good at hiding damage inside useful things. Cracked men returned to machine shops. Burned women became clerks. Boys with weak hearts were placed near thermometers, ledgers, doors and clocks: anything light enough not to kill them.

Hadley & Sons Steel Works stood west of the Soho Loop, on the old main line of the canal, where the city stops being a city of a thousand small trades and starts being something heavier. Birmingham proper made buttons, pens, jewellery, gun barrels and bicycle lamps. Hadley's made billet, and made it in furnaces that had been old before the first war.

It was neither the largest nor the most infamous site on that stretch of water. Its owners were careful in public and cheap in private. Its accident books were incomplete, though not unusually incomplete by the standards of the time.

To men who worked there, the place had a voice: a layered thunder of belts, pistons, chains, fans, hammers, whistles and molten metal. It was said a man could lose his own thoughts inside that noise.

James Revex was one of the few who seemed to go there looking for them.

## II. The Revex Household

### Thomas Revex

Thomas Revex worked metal for most of his adult life and died beneath it.

He was employed intermittently by foundries, repair yards and later Hadley & Sons, depending on contracts and disputes. Surviving wage entries describe long shifts in heat-intensive departments. He was called up in the spring of 1940 and released back to foundry work in the autumn of 1941 under the schedule of reserved occupations, the Midlands needing his hands more than the army did.

The men who remembered him described him as blunt, tired and reliable. None called him gentle. None called him cruel.

He believed quiet made boys stronger.

This was not unusual.

In the Revex house, quiet was first discipline. Later it became medicine.

Thomas died in 1944 at a repair yard in Smethwick. The exact mechanism differs between accounts: one says a support gave way, another says a load slipped, another says a foreman demanded speed where caution was required. A claim was opened under the Workmen's Compensation Act and abandoned within the year. No award was made.

James was twelve.

At the funeral, he did not cry.

By evening his nose had bled through two cloths and onto the kitchen floor.

The family called it shock.

The attending physician called it vascular strain.

James called it nothing.

### Elizabeth Revex

Elizabeth Revex stitched cloth in a textile factory and later took in mending when factory work weakened her chest. After her son's early cardiac episodes she altered the household around him.

Doors were padded with strips of rag.

The kettle was lifted before it whistled.

Arguments moved outside.

Visitors were told not to startle the boy.

When James laughed too loudly, her hands went white before her face changed.

Witnesses describe Elizabeth as devoted, careful and increasingly silent. Several women from Handsworth later recalled that she developed the habit of speaking around James rather than to him whenever bad news had to be carried through the house.

It was an act of love.

It trained him to understand love as a hand over the mouth.

Elizabeth died in February 1950 after a respiratory illness worsened during a bad winter. The local account is simple: she collapsed in the house while James was present. Neighbours were close enough to hear a scream.

No scream came.

When help arrived, Elizabeth Revex was dead and her son was sitting on the floor with her hand in both of his.

One neighbour claimed James's lips were moving.

No sound came out.

## III. The Boy Before the Engine

Early medical summaries agree on three points: congenital cardiac weakness, dangerous stress response and poor long-term prognosis.

What the summaries fail to capture is the condition built around the condition.

James Revex grew up under restrictions that turned ordinary childhood into a series of prohibited impulses. He was warned against shouting, running, sobbing, panic, exertion, sudden joy and grief in excess. He was not told not to feel. He was told his body might punish him if he did.

At eight years old he nearly died after screaming with joy when his father came home on leave. The sound was brief. The attack lasted hours. Afterward he began counting his own pulse in bed.

At twelve he learned that grief could rupture blood vessels before it found tears.

At fifteen he began drawing machines.

The drawings survive, though not in any collection that would admit to holding them. They fill a black cloth-bound notebook of the kind sold for stock-taking, and they are not the drawings of a boy who liked engines. They are the drawings of a boy who had been told his heart was a fault and who had spent four years designing a replacement.

Factories with crowns.

Engines large enough to stand inside.

And on the fourteenth leaf, undated, in pencil pressed hard enough to emboss the reverse: a human chest cavity, opened, containing a four-cylinder block, a bronze valve gallery and a second heart mounted above it in brass.

The lettering beneath it reads: *it doesn't get tired.*

By eighteen, after Elizabeth's death, James had become a young man trained to survive by containment.

He was not calm.

He was compressed.

## IV. Hadley & Sons Steel Works

James Revex entered Hadley & Sons as a temperature monitor in April 1950.

It was a position designed for someone who could read, record and stay out of the way. He sat in an isolated booth above the lower seam with a clipboard, watched gauges, noted fluctuations and called for senior workers when readings moved beyond permitted limits. The job was sedentary enough to be considered safe.

Those who worked with him remembered the contradiction.

He was pale and narrow-shouldered, easily winded by stairs. Yet during meal breaks he drifted toward the furnaces whenever no one stopped him. He stood close enough to feel the heat on his face, eyes half-closed, as though listening to a sermon.

One worker, Albert Goss, gave a statement in 1953:

> "Revex was quiet, aye, but not like a shy lad. More like a boiler with the pressure kept down. You could see it in his jaw. Always counting something. Always listening to the furnace as if it were talking back."

In February 1952 a physician at the Dudley Road infirmary told James he might have less than a year. The statement does not appear in the formal medical packet, but two witnesses heard him repeat it in different forms.

One said he laughed.

One said he looked relieved.

By early March several workers noticed him standing too close to active furnace lines.

No supervisor filed a warning.

The booth kept a personal locker. James kept the notebook in it.

## V. March 15, 1952

The incident occurred at approximately 11:47 AM.

Hadley & Sons attributed the blast to pressure failure aggravated by suspected sabotage. Union men denied the charge. Maintenance logs suggest valve fatigue at the lower seam, which had been reported twice in the preceding eleven months and not acted upon.

One erased line in a recovered ledger mentions unusual heat shimmer at the lower seam before rupture.

The shimmer was a bleed valve standing open where no bleed valve had any business being open.

The bleed valve on number three regulator sat four feet below the monitor's booth, on a walkway no monitor had cause to use. It was designed to vent pressure gradually under supervision. Opened fully and left open, with the seam already fatigued, it produced exactly the failure that occurred, at a delay of between eight and twelve minutes.

James Revex opened it at eleven thirty-five and went back up the ladder to his booth to wait.

He did not tell anyone.

He had eleven months to live and he had decided not to take them.

The explosion broke windows three streets away. Men described the sound as a furnace tearing its own throat open. Molten fragments struck the far wall.

Leonard Pace, aged forty-one, charge hand, died at the seam.

Douglas Weir, aged twenty-three, died four hours later at the General.

Four were injured. Eleven more were treated for burns and smoke inhalation.

James Revex should have been among the dead. His body was not found in the first recovery sweep. Nor was he admitted to any local hospital under his own name.

At 2:10 PM a covered stretcher was seen being moved down toward the lower workshops by Silas Crowther and two men who later claimed not to know what they were carrying.

At 2:35 PM the basement doors were locked.

At 3:00 PM Crowther requisitioned copper tubing, surgical alcohol, insulating cloth, telegraph wire and the engine block from a dismantled Morris Minor that had been standing on blocks in the fitters' yard since before the war.

No procurement form explains why.

On 2 April 1952 Hadley & Sons entered James Revex in the company register as *presumed destroyed, no remains recovered.* The entry was never amended.

Everything that followed, he did as a dead man.

## VI. Silas Crowther

Silas Crowther was sixty-seven in 1952.

He had learned his trade in France in 1917, in the Central Workshops at Erin, where the first tanks were dragged in broken and sent back out running. He was thirty-two years old and he spent eighteen months inside machines that had been built to carry men into fire and had not been built to bring them out.

He came home with a talent for repair and a conviction he never argued in public: that a body is a housing, and a housing can be improved.

His son Robert was born in 1921.

Robert Crowther had a weak heart from childhood. He survived illness, adolescence and training. He died on the Dunkirk perimeter on 1 June 1940, aged nineteen, without being struck. His officer's letter said he had simply gone down in the dunes under shelling and not got up again.

Silas never forgave the heart.

Men said he blamed the army. Later they said he blamed himself. His private papers, recovered years after the first Revex reports, suggest something more specific: he blamed flesh for failing where machinery could have been reinforced.

He made his first attempt in 1948.

He made his second on the afternoon of 15 March 1952.

James Revex entered Silas Crowther's hands as a dying young man.

What emerged from the basement was neither patient nor corpse.

Crowther never filed a medical claim. He never requested recognition. He never admitted to surgery under questioning. His only surviving direct explanation came from a torn page attributed to his workshop ledger:

> "The boy's own ticker had no discipline left in it. It fluttered, panicked, pleaded and failed. A heart like that is a traitor to the spirit inside. I did not remove the boy. I removed the weakness."

Another page, found much later, contains only three words repeated down the margin:

> Robert would live.

## VII. The Crowther Procedure

No recognised medical authority has authenticated the procedure.

The reconstruction relies on industrial diagrams, witness descriptions, recovered components, scorch patterns, Sarah Mitchell's maintenance records and the repeated survival of James Revex in conditions inconsistent with ordinary human anatomy.

### The unit

The engine-heart was built around the 847cc block of a Morris Minor of the first series, a car built between 1928 and 1934 and long out of production by the time one was standing derelict in Hadley's yard.

It was not the smallest block available in British salvage. An Austin Seven is smaller by a hundred cubic centimetres and there were ten times as many of them on blocks in ten times as many yards.

It was simply the one already inside the works, forty yards from the man doing the cutting, on an afternoon when nothing could be fetched from anywhere.

Everything about the engine-heart follows from that. It is not a design. It is what was within reach.

The block was stripped to two working cylinders and rebored. Its ignition was retained. Its gearbox, sump and road fittings were discarded.

Visible materials included copper, brass and bronze, consistent with post-war salvage availability. Pressure regulation was carried by bronze valves from naval scrap, believed to be torpedo stock, bought by weight in Wolverhampton in 1947 and held in Crowther's stores for five years.

The original car engine had no water pump. It circulated coolant by thermosiphon — by heat alone, hot fluid rising, cool fluid falling, no mechanism to force it. Crowther kept that arrangement because he had no room for a pump.

This single decision governs almost everything that follows.

### The fitting

The engine did not sit inside James like a prosthetic.

It was fused through him.

The sternum was removed and replaced with a bronze yoke. Ribs three through eight were cut, braced and partly reshaped around the housing. A strap of flat steel was run from the yoke down the line of the spine and anchored at the pelvis, so that load taken by the arms passes through the frame and not through the vertebrae.

Valve lines were connected to the major arteries at the aortic root. Improvised neural contacts were threaded along damaged tissue and into the intercostal nerves, which is why the scarring across his chest runs in pale branching lines rather than a surgical seam.

Steam piping forms part of the venting system and surfaces at four apertures: two at the upper chest, one at the base of the throat, one below the left scapula.

The remaining biological heart tissue was not fully removed. It was absorbed into a hybrid rhythm and still contributes, weakly, to circulation at the lowest regimes.

The result was not clean.

It was not elegant.

It worked.

### Consequences of mass

James Revex weighed nine stone four before the procedure.

He weighs nineteen stone six after it.

He cannot swim, and knows it.

He has gone through two floors: a rope-loft boarding in 1954 and the upper landing of a lodging house in Nechells in 1956. He does not use ladders rated for one man. He climbs chimney courses and iron fire escapes, which is why the rooftop sightings cluster on old warehouse stock and never on houses.

He cannot ride a bicycle, cannot be carried on a standard stretcher, and in 1955 he put the rear springs of a police van flat on the axle simply by getting into the back of it.

### Limits of the frame

The engine can deliver more than the man can transmit.

This is the single most important fact about him and the one he understands least.

At full surge the unit produces force well beyond anything the human shoulder girdle is built to carry. The bracing protects the spine. Nothing protects the arms.

When James lifted the fallen slab off a child in Aston in September 1954, he tore both shoulders out of their sockets, ruptured the long head of the left biceps and fractured the right humerus at the neck. He put the slab down, walked two hundred yards, and collapsed in an entry off Rocky Lane.

He was unusable for nine weeks.

Every extraordinary thing he has ever done has cost him some part of the body that did it.

### Observed characteristics

| Category | Recorded manifestation |
|---|---|
| Resting heat | Skin temperature between 99 and 103 °F at the lowest regime; never normal |
| Chest activity | Visible pistons, valve gallery, pressure pulse and spark discharge through the housing aperture |
| Circulation | Blood contaminated with a dark oil-like fluid; separates on standing |
| Respiration | Steam venting through mouth, nostrils and the four fitted vents |
| Nerve response | Mechanical acceleration under emotional stimulus, involuntary |
| Pain response | Heightened during Stall; suppressed and distorted during Redline |
| Structural limit | Shoulder girdle and forearms; the frame outlasts the limbs |
| Principal hazards | Quench, seam fracture, fuel exhaustion, charge exhaustion |

## VIII. The Two Burns

Every serious error made about James Revex during his lifetime comes from the same mistake: treating the engine as one system.

It is two.

### The sustaining burn

The sustaining burn is what keeps him alive. It runs continuously, at a low and stable rate, and it is fed by ordinary combustible material.

It is fuel in the plain sense. It can be bought, stolen, rationed, poisoned or withheld.

It does not require emotion, consent or consciousness. It ran through the spring of 1952 while James was sedated, insensible or strapped to a table. It runs while he sleeps.

Left alone, on a full charge of fuel, the sustaining burn will keep a body going indefinitely.

That is all it will do.

### The surge burn

The surge burn is what makes him dangerous.

It cannot be lit by fuel. It requires emotional charge, which the unit draws directly through the neural contacts and consumes in the process. What is spent does not go anywhere. It is burned.

Every account of impossible strength, every broken wall, every melted restraint, every thing the city has made a legend of, is surge.

Fuel determines how long he lasts.

Charge determines what he is capable of.

He can be full of one and empty of the other, and both states have killed men.

## IX. Fuel

James Revex eats at roughly three times the intake of a working man and is always hungry. In the years of rationing this was a problem solved dishonestly and without apology.

He also takes oil directly.

A threaded charging port sits below the left floating rib, closed by a brass cap on a short chain. It admits a light mineral and castor blend of Crowther's specification, warmed first.

In ordinary use — waking, walking, a fight a fortnight — he takes between six and nine pints a week. A bad week costs more than a woman's wage.

The blend is the reason for the glycerin and castor oil crates traced to the Wolverhampton yard, and the reason a man who was rumoured to drink oil was being laughed at for the wrong reason.

He does not drink it.

He is fed it, through a port, by someone else, while lying still.

| Regime | Fuel draw |
|---|---|
| Banked | Under half a pint in twenty-four hours |
| Steady | Roughly one pint in twenty-four hours |
| Drawing | One pint in under two hours |
| Redline | A full day's fuel in under ninety seconds |

Fuel exhaustion does not kill quickly. It walks him down the ladder instead, one regime at a time, over days: Steady to Banked, Banked to a man who cannot get off a floor, and only at the very end into Stall.

The Stall at the bottom of that descent cannot be restarted, because a spark at the ignition contact does nothing to a unit with nothing left to light.

Crowther's estimate, entered twice in the ledger and never corrected: four days from the last charge of oil to the last minute, and the last minute is the only part of it that is quick.

The oil tins left at alley mouths after word went round that he had been hurt were not superstition and were not offerings. The men leaving them had worked out exactly what he was.

Hadley & Sons management denied knowledge of the practice.

The tins disappeared anyway.

## X. Charge

Emotion is not fuel. Emotion is what the unit spends to exceed itself.

The following table appears in no formal medical report. It was assembled by Sarah Mitchell between 1953 and 1957 from her own maintenance notes and from Crowther's private classifications, and it is the closest thing to a specification the case contains.

| Charge | Mechanical behaviour | Recovery of charge | Human aftermath |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anger, hatred | High output, stable, sustains Redline longest | About forty hours | Apathy, moral flatness, irritability without feeling, for as long as the charge is down |
| Fear | Long endurance at Drawing; will not lift, only carry | Four to six days | Reckless calm, impaired self-preservation, for as long as the charge is down |
| Grief, sadness | Low, steady, will not exceed Drawing | Effectively inexhaustible | Numbness, slow speech, dissociation; hours |
| Joy | Violent spike, highest peak output recorded, cannot be held | Four to six weeks | Depressive collapse, emotional starvation |
| Love | Surge beyond every rated limit of the unit | Months, and never to the previous level | Attachment absence, relational damage, recognition without feeling |

Grief is why he is still alive. It is the only charge he never runs out of, and it is the reason a man who has spent everything else can still drag himself home. Mitchell called it the limp fuel and wrote that he hated it more than the rest together, because using it meant admitting what was always in the tank.

Anger is why he seeks fights. Forty hours is short enough to live on.

Joy is why he stopped laughing.

Love has been recorded twice with confidence.

The first was at the Wolverhampton yard in October 1953, when a chain hoist came down across the pit and Silas Crowther was underneath it. James took the whole weight off the old man's back and held it while two fitters dragged him clear.

What that surge spent was everything James had left for the man who made him.

It is the reason he has never asked Silas about the six days in 1955, or about the notebook, or about anything else. People have read the silence as fear and some have read it as respect. It is neither. There is nothing on that side of him to speak with, and there has not been since 1953.

The second occasion was the slab in Aston in September 1954. He did not know the child. He had been three days without a fight and had nothing else in him.

What burns in a love surge is not a general capacity. It is a specific attachment, to a specific named person, and it burns from his side only. The person saved is not the person spent.

For six days afterward Sarah Mitchell reported that he responded to her as though imitating affection from memory. He knew her name, her face, what she took in her tea, the order in which she laid out her tools. He performed the whole of it correctly and felt none of it.

He had saved a child.

He had spent the thing that made the saving matter.

Her accusation survives in several retellings:

> "You burned something of mine without asking."

She was not wrong about the theft. She was wrong about the owner.

## XI. The Deficit

Charge recovers. It does not recover fully.

Mitchell reconstructed eleven Redlines between March 1952 and March 1957. The first two she had from other people's accounts and from Crowther's ledger; the rest she saw, or dressed the damage from afterward.

By the fourth she was convinced that each one took something that did not come back. Her method was crude and consistent: she kept a list of things he could still feel, and she read it to him.

The list got shorter.

By 1954 he could no longer be made to laugh.

By 1955 he had stopped being able to recall his mother's voice, though he could still describe the padded doors, the lifted kettle, the white hands. The facts stayed. The sound went.

By 1957 he could sit through the anniversary of the blast without the engine moving.

He asked her to stop reading the list in the spring of 1958. She wrote that he did not ask angrily, and that this was the point.

This is the whole horror of the case, stated plainly: the engine works. It will keep him alive for years. It simply requires, as its price, the incremental destruction of the man it is keeping.

A body maintained by expenditure of its own interior.

Crowther knew. There is no version of the ledger in which he does not know.

## XII. The Regime Ladder

The names are the workers' names. Nothing official was ever agreed.

| Regime | Skin surface | Sound | Trigger | Capability | Sustainable for | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Banked** | 99–103 °F | Slow cycling: huff, click, wheeze, turn | Default; sleep; cold | Ordinary movement, tires on stairs, weaker than a healthy man of his size | Indefinite | Fuel only, minimal |
| **Steady** | 104–110 °F | Even, audible at six feet | Waking, walking, conversation | Roughly the strength of a strong labourer; cloth stays intact; a bare hand on him reddens within a minute | Indefinite | Fuel |
| **Drawing** | 112–125 °F | Rising, uneven, teeth spark on sibilants | Any emotional stimulus, involuntary | Two to three men; bends bar stock; scorches cloth in contact; sweat vanishes before it falls | Twenty to forty minutes | Fuel heavily; charge slowly |
| **Redline** | 140 °F and above at surface | Mechanical roar, glass rings, rubber softens underfoot | Emotional flood, or deliberate provocation | Structural: doors, brickwork, vehicles. Proximity within four feet is dangerous. Restraints fail | Ninety seconds to four minutes | Fuel catastrophically; charge permanently; frame and limbs |
| **Stall** | Falls to 80 °F and below within minutes | Coughing, then nothing | Quench, fuel exhaustion, charge exhaustion, valve seizure, blunt trauma to the housing | None. Weaker than an ordinary man. Conscious and aware | Until restarted | Everything |

He does not choose the ladder. He only chooses whether to feed it.

Drawing is involuntary and arrives with the emotion, which is why Elizabeth Revex's son spent his childhood being told not to feel and his adulthood proving her right.

The step from Drawing to Redline is the only one he can refuse.

He has refused it forty or fifty times.

He has taken it eleven.

## XIII. Redline

Workers gave the state its name before any physician could describe it.

It meant the point at which James Revex stopped resembling a damaged man and became industrial weather.

During Redline his body temperature rises violently. His eyes brighten until witnesses compare them to embers, furnace mouths or signal lamps in fog. His voice distorts into a mechanical roar. Steam vents from chest and throat. Nearby glass shakes. Rubber softens beneath his boots.

The most troubling reports mention pleasure.

Not relief.

Pleasure.

Several witnesses saw him smile while burning.

One statement from a warehouse fight in 1955 remains among the clearest:

> "He was screaming like it hurt him, but his mouth — God forgive me — his mouth looked happy."

There is a mechanical reason and it makes the moral problem worse rather than better. Pain response is suppressed at Redline. The neural contacts saturate. For between ninety seconds and four minutes, a man who has been in pain since March 1952 is not in pain.

That is the thing he craves. Not the power.

The absence.

Each use leaves him ashamed, depleted, structurally damaged and more likely to seek conditions that will raise him there again.

This is why several investigators rejected the word weapon.

A weapon does not crave discharge.

## XIV. Stall

If Redline made James terrifying, Stall made him pitiful.

When the engine fails, the heat drops out of him with frightening speed. His skin greys. His hands shake. The voice that cracked windows thins to a boy's whisper. He becomes weak, gasping, almost ordinary.

Witnesses found this more disturbing than his violence.

He remains conscious throughout. This is not mercy. The biological remnant is not sufficient to support him and he knows precisely what is happening, in order, as it happens.

Restart requires external intervention within roughly four minutes: a spark applied at the ignition contact below the yoke, and the housing brought back above 90 °F. Mitchell carries the means to do it. So did Crowther. Nobody else in Birmingham does.

The first Stall of record happened in Crowther's basement in June 1952, with nobody present but Crowther, and survives only as four lines in the ledger.

The first Stall seen in public happened during a fight with union enforcers in June 1953. James had been dominating the encounter until the engine coughed audibly. One witness smelled oil before seeing blood at the corner of his mouth. His left arm began shaking. Seconds later he collapsed.

A man who had been afraid of him stepped close enough to kick him.

James reportedly said:

> "Please."

Sarah Mitchell arrived with a shotgun and ended the encounter without firing. She restarted him in the entry with four men watching and did not speak to any of them.

James survived.

The humiliation did not pass.

Afterward he took greater risks to avoid ever being seen that helpless again, which is the precise mechanism by which the case became lethal to other people.

## XV. Cooling, Cold and Water

The unit has no radiator and no pump. It circulates by thermosiphon, by temperature difference alone, and it sheds its heat through him: through skin, through the four vent apertures, through everything he touches.

He is the radiator.

This resolves the behaviour that puzzled every officer who followed him.

**Cold air is not a hazard. Cold air is relief.** In still warm weather he cannot dump heat fast enough and slides toward Drawing without any emotional cause at all. In cold he is comfortable for the first time. A frost night on a warehouse roof with a wind off the canal is the closest thing to rest available to him.

**Rain is not a hazard.** It flashes off before it reaches the housing. It is the only weather that touches him without consequence, and he stands in it deliberately.

**Standing water is lethal and the threshold is low.**

The failure is quench. A sudden volume of cold water reaching the housing collapses the thermosiphon, cracks seams, seizes valves and puts him into Stall inside three seconds.

| Exposure | Effect |
|---|---|
| Rain, mist, damp air | None. Steam. Preferred |
| Wet clothing, a soaked cloth | None at Banked or Steady; steams off at Drawing |
| A thrown pint or a splashed bucket at the limbs | Localised burn shock, pain, no mechanical failure |
| A bucket or more, striking the chest | Quench. Seam risk. Stall in seconds |
| Immersion above the ribs | Quench, seam fracture, drowning. He weighs nineteen stone and cannot swim |

He knows all of this exactly.

He stands by the canals after fights anyway. He crosses bridges late at night. He watches rain strike his arms until steam surrounds him.

One officer believed he was testing his limits.

Crowther called it reckless exposure.

Sarah Mitchell believed he was courting the idea of stopping without admitting it, and noted that he never once stood on the towpath side with the water at his back.

James, when asked, gave a simpler answer:

> "It's the only thing in this city that don't want to burn."

## XVI. Maintenance: What Sarah Mitchell Can Do

Maintenance keeps him running. It cannot keep him whole.

Sarah Mitchell can:

- draw and replace packing at the valve gallery;
- re-seat and lap the four accessible bronze valves;
- clear carbon from the two working cylinders through the upper aperture;
- charge fuel and correct the blend;
- dress the tissue at the contact scars, which suppurate under heat;
- restart him from Stall;
- adjust running trim so that Drawing arrives later and leaves sooner.

She cannot make parts.

That distinction is the hinge of his entire life and neither of them says it out loud until 1955.

Sessions run between forty minutes and four hours. They are physically invasive, mechanically delicate and emotionally dangerous. James must be partly sedated or restrained during deep adjustment, not because he is violent, but because pain and shame raise output, and a man at Drawing on a table with his housing open will cook the person leaning over him.

Mitchell keeps her tools in a locked case.

James trusts the case more than hospitals.

He can break steel.

He still has to lie still while someone else opens him.

## XVII. Manufacture: What Only Silas Crowther Can Do

Every engine-heart is hand-fitted. There is no second set. There are no drawings in circulation, no patterns lodged anywhere, no measurements written down in any hand but Crowther's — and Crowther's notation is private, partly in trade shorthand and partly in a system of his own that nobody has read successfully.

Adjustment is maintenance.

Replacement is manufacture.

When a component fractures beyond lapping and packing, it must be cast, machined and fitted to a body that no longer matches any standard, by a man who is the only person alive who knows what the inside of that body is shaped like.

The demonstration came on 9 August 1955.

During a Redline at a warehouse off Bridge Street the lower seam plate of the housing cracked across two bolt holes. Mitchell held him at Banked for eleven hours with wet cloth, a jack and a strap, and reported afterward that she had known within ninety seconds that it was past her.

Silas Crowther took the measurements on the tenth.

He began cutting on the sixteenth.

He fitted the new plate on the twenty-second.

The cutting and the fitting took six days and were honest work.

The six days between the measuring and the first cut were not. He had the stock. He had the tools. He had been a man who could repair a tank under shelling in the dark, and he gave no reason and was never asked for one.

James spent that first week at Banked, unable to stand for more than a few minutes, in a room he was not allowed to leave, being brought food by the man who had made him.

He never asked Silas about the delay.

Mitchell wrote the only sentence anyone has written about it:

> He is not kept by love and he is not kept by fear. He is kept by the fact that there is one pair of hands in England that can make him, and those hands are attached to somebody with an opinion about how he should behave.

This is the dependency. It is not metaphorical and it has a date.

## XVIII. Sarah Mitchell

Sarah Mitchell entered the case in March 1953.

She was twenty-five, mechanically trained, and already marked by war work. She had gone into the Castle Bromwich sheds at fifteen in 1943; by the end of the war, solvent and stripping-compound exposure had damaged the peripheral nerves of both hands and forearms. She can feel pressure, temperature and pain, but not always at the level required for self-protection.

This lets her touch James longer than most.

It does not make her immune. It only removes the warning.

Their first meeting occurred after a violent disruption at a factory yard. Witnesses expected her to flee or call for men. Instead she stepped close enough to see the housing and identified the block.

Her first recorded words to him were remembered by two people:

> "That's Morris Minor work, isn't it? Thirty-four, maybe. Who did this to you?"

The question mattered because it did not begin with *what are you.*

It began with *who hurt you.*

She knew the block on sight because the overhead-cam version of it had been built at the Wolseley works at Ward End, four miles from where they were standing, by people she had trained beside.

The engine in his chest was made in Birmingham.

So was he.

Their relationship was never simple enough to be called cure, romance or study. It contained all three and was cleanly none of them.

She loved him.

She examined him.

She enabled him.

She feared what fascinated her.

After the Aston slab she left for three weeks. She returned with new gloves, a valve wrench and colder eyes.

### Her hands

The damage is progressive and it is documented, because she documented it herself, in the same hand and the same book as the valve clearances.

1953: reddening during maintenance, resolving overnight.

1954: blistering across both palms, the left worse. She begins working left-handed at the gallery to spare the right for fine work.

November 1956: necrosis in the left index and middle fingers after a session she does not describe. Both taken at the second joint at the Queen's Hospital. She tells the surgeon she caught them in a press. He writes it down.

1957: grip failure in the right hand under load. She begins tying the wrench to her wrist.

Spring 1958: she starts writing the procedures out in longhand, in order, with measurements, in a clear untechnical English that a stranger could follow.

She has never told James she is doing this.

He has not asked why she is writing.

## XIX. Arthur "Wrench" Davies

Arthur Davies was not a rumour.

He was the first one.

He had been a gasworks fitter. In February 1948 a retort house collapse crushed his chest and pelvis and he was expected to die within the day. Crowther took him out of a mortuary annexe in Tipton with the assistance of two men who were paid and never traced.

Arthur consented.

This is the difference that matters, and it goes the opposite way to how the city tells it. Arthur was asked and said yes. James was unconscious.

For the first two years Arthur felt everything James feels. The 1948 unit is cruder and heavier — a bigger block run on two cylinders, a cast yoke instead of a fitted one, no throat vent, no bracing at the pelvis — but it ran on the same two burns and it took the same price.

Arthur was used. Between 1949 and 1953 he was hired, lent, and eventually kept by men who understood exactly what they had, and he spent everything he had in their service.

By 1953 there was nothing left to draw.

This is what the city mistakes for perfection.

Arthur Davies cannot Redline. He has not been able to Redline since the spring of 1953, because Redline requires charge and Arthur has none and will never make more. He runs at Steady, permanently, on fuel alone, and he will run that way for as long as somebody feeds him.

His output does not spike unpredictably because it cannot spike at all.

He does not rage because raging requires something to burn.

He kills precisely because precision is all that is left when force is gone. He uses a knife. He uses placement. He is never seen to hurry.

A Scotland Yard behavioural packet described him as *functionally animate, morally unresponsive, mechanically stable.*

Arthur considered James unfinished. He believed emotion was a fault in the process: inefficient, noisy, wasteful. James, according to one overheard exchange, called Arthur "a dead man with better posture."

Arthur did not object.

That was what horrified James.

The fear Arthur inspired was never fear of being killed.

It was fear of arriving.

Because Arthur is not a different design and he is not a better one. He is the same design, four years further down the same account, and every one of James's eleven Redlines was a payment toward becoming him.

Crowther knew this too, and kept James close and kept Arthur at a distance, and the reason is in the ledger if you are willing to read it uncharitably:

A machine that obeys proves the method.

A son who still suffers has not finished forgiving you yet.

## XX. Tommy Byrne

Thomas Byrne was twelve when he entered the case in 1955.

He was the son of a Hadley & Sons crane driver and he carried a congenital cardiac weakness close enough to James's childhood condition to alarm everybody who noticed the parallel.

Tommy first saw James during a public fight on Icknield Port Road and followed him afterward. Children fear monsters less reliably than adults.

Tommy did not see the burned skin, the housing or the smoke first.

He saw strength. He saw a man with the same doomed body remade into force.

To Tommy, James was not a warning.

He was a way out.

A witness from the canal district recalled James forcing Tommy's hand flat against the housing and saying:

> "Feel that heat? That's me staying alive. It ain't strength, kid. It's a slow fire with a name."

Tommy did not pull away.

That is why the exchange failed. He was dying too, and a slow fire with a name is a better offer than nothing.

Silas Crowther examined him twice, in October 1956 and January 1957, with the father's knowledge and the mother's refusal. He took measurements. He drew the chest. He ordered two housing blanks in bronze from a founder in Tipton and collected them himself on 14 February 1957.

James found out on the eighteenth.

---

## XXI. The Third of March

The procedure was set for the night of 3 March 1957 at the Wolverhampton yard.

Tommy was there. His father was there. Crowther had the blanks, the gallery stock, the sedative and a table.

James arrived at some point before eleven o'clock. There were four people in that room, and accounts of what was said do not agree, and the only one of the four with no reason to shade it is dead.

What is not disputed:

He did not touch Silas Crowther.

He did not touch the boy.

He gave them warning. It was one word and every account has it: *out.*

Peter Byrne had his son through the door before the second breath and took the burns himself, across the face and both forearms, and did not report them, and told the hospital at Wolverhampton that he had fallen against a stove.

Crowther stayed. He was seventy-one years old and he stood four feet from a Redline for the length of it, and afterward he could not hold a cup for two days and said nothing about that either.

James put both hands on the bench. When it was over the two bronze blanks were slag, the gallery stock was ruined, the table was on its side and the roof timbers above the bench were alight.

He then walked out and stood in the yard in the rain until the engine came down.

It was the eleventh Redline and it broke the right forearm in two places.

Silas Crowther did not order more blanks. He was seventy-one years old and his sight was going and both of them knew it.

Tommy Byrne died at home in his bed on the night of 19 May 1957, aged fourteen, eleven weeks later. He had been sitting up that evening and his mother had thought he looked better.

The last thing anybody recorded him saying to James was said in the yard, in the rain, before his father took him home:

> "If it hurts, that means I'm still there."

James Revex has never said whether he was right.

He carries a folded piece of paper from the funeral notice and he does not talk about it, and Sarah Mitchell's records, which are meticulous about everything else, contain no entry at all between 18 May and 4 June 1957.

---

## XXII. Detective Inspector Margaret Thorne

Margaret Thorne is a Woman Detective Inspector of the Metropolitan Police, and every word of that title is doing work.

She is in Birmingham at the invitation of the Chief Constable of the Birmingham City Police, under the arrangement by which a provincial force calls in the Yard for matters beyond its own capacity.

Her warrant runs in England and Wales. Her presence here does not. Birmingham has its own force, its own cells, its own men, and it asked for her; and what has been asked for can be unasked in a single telephone call, and everybody involved understands this, including her.

Her formal remit is the one women in the CID are given: statements from women and children, offences against them, the cases the male establishment considers appropriate. Nothing about illegal surgery, industrial violence or the disappearance of a temperature monitor falls inside it.

She works the Revex file around the edges of that remit, and she has been careful to keep a Byrne-family interview and a Mitchell interview in the paperwork for exactly this reason.

Thorne lost her husband in a factory accident in Coventry in 1949.

This makes her dangerous to negligent owners and extremely difficult to intimidate with industrial language.

She does not classify James Revex as the origin of the problem.

She classifies him as evidence.

Her working theory connects:

- post-war salvage and the men who know what to do with it;
- illegal medical improvisation by people with no licence and considerable skill;
- private industrial cover-up;
- desperate workers with no compensation route;
- traumatised veterans;
- owners willing to purchase silence;
- bodies treated as machinery at the point where machinery became expensive.

Her file treats James as suspect, victim and witness in the same hand.

She has had at least four opportunities to arrest him and has taken none of them. Not out of mercy. She understands that James cornered indoors will burn down half a street, and she understands that caging him solves nothing while Crowther, Hadley & Sons and the men making enquiries in Wolverhampton remain untouched.

She also understands that she is one complaint away from being sent home, and that arresting a man the register says is already dead is exactly the kind of complaint that would do it.

Her most pointed recorded question came during an interview that was never formally logged:

> "Is that living, Mr. Revex, or have you only mistaken maintenance for mercy?"

No answer appears in the transcript.

Only this line follows:

> Subject's engine accelerated. Interview suspended.

## XXIII. The Envelope

James Revex has no wages, no ration book, no tenancy, no insurance stamp and no legal existence. The register at Hadley's says he was destroyed on 15 March 1952.

He has been paid anyway.

From January 1953 to November 1956, four pounds a week came to him in an envelope through Ellis Stark, a foreman at Hadley's, handed over at the same entry off Dartmouth Street on the same evening each week.

The reason is not charity and it is not hush money in the ordinary sense, because James has never threatened to say anything.

The reason is that the lower seam had been reported twice and not acted upon, and a living man walking the canal with the company's failure built into his chest is worth four pounds a week to keep quiet, unremarkable and elsewhere.

The payments stopped in November 1956, three weeks after Thorne first interviewed Stark.

Since then he has taken purses from arranged fights in Digbeth and Ladywood, which he despises, and which is where roughly half of the violence attributed to him after 1956 actually comes from.

Mitchell buys the oil out of her own wages.

She has never told him that either.

---

## XXIV. Where He Lives

The rope loft above Farrier's Yard, Ladywood, from 1952 until the boards gave way in 1954.

A condemned back-to-back in a court off Alma Street, 1954 to 1956, alone, the neighbouring houses already emptied for demolition.

A lodging house in Nechells for four months in 1956, until the landing collapsed and the landlord asked questions.

Since then: Mitchell's back room when she permits it, and otherwise roofs, boiler houses, and the dry arch under the Icknield Port bridge.

The Wolverhampton yard was on that list until March 1957. It has not been on it since, and neither of them has said so out loud.

He cannot take a room like an ordinary man. He scorches bedding, spoils food, warps frames and weighs more than the floors of the housing stock he can afford. He has burned two mattresses and set fire to one wall.

He sleeps at Banked, on brick or stone, with something cold against his back.

---

## XXV. Crowther's Other Work

Rumours of additional attempts predate the police files.

Some came from drunk mechanics. Some from nurses who later denied speaking. Some from men paid to move covered crates between Birmingham, Wolverhampton and Manchester.

Arthur proves the method did not begin with James.

James proves it did not end with Arthur.

By 1956 three separate industrialists had made enquiries into what one typed memorandum, recovered from a solicitor's office after a warehouse fire, calls *combustion-assisted labour viability.* No signature survived.

The implication is not subtle. A man who does not tire, does not unionise, does not require a wage in any form the Revenue recognises, and can be kept running on a gallon of oil a week.

What none of them have understood is the second burn, and Crowther has not corrected them.

A man like Arthur is cheap.

Making a man like Arthur requires starting with a man like James and using him up, and no memorandum has yet been recovered in which anybody asks how long that takes.

To workers, James was a warning of what factories did to men.

To owners, he was a prototype with a costing problem.

---

## XXVI. The Notebook

The notebook has not been recovered by any authority.

Silas Crowther has it.

He took it from the monitor's booth locker on the afternoon of 15 March 1952, along with a coat, a tin of tobacco, a set of gauge keys and a wedding ring on a chain, while the fire crews were still at the seam.

He had it open on the bench by the following morning, which is to say two days before the third adjustment and three before the boy woke at all.

The ring he gave back. The notebook he did not mention.

He built the valve gallery high, above the block rather than beside it, which is mechanically unnecessary and cost him three days of fitting.

It is where the boy drew it.

James has seen the notebook once since, in 1956, at the Wolverhampton yard, on a shelf, closed.

He did not ask.

Sarah Mitchell's correspondence contains one line about it, written after the yard:

> It was on the shelf and I knew what it was before he said anything, because he had gone the colour of a man reading his own death notice. He would not take it down. He said the drawings were a boy's. Then he said, the gallery sits high. Then he did not say anything else for an hour and I did not make him.

For four years he had been afraid of two possible answers.

He got the worse one and has declined to look at it since.

## XXVII. Material Objects

### Elizabeth Revex's wedding ring

James wears his mother's ring on a chain at his neck.

Those permitted to touch it report that it holds warmth for a long time after leaving his skin, which is a property of a small mass of gold against a body that runs at 104 °F and not a property of anything else.

The ring survived the explosion because it was in the booth locker.

It is the only object conclusively tied to his life before the engine, and it is the only thing he has never put down.

### The maintenance tools

Mitchell's valve tools are custom-ground and stored separately from ordinary mechanical equipment. Several are shaped to enter gaps in the housing without tearing the surrounding tissue. Two are bent at angles that make no sense for any engine that stays in a car.

Inside the lid of the case, in pencil, in a hand that is not hers, are two names and two ages.

Leonard Pace, 41.

Douglas Weir, 23.

She has never mentioned that she found them. He has never mentioned that he wrote them.

### Mirror fragments

Whole mirrors appear rarely in rooms associated with James Revex. Fragments appear often.

One was found wrapped in cloth near a maintenance basin. Another was wedged into a window frame at an abandoned workshop. A third, cracked and smoke-stained, was found beside a canal lock.

The pattern suggests selective looking.

Eyes.

Hands.

Chest.

Never the whole.

## XXVIII. Heat

Heat follows James Revex in physical and social forms.

Rooms retain him after he leaves. Tools warm in his vicinity. Milk spoils quickly where he sleeps. Wallpaper dries, cracks and curls near his shoulders. Metal bed frames expand with small nightly sounds.

A room he has slept in reads eight to twelve degrees above the rest of the house until the following afternoon.

People also change temperature around him.

Some flush.

Some sweat.

Some step back before admitting fear.

Sarah Mitchell's hands redden during maintenance and stay steady. Silas Crowther's hands do not tremble, though witnesses have claimed his eyes water from the fumes. Arthur Davies produces no heat response worth recording, and the reason is the tell. He is as warm as James at Steady and always exactly that warm. Nothing rises. Nothing drops. People flinch at change, not at temperature, and there has been no change in Arthur Davies since 1953.

Tommy Byrne once fell asleep against a wall that James had been leaning on.

When James noticed, he moved away.

The boy woke cold and complained.

James did not go back to the wall.

---

## XXIX. Silence

Before 1952, silence kept him alive.

After 1952, silence is what failure sounds like.

At Banked the engine is nearly inaudible. At Stall it is inaudible entirely. The quieter he gets, the closer he is to the thing that kills him, and his body has been trained since childhood to treat quiet as safety.

He seeks noise because noise means function.

He fears quiet because quiet means collapse.

He mourns the quiet anyway, because it belongs to the boy he was before the furnace, and because his mother is in it.

Several witnesses describe him pausing at the edge of calm as if listening for a lost room.

Then the engine coughs.

Then panic returns.

Then the motor roars back.

In one of Sarah Mitchell's fragments:

> He does not want peace.
> He wants proof that peace would not kill him.

No such proof exists in the record, and on the evidence of the ladder, no such proof is available.

---

## XXX. Touch

He does not hear machines. He has never claimed to.

He reads them through contact.

The neural contacts thread the intercostal nerves and the unit shakes at a frequency he has lived inside for six years, and the consequence is that vibration arriving through his hands, his feet or the wall at his back resolves into something he can interpret the way other men interpret a smell.

He can tell a dry bearing through a bench top.

He knows when a lift is loaded before it moves.

He identified a cracked flywheel in a Digbeth yard in 1955 by putting his palm on a doorframe forty feet away, and the man who owned it called him a devil and paid him anyway.

He puts his hand flat on walls constantly, in every account, in every district, for six years.

Not for balance.

He is checking whether the building is running better than he is.

---

## XXXI. What the City Said

### Names

The Furnace Man. Crowther's Boy. The Walking Boiler. The Birmingham Devil. Revex the Engine. The Man Who Should Have Burned.

The names reveal less about James than about the people using them.

Men frightened of him called him Devil.

Men who pitied him called him Boy.

Men who understood factories called him Engine.

### Rumours, and what is in them

*He drinks oil.* Half right, and the half that is right is worse.

*Bullets melt before they touch him.* No. He has been shot four times. Two went straight through him. The third struck the housing and did not, and the seam had to be replaced afterward at some cost to everybody involved. The fourth took the top off his left ear.

*His heart is a tank motor from the desert war.* No. It is a small family saloon engine, out of a car that was being sold for a hundred pounds when his mother was a girl.

*He can set coal alight by looking at it.* No. His skin will scorch cloth and blister a hand and it will never in his life light a fire; nothing on the outside of him gets anywhere near the temperature of paper.

The inside of him does.

The London paper that printed the coal claim in 1955 also called him a human boiler possessed of criminal strength and got every medical fact wrong. James reportedly laughed at the article, held the page flat against the housing aperture in his chest until it took, and dropped it on the floor to finish.

He did not use a match. That part is true and it is the only part.

*He can hear machines begging.* No. See above; the truth is stranger and quieter.

*He climbs on roofs at three in the morning to remember silence.* Yes.

### Newspapers

Most Birmingham papers avoided naming him until the rumours had outrun print. Early references preferred euphemism: factory disturbance, heated altercation, industrial madman, unknown furnace attacker, Birmingham's burning man.

### Police

Classification remained inconsistent. Ordinary assault categories failed when witnesses insisted the suspect's body temperature melted restraints. Medical detention required medical authorities willing to admit he existed. Military interest was suspected and never confirmed. Charging a man who appears in a company register as destroyed raised a question nobody at the Victoria Law Courts wanted to answer in writing.

A practical instruction circulated unofficially:

> Do not corner Revex indoors.
> Do not wet him unless you mean to stop his heart; a bucket to the chest will do it and you will not get him started again.
> Do not strike the chest unless lethal escalation is intended.
> Do not mention his mother.

No officer admitted writing the last line.

### Workers

Workers were divided. Some saw him as a threat to organising because his violence drew police. Some saw him as punishment walking on two legs. Some treated him with superstitious respect.

And some left the tins out, because they had done the sums, and because a man who can be kept alive on a gallon a week is a man and not a story.

## XXXII. The Clean Air Act, 1956

The Act received royal assent in the summer of 1956 and Birmingham began preparing its smoke control orders shortly afterward.

It prohibits the emission of dark smoke from the chimney of any building. It requires new industrial furnaces to be, so far as practicable, smokeless. It empowers the local authority to declare areas within which smoke from a chimney is an offence.

The offence is committed by the occupier of the building.

James Revex is not a building.

He has no chimney, no occupier and no premises. He vents through four fitted apertures in his own body at a rate that would prosecute a small works, and there is no clause in the Act that touches him and no officer in the city who could write a summons that would survive an afternoon in court.

It is the closest the law came.

A country that had watched thousands die in smoke passed a statute about combustion in residential districts in the same years that a man walked those districts burning, and the statute could not see him, because the men who drafted it did not imagine that a source of dark smoke might have a name, a mother's ring and a preference for cold weather.

Detective Inspector Thorne kept a copy of the Act in her file with one line underscored twice.

She did not write why.

## XXXIII. Crowther's Ledger

Silas Crowther's records are not scientific in any modern sense.

They move between measurement, confession, engineering notation and prayer.

Selected entries:

**March 15, 1952**
Brought down at ten past two. Chest crushed left side, heart failing on its own account before ever the blast touched him. Decision taken before I had washed my hands.

**March 16, 1952**
Block bored to two. Gallery set high. The height is wrong for the space and I have set it high regardless and I will not write down why.

**March 18, 1952**
Combustion rhythm stabilised after third adjustment. Boy conscious twelve seconds. Attempted speech. Pressure spike. Sedation.

**March 21, 1952**
He asked what happened. I told him he lived.

This was true.

**April 3, 1952**
Subject rejects housing. Repeated attempts to tear at the assembly. Burns to both forearms self-inflicted by friction and heat. Must restrain hands during waking period.

**April 9, 1952**
Engine responds to anger better than fear. Fear sustains but does not lift. Anger lifts. Must monitor.

**May 2, 1952**
He dreams loudly. Not words. Pressure.

**June 17, 1952**
Stall event nearly fatal. External spark required. He wept afterward and denied it.

**October 1953**
The hoist came down on me and he took it off my back.

I have been under a great deal of iron in my life and I have never once been lifted out from under it.

He sat with me for an hour afterward and by the end of that hour he was looking at me the way a man looks at a stranger who has stopped him in the street. I know what it was. I have known since 1949 what it costs and from which side.

He gave it for me. That is the plain fact and I will write it plainly because there is nobody left who would believe I meant it kindly.

**September 1954**
Second surge on attachment. Both shoulders out. He does not know what it cost him and I have not told him, and I am writing that down so that I cannot later say I did not know.

**August 22, 1955**
Plate fitted. Six days. He did not ask.

**October 1956**
The Byrne boy's chest is narrower than James's was. Two blanks ordered. Everything I have learned in eight years and I could do him properly this time.

**March 4, 1957**
Blanks ruined. Stock ruined. Bench gone. He stood in the rain afterward and would not come in.

I have no more bronze and no more eyes and I am glad and I would not say that aloud.

**Undated**
Robert would have hated the noise.

**Undated**
James is not Robert.
James is not Robert.
James is not Robert.

The final repetition is written harder than the first two.

The paper is torn beneath it.

## XXXIV. The Sarah Mitchell Correspondence

Only fragments remain.

Mitchell wrote in a controlled technical hand that often gave way under emotional strain.

One unsent letter:

> I can map the valves better than I can map his shame.
>
> When the pressure rises he looks at me as if I am both anchor and accusation. He wants me near enough to prove he is not alone, and not so near that his body can prove him monstrous.
>
> The engine is not separate from him. That is what he wants me to say and what he cannot bear to hear.

After the Aston slab:

> He came home wearing heroism like a dead man's coat. Everyone else saw the child alive. I saw what it had cost, and I could not say so, because the child was alive.
>
> He kissed my forehead because he remembered that he should.
>
> There is no loneliness like being loved by memory.

Undated, 1957:

> I am afraid I love the machine because it is the part of him that lets me touch the wound.

Undated, 1958, in the back of the maintenance book, in the clear untechnical hand she uses for the procedures:

> If you are reading this you have got the case open and you do not know what you are looking at.
>
> Start at the gallery. Do not use water. He will tell you he is fine.
>
> He is never fine. He is running, which is a different thing, and he has not been able to tell the difference since before I met him.

## XXXV. Private Fragments Attributed to James Revex

Collected from overheard speech, partial transcripts, witness memory and one burned scrap believed to have been in his hand. None can be treated as formal confession. All are consistent with established speech patterns.

> "Twenty years quiet and now every thought's got pistons."

> "Don't tell me to calm down. Calm was the coffin they built before I died."

> "Silas saved me. Silas killed me. Some days there ain't a difference worth naming."

> "I can lift a wall off a child and still not hold a woman without thinking about heat."

> "The engine don't make me angry. It just gives anger somewhere to go."

> "You ever miss something that would kill you if it came back?"

> "I liked it. That's the bit you don't get to forgive."

> "Me chest don't beat. It detonates."

> "I opened it. Nobody's ever asked me that, in six years, and I've been stood there waiting."

> "She reads me a list of things I can still feel. It used to take her a while."

## XXXVI. Birmingham's Interpretation

Every city creates myths to explain what it cannot prosecute.

James Revex became Birmingham's industrial conscience because he made the metaphor physical.

The men who coughed black into handkerchiefs saw their lungs in his smoke.

The widows saw husbands swallowed by machinery and returned wrong.

The owners saw damage, liability and a figure at the bottom of somebody's estimate.

The police saw a file that refused closure.

The doctors saw impossible survival and declined to sign their names too close to it.

The children saw fire.

The workers saw a man.

That division may explain why nobody agreed whether he was monster, victim, weapon, miracle, scandal or warning.

He was all of them, depending on where one stood and how close one dared to come.

---

## XXXVII. The Last Stable Classification

By 1958 the case could no longer be contained as a single industrial accident.

It intersects with unlawful surgery, post-war salvage, labour violence, corporate concealment, falsified registers, anomalous physiology, progressive emotional destruction, commercial enquiry into replication, two deaths at the seam, one boy in a bed in May, and the private grief of a man who mistook invention for resurrection.

The most complete classification was proposed by Detective Inspector Margaret Thorne:

**Human subject. Industrial alteration. Continuing agency. Dependency by manufacture. Public hazard. Primary witness.**

No court accepted the language.

No hospital accepted the body.

No factory accepted responsibility.

James Revex continues to move through Birmingham: hot enough to mark doorframes, human enough to avoid mirrors, dangerous enough to frighten police, tired enough to climb roofs looking for quiet.

His engine runs.

That is survival.

It is not acquittal.

---

## XXXVIII. Closing Record

The final confirmed sighting in the central file occurred near the canal after midnight.

Weather: cold rain.
Visibility: poor.
Witness: unnamed night watchman.
Distance: approximately thirty yards.

James Revex stood beneath a warehouse awning, shirtless, steam rising where the rain struck his shoulders. One hand held the chain at his neck.

The other was pressed flat against the brick, the way he always does it, reading whatever was running on the other side of the wall and comparing it to himself.

The witness heard him speak, though no one else was present.

> "I burn, therefore I am."

The engine was still audible after he went into the fog.

For several minutes the wall stayed warm.
