# CALL OF DUTY: WAR SERPENT
### A World Bible

---

## I. THE NAME

There is no single moment when the name *War Serpent* was chosen. It was assembled, the way everything in this world is assembled — out of fragments, misheard reports, and the last transmissions of soldiers who did not live long enough to be believed.

Radio operators across three different American deployments, separated by decades, used the same phrase to describe the thing they could not explain: a war that eats its own tail. A war with no beginning anyone can find and no end anyone can imagine. A serpent, coiled around the whole of human history, feeding forever on what it has already killed.

The people who live there now — the ones with enough mind left to name anything — do not call it War Serpent. They call the world itself **Oroboros**, and they have called it that for longer than any of them can measure. War Serpent is the operational designation given to it afterward, on the other side, by the four people who found a way back out.

---

## II. THE PREMISE

Oroboros shares the geometry of the living Earth — the same coastlines, the same mountain ranges, a second globe folded directly beneath the one humanity walks on. But it is not a mirror world frozen at one moment. It is a world where every war humanity has ever fought, and every war it has not yet fought as of the year 2045, is happening at once, forever, without pause and without resolution.

It is not another dimension in the sense of an alien plane with alien rules. It is *this* Earth, wearing the last moment of every violent death as a permanent, standing present. When a soldier dies in combat — on the beaches of Normandy, on the walls of a Roman frontier fort, in a firefight fought last month in the calendar of the living world — some fraction of them, not all, not evenly, and nobody on Oroboros has ever fully explained the selection, opens their eyes again in that place. Not healed. Not new. Continuing.

The dead do not rest on Oroboros. They redeploy.

In the summer of 2045, in the third year of the conflict the United States government still refuses to formally name a World War, a classified Department of Defense initiative operating under the cover designation **Project Phantom Corridor** attempted to tear open a controlled rift into a parallel dimension. The objective was explicit and entirely strategic: a rapid-deployment corridor that would let American forces enter and exit any theater of the war on Earth faster than any enemy could reposition to meet them — a door that let you leave from Texas and arrive in the Pacific before the other side's radar finished its sweep.

What the project's rift actually opened onto had been waiting, in some sense, for a very long time. It did not need to be discovered. It had simply never been reached.

The rift did not stabilize. It did not behave as a corridor. It behaved as a mouth, and for the seconds it remained open, it swallowed four living people out of the facility and closed itself around them.

They are, as far as anyone left in the world above knows, dead. A containment failure buried under a classification most of the surviving research staff never had clearance to name. Four notifications sent to four families, care of a government that could not tell the truth about Phantom Corridor even if it wanted to.

They are not dead. They are simply the only four living people in a world built entirely from the dead.

---

## III. THE FOUR

They did not arrive together. That is the first and most important fact of their story, because Oroboros does not deliver anything gently, and it did not deliver them as a unit. It scattered them the way it scatters everything — across a warzone the size of a planet, with no compass that points toward each other, only toward the next fight.

All four are American, drawn from the same classified roster at the same Phantom Corridor test site. That shared origin is the only thing Oroboros left intact.

**THE SOLDIER — Staff Sergeant Marcus Kowalczyk**, twenty-eight, U.S. Army infantry, assigned to Phantom Corridor's security detachment. The most physically capable of the four, and the one for whom survival is the smallest problem he has on Oroboros. He knows how to move through a warzone. He does not know how to move through a warzone with no front line, no command structure above him, and no chain of command that will ever hear his after-action report. His crisis is not tactical. It is orientational. Everything he was trained to trust — that a war has a side, a purpose, a hierarchy that knows more than he does — turns out to be true on Oroboros and completely useless at the same time, because there is no rear echelon here. There is no home to report back to. There is only the front, everywhere, always.

**THE SCIENTIST — Dr. Elena Farro**, thirty-two, Phantom Corridor's lead field theorist on rift stability, the only member of the four who partially understands what the rift was and was not. She is the worst-equipped of the four for physical survival and the best-equipped for understanding what is actually happening to them. Her arc is discovery in the most dangerous sense: every answer she finds makes her more certain that Phantom Corridor did not create Oroboros, and more afraid of what that means about who — or what — has been waiting on the other side of human history the entire time.

**THE ENGINEER — Thomas "Whit" Whitfield**, thirty-nine, civilian contractor attached to Phantom Corridor's hardware division, the oldest of the four. He understands machines, fortification, and the physics of holding a structure together under stress — all of which turn out to be exactly the skills a person needs to survive somewhere that has been at war since before recorded history. His arc is reconstruction: shelter, salvage, eventually the horrifying temptation to try to rebuild a version of the very device that stranded them here, because it is the only path home he can conceive of, and because he does not yet understand that the rift was never really Phantom Corridor's to control in the first place.

**THE OFFICER — Colonel Desmond Ashgrove**, forty-six, U.S. Army, Phantom Corridor's ranking officer and the man who signed off on the live test. He is the character the story does not let off easily. His first instinct — correct, in its own cold logic — is that a dimension containing every army in human history is not a horror to escape. It is the largest strategic resource that has ever existed. His arc is the slow, resisted realization that "resource" is the wrong word for something that used to be people, and the slower, more dangerous realization that even after he understands that, some part of him still wants to use it anyway.

None of the four know, at the outset, that the others survived. Each of them, waking alone in a ruined and burning world, believes — reasonably — that they are the only one who made it through.

## IV. THE PHYSICS OF THE PLACE

### 4.1 Geography

Oroboros shares the Earth's geography. Its Italy is Italy-shaped. Its Pacific is exactly as wide as the real Pacific. This is deliberate, and it is the thing that makes the world *survivable* as fiction rather than an abstraction — Marcus can read a real map and understand, in general terms, where he is standing, even as everything about who controls that ground and what waits in it obeys none of the rules the map used to promise.

The landscape does not stay still, however, in the way the living world's landscape does. Cities are not preserved as museum pieces of their worst day. They are actively, continuously fought over, which means that the physical terrain of Oroboros is a palimpsest: a Roman road cutting through a WWI trench line cutting through the collapsed shell of a building leveled in a war fought decades into the future of the century that built it. Ground gets fought over so many times, by so many different armies from so many different centuries, that its surface geology is less like a battlefield and more like a wound that has scarred over a thousand times without ever fully healing.

### 4.2 The Rule of Endless Front

There is no rear on Oroboros. There is no safe zone maintained by any army, because no army on Oroboros is capable of *winning.* This is the central mechanical law of the place, and every other piece of its logic follows from it.

An army can take ground. A Roman legion — its soldiers have come, informally, to call themselves **the Ninth That Would Not Die**, referencing an old and half-true legend about a lost legion from the real world — held the Italian peninsula for what its soldiers experienced as centuries, turning back wave after wave of other pre-gunpowder forces drawn into Oroboros from other corners of antiquity and the early medieval world. Spears, discipline, and superior numbers can hold ground for a very long time against other spears. But holding ground is not the same as winning the war, because the war has no terminal condition. There is always another army arriving, drawn from a different moment of human history, entering Oroboros through the same mechanism that brought every army before it: the accumulated dead of a real war, somewhere, sometime, in the world above.

**Every war fought on the living Earth feeds Oroboros a new army.** This is the engine that makes the endlessness of the place literal rather than merely thematic. As long as humanity keeps making war, Oroboros's stockpile of soldiers is inexhaustible, replenished from every century simultaneously, arriving with the weapons and tactics and technology of their moment of death — up through the newest arrivals from 2045 itself, wearing the war the four protagonists just walked out of. A phalanx does not fight another phalanx forever. Eventually the front reaches it, carrying rifles, or tanks, or whatever the twenty-first century's wars have most recently built. Numerical attrition, technological mismatch, and sheer accumulated momentum are what actually turn ground over — not victory, because victory, in the sense of an ending, does not exist here. Only turnover. Only the next occupation.

The Ninth That Would Not Die finally fell not to another legion but to an infantry division out of the First World War — the first force to reach Italy carrying gunpowder at all, let alone rifles and artillery. Centuries of discipline meant nothing against six times the numbers and weapons the legion had no answer for, wielded by men who no longer feared their own deaths, having already died once already. Superior organization can hold a line against equal or lesser technology almost indefinitely. It cannot hold against a categorical leap, no matter how long it has had to prepare.

**Captured ground means captured arsenals.** When a force falls, what it was carrying does not vanish with it. The soldiers who take the ground take the weapons left on it, and Oroboros's dead do not need training manuals to understand how to use what they've taken — the same battle-instinct that lets a legionary read a firing line reads a rifle's function on sight. It is not unusual, deep in ground that has changed hands a dozen times over a dozen different centuries, to find Roman regulars in salvaged body armor working a captured autocannon, or a company of First World War infantry carrying swords they picked up off the men they replaced because a blade never runs out of ammunition. Nothing on Oroboros stays in its own century for long. The front doesn't just move the map. It cannibalizes it, and every occupying force looks a little less like the army it used to be with every ground it takes.

### 4.3 Consciousness and Coherence

Not every dead soul who wakes on Oroboros wakes the same way, and the difference between a soldier and a civilian here is not a difference of degree. It is a difference of *kind.*

**Soldiers** retain consciousness, but it is consciousness with exactly one shape. A soldier on Oroboros can read a battlefield with total, instinctive fluency — assess a firing position, operate a weapon he has never touched in life, coordinate with the unit around him, recognize rank and give ground to it, adapt to a captured piece of enemy equipment inside a single engagement. He speaks, but only the one language war ever gave him: orders, callouts, rank, position, threat. A soldier can bark a command, report a contact, demand a password, warn an approaching stranger off at gunpoint in words that are unmistakably words. What he cannot do is anything outside that vocabulary. He cannot explain himself. He cannot be reasoned with about anything that isn't the battlefield in front of him, cannot answer a question about who he used to be, cannot be talked out of an order once it's been given — not because the words don't reach him, but because there is nothing left behind them to be reached. What's left is total fluency inside war, including its speech, and total absence outside it — a mind that is, in the most literal sense available, *only a soldier now, and nothing else a person used to be.* This is the register that carries the "zombie" texture of the title, and it is important that it is not mindlessness or muteness. It is a mind narrowed to exactly one function, executed with total fluency — language included — and nothing beyond it.

**Civilians** retain something categorically wider. A civilian who dies in a bombardment, a famine, or any of the machinery of atrocity that surrounds every war humanity has ever staged wakes on Oroboros still able to speak, reason, remember, grieve, plan, lie, love, and fear in every way a living person can. They do not become soldiers. They do not fight, as a rule, and they do not gain any of the battlefield fluency a soldier has. What they keep, instead, is everything a soldier lost: the full, general, undiminished consciousness of a person, unmoored from any single function. Some of them have been on Oroboros for centuries. They do not age. They do not heal old wounds, but they also do not develop new frailty. They have built lives in the margins — communities, informal economies, oral histories reaching back further than any written record of the living world, entire folk religions organized around trying to explain a world in which death is not an exit.

They live between armies, in the terrain no force considers worth permanently garrisoning, in the ruins that change hands too often for any military to bother fortifying. They are not safe there. They are simply *less immediately valuable as ground*, which is the closest thing to safety Oroboros offers anyone. When a front does roll through a civilian settlement — and it always eventually does — the result is what it has always been, in every war in human history, when the front reaches people who were never soldiers to begin with. Oroboros does not soften this. It is one of the central things the book is *about*: that the machinery of endless war does not spare the people who never volunteered for it, and that the four protagonists, particularly Ashgrove, will eventually have to stand inside the wreckage of that fact and decide what it means that a piece of him still sees a strategic asset where he should only be able to see the wreckage of a people.

### 4.4 The Hierarchy Beneath the Silence

A soldier on Oroboros speaks — orders, callouts, warnings, the vocabulary war gave him and nothing more. What he cannot do is be formally commissioned, promoted, or given rank in any way a living military would recognize. And yet soldiers on Oroboros still recognize rank among themselves, instantly and without a word of it ever being declared.

It is not insignia. Rank markings rot, get lost, get taken as trophies by whoever wins the next ground, and mean nothing on their own to a mind narrowed down to war. What soldiers on Oroboros recognize sits underneath the words they do have — a structure of authority that seems to exist independently of any individual soldier's memory of the living military he came from, as if "army" itself survived the crossing as a shape, even after every soldier's ability to formally name its parts did not. Within any given unit, at any given moment, there is a corporal, a captain, a general, whether or not anyone alive would recognize the insignia on them — and every soldier in that unit *knows* it, instantly, the way a body knows which way is down, and obeys the orders that authority speaks aloud the same way a living unit obeys a living officer.

Nobody on Oroboros has ever been formally promoted into that structure. You do not receive the rank. The rank receives you — accumulated, unconsciously, out of nothing more than sustained proximity to the fighting and a pattern of decisions the surrounding soldiers begin, without any of them choosing to, treating as command. It happens slowly enough that the person it is happening to rarely notices until it is already done.

This is the mechanism by which Oroboros's oldest civilians have, in isolated and catastrophic cases, come to command armies of hundreds of thousands.

## V. THE ASCENDED

### 5.1 The Two Doors

Every civilian who has spent long enough on Oroboros eventually arrives, whether they ever say it aloud or not, at the same choice. This world is not going to end. The war surrounding their settlement is not going to stop, this decade or this century, because it cannot stop — the living world keeps feeding it. A civilian can spend that time as what Oroboros calls a **Margin-Dweller**: hiding, running, rebuilding, grieving, watching the front take everything they build every few decades and starting again, forever, at the edge of a war that will never once acknowledge they exist.

Or they can walk into it.

The oldest civilians on Oroboros — the ones who have watched this cycle for so many repetitions that the distinction between patience and madness has stopped meaning anything to them — are the only ones who ever choose the second door. Not out of ambition, in most documented cases. Out of exhaustion. There are only ever two options, on Oroboros, for a mind that refuses to simply stop: keep running from a war that cannot be outrun, or become something the war has to answer to.

### 5.2 What They Become

A civilian who commits fully enough, for long enough, to fighting alongside a soldier faction begins to be read by that faction's silent hierarchy the same way any soldier is — proximity, competence, decisions the surrounding dead start treating as command. But a civilian brings something into that hierarchy that no soldier ever could: the full, unnarrowed consciousness Section 4.3 describes, a mind that never lost language, reasoning, memory, or will. When that mind is folded into a structure built to recognize command instinctively and absolutely, what results is not a general. It is something Oroboros has no other name for than what the oldest Margin-Dwellers call them, in the tone usually reserved for weather that kills: **the Ascended.**

An Ascended is not a civilian who learned to command soldiers. It is the fusion of a soldier's total, single-purpose fluency in war with a civilian's total, unbroken personhood — two kinds of consciousness that were never meant to occupy the same mind, welded together by centuries of accumulated rank neither side ever consciously granted. They can speak. They can plan on a scale no ordinary soldier faction ever could, because they still remember, in full civilian detail, everything a person is capable of remembering — logistics, geography, the layout of a city that stopped existing four hundred years ago, the names of everyone they have ever lost. And they can command, absolutely, because every soldier under them reads their authority as instinctively and totally as they read any general's. An Ascended does not lead an army. An Ascended *becomes* the point where an army's entire silent hierarchy resolves, the same way a compass needle resolves toward north.

The largest Ascended commands, by the time the four protagonists' paths begin crossing theirs, number in the low millions — entire theaters of Oroboros's endless front reorganized around a single mind that used to be an ordinary person hiding in the margins, centuries or a lifetime ago, before they ran out of the will to keep running.

### 5.3 The Cost

Nothing on Oroboros is free, and the price of becoming Ascended is the last thing that made a civilian a civilian in the first place. The fusion is not stable. The longer an Ascended holds their command, the more the totalizing, single-function fluency of the soldier consciousness they've merged with bleeds into the civilian mind that used to be whole. They do not become soldiers — they never lose language, never lose the capacity to reason on a scale no ordinary soldier retains. What they lose, slowly and by every account irreversibly, is anything resembling the *proportion* an ordinary person carries: the sense that any single life, including their own, matters more than the front. An Ascended a century into command still sounds, in conversation, entirely human. What they say, with total and unshaken calm, is the kind of thing that stops being sayable by anyone still capable of being called a person.

The oldest Ascended are rarely spoken of by name, even by the soldiers under them. They are spoken of the way a Margin-Dweller speaks of weather. The four protagonists will not meet the oldest ones directly until deep in the story's second half — and when they do, the horror is not that the Ascended is inhuman. It is that she remembers, in perfect and devastating detail, exactly what being human used to feel like, and has simply stopped being able to let that memory outweigh anything else.

## VI. WHAT ELSE LIVES THERE

### 6.1 The Historical Register

Oroboros is not populated exclusively, or even primarily, by named figures from any specific media property. Its overwhelming majority population is the vast, anonymous weight of ordinary human history's ordinary dead: Roman legionaries, medieval men-at-arms, Napoleonic conscripts, colonial-era soldiers on both sides of history's ugliest expansions, First and Second World War infantry from every theater and every flag, and the full weight of every conflict fought on the living Earth up through 2045, arriving with whatever weapons and doctrine they carried at the moment of their deaths.

This is deliberately built as an ocean, not a roster — a small number of specific, sharply drawn figures rising out of an overwhelming anonymous mass, some invented for this story and some drawn, with real specificity and real respect for their established history, from the wider Call of Duty canon of human (not supernatural, not Aether-adjacent) conflict.

### 6.2 Figures Drawn From Established Conflicts

The following figures are woven into Oroboros as fully realized inhabitants the four protagonists can meet, fight beside, or die to — never deployed for cameo pleasure alone. Each is used because their canonical death is a documented, in-universe combat or war-adjacent death, which is the only kind of death Oroboros's premise allows through its gate.

**Vladimir Makarov** commands a faction the story calls **the Inner Circle Remnant** — a splinter force still fighting the ultranationalist war he died in the middle of, no closer to its objective now than he was the day Price killed him, and no less certain that he is owed a victory the world above has long since stopped caring about. Makarov is used sparingly and precisely: not as a horror-movie monster, but as the single clearest embodiment of Oroboros's central cruelty. He *believes* his war still matters. He has had all the time since his death to notice, dimly, that it does not, and to refuse the thought every time it surfaces. He is a mirror held up to Ashgrove long before Ashgrove is ready to look into it.

**General Shepherd** commands with the same cold operational competence he had in life, and the story uses his presence to dramatize one of Oroboros's cruelest ironies: he still believes, or has convinced what remains of him to believe, that there is a chain of command above him worth serving, in a place with no command above anyone. His forces intersect briefly with the remnants of the men he burned alive in life — Ghost and Roach are present in Oroboros's population, though the story treats their reunion with Shepherd as one of the few genuinely *just* moments the world allows, rather than mining it for shock value.

**Raul Menendez** does not command a faction so much as *infect* one — his gift in life for turning grief into ideology follows him into death, and the pocket of Oroboros's civilian margins he has gathered around himself operates less like an army and more like a cult that has convinced itself it is still fighting Cordis Die's war, decades after the living world stopped remembering what Cordis Die was.

These figures are never played for cameo pleasure alone. Each one exists to demonstrate the same thesis from a different angle: that the wars of the living world do not end when the fighting stops. They end when the *reason* stops mattering to the people who fought them — and on Oroboros, the reason never gets the chance to stop mattering, because nothing here ever ends.

### 6.3 The Deep Past

Beneath the historically legible layers — Roman, medieval, gunpowder-era, modern — Oroboros's oldest strata are barely human anymore, in ways the story treats with real dread rather than exposition. There are things down in the deepest, most fought-over ground, the parts of the world that have changed hands more times than any single mind could count, that no longer remember which war they were part of, or whether they were ever really people in the way the word means anything. This is where the mystery of the place's true origin lives, and the book keeps it exactly that: felt, not explained, until the moment the story is ready to explain it.

## VII. THE QUESTION UNDER THE QUESTION

### 7.1 Why the Four?

Phantom Corridor's rift did not take everyone in the room. It took four people, out of a research and security staff numbering in the dozens, and it did not take them at random.

The four were not chosen by Oroboros because of who they were as individuals. They were chosen because of what they collectively *represented* at the moment the rift opened: the four functional roles a war effort depends on to sustain itself — the soldier who fights it, the scientist who arms it, the engineer who builds its infrastructure, and the officer who directs it toward a purpose. Oroboros's oldest, least legible layer — the thing sleeping under every more recent war fought on its surface — is not a mind in any sense the living world would recognize. It does not think in words. But it *responds* to structure, the way a magnetic field responds to iron, and Phantom Corridor's four-person nucleus was, however briefly, one of the most concentrated distillations of "an army's complete internal structure" that has ever existed in one place at one time.

Oroboros did not take four soldiers. It could have any number of those. It took a *war*, in miniature — the whole shape of what makes a war able to sustain itself — because something at the bottom of that world has spent longer than human history exists trying to understand why its own war never ends, and four living people carrying that shape into its territory was the closest thing to an answer walking through its door in longer than it can measure.

This is not resolved cheaply. The four do not receive a clean explanation early. It surfaces in pieces — through Farro's research, through what the deep, barely-human strata seem to want from them, through the shape of the story's climax — and even at the end, the book does not claim to fully explain what, if anything, is *aware* at the bottom of Oroboros. It commits to one structural truth (the four were taken as a set, for what they represented, not for who they individually were) and leaves the deeper metaphysical question — is there something *down there*, and is it a mind, a mechanism, or a hunger — genuinely, deliberately open, the way the unanswerable is meant to stay unanswered in stories that know the difference between mystery as design and mystery as a gap nobody filled.

### 7.2 Why the War Never Ends

The in-world mechanical answer is the one laid out in Section IV: attrition and reinforcement, not resolution, are what move Oroboros's front lines. But the four eventually confront a harder question sitting underneath the mechanical one, and it is the question the book is actually about.

Oroboros is not a closed system being fed from outside. It is fed *by* the living world, continuously, for as long as the living world keeps making war. Every death Marcus, Elena, Whit, and Ashgrove witness on the other side is, in some sense, a death that happened because human beings, on the world they came from — a world in 2045 that has itself been at war for three years and counting — have never once stopped fighting each other long enough to let the reservoir run dry.

The story's final and most damning implication is not supernatural. It is historical. Oroboros does not need a villain orchestrating it from the shadows, because humanity has been supplying its engine, unassisted, since long before Phantom Corridor ever opened a door into it. The place does not have to trap the four. Reality does that on its own, every day, on the world they are trying to get back to.

## VIII. THE SHAPE OF THE STORY

### 8.1 Structure

The narrative alternates among the four point-of-view characters, tracking their separate survival and separate discoveries across a shared but unmapped-to-each-other territory. Readers know, at all times, that the four are in the same world; the characters do not have this certainty, and for a significant portion of the book do not even know whether the others survived. Near-misses are used deliberately and are not telegraphed as such to the reader in advance: a facility one of them shelters in for the night, abandoned hours before by another; gunfire in the distance mistaken for a rescue; a message scratched into a wall or recorded on salvaged equipment, found by the wrong person, or the right person, much later than it was left.

The convergence of the four is the structural midpoint, not the climax — the moment the book's scale changes rather than the moment it resolves. Before it, the driving questions are *how do I survive* and *how do I find the others.* After it, the driving questions become *how do we get home*, and — once it becomes clear the rift that stranded them no longer exists to be reopened — *what does going home even mean, and what is each of us willing to do to make it happen.*

### 8.2 The Fracture

The four's reunion does not resolve their differences. It exposes them. Whitfield wants to reconstruct the mechanism that stranded them, believing replication is the only path home, without yet reckoning with the deep-strata implication that Oroboros's original rift was never Phantom Corridor's invention to begin with — it was a door that had simply never been *found* before, and rebuilding the key does not guarantee anyone controls what it opens. Farro wants to understand the place fully before anyone touches it again, certain that acting on incomplete knowledge is what stranded them in the first place. Marcus wants, more than anything else, an exit that does not require anyone to build a weapon first. And Ashgrove — who has by now seen enough of Oroboros's inexhaustible supply of soldiers, and enough of what the Ascended are capable of commanding, to understand its scale — begins to articulate the story's central temptation aloud: that a door into a bottomless reserve of combatants drawn from every war in human history up to the present is not a horror to seal shut. It is the largest strategic asset in the history of warfare, and it is sitting open behind them.

This is not decoration. It is the engine of the book's second half, and it is meant to cost something real: not a cartoon villain turn, but the slow, resisted, partially successful argument of a man who has spent his whole career believing that anything which can end a war faster is worth the price of using it — confronted, this time, by the fact that he has personally walked through the wreckage of what "using it" has already cost, for every army and every civilian population that ever got caught underneath this world's endless front.

### 8.3 What the Book Refuses to Resolve

Consistent with this world's governing principle — that not every mystery is a gap to be closed — the final chapters do not offer a tidy account of what, if anything, is truly aware at Oroboros's oldest depths, and do not confirm or deny whether Ashgrove's temptation is ever fully answered one way or the other for good. What the book does resolve is smaller and more human: whether the four leave together or leave something behind; what it costs each of them, individually, to walk back through a door that failed them once already; and whether "home" is a place any of them can actually still call by that name, having seen what they've seen.
