# CALL OF DUTY: WAR SERPENT
### A World Bible

---

## I. THE NAME

There is no single moment when the name *War Serpent* was chosen. It was assembled, the way everything in this world is assembled — out of fragments, misheard reports, and the last transmissions of soldiers who did not live long enough to be believed.

Radio operators across three different American deployments, separated by decades, used the same phrase to describe the thing they could not explain: a war that eats its own tail. A war with no beginning anyone can find and no end anyone can imagine. A serpent, coiled around the whole of human history, feeding forever on what it has already killed.

The people who live there now — the ones with enough mind left to name anything — do not call it War Serpent. They call the world itself **Oroboros**, and they have called it that for longer than any of them can measure. War Serpent is the operational designation given to it afterward, on the other side, by the four people who found a way back out.

---

## II. THE PREMISE

Oroboros shares the geometry of the living Earth — the same coastlines, the same mountain ranges, a second globe folded directly beneath the one humanity walks on — because it was never a separate creation. It is the Earth itself, the same Earth every living person stands on, corrupted rather than replaced: not a mirror world built to resemble the original, but the original, still there, still the same planet, turned into a place where every war humanity has ever fought, and every war it has not yet fought as of the year 2045, is happening at once, forever, without pause and without resolution.

It is not another dimension in the sense of an alien plane with alien rules. It is *this* Earth, wearing the last moment of every violent death as a permanent, standing present. When a soldier dies in combat — on the beaches of Normandy, on the walls of a Roman frontier fort, in a firefight fought last month in the calendar of the living world — some fraction of them, not all, not evenly, and nobody on Oroboros has ever fully explained the selection, opens their eyes again in that place. Not healed. Not new. Continuing.

The dead do not rest on Oroboros. They redeploy.

In the summer of 2045, in the third year of the conflict the United States government still refuses to formally name a World War, a classified Department of Defense initiative operating under the cover designation **Project Phantom Corridor** attempted to tear open a controlled rift into a parallel dimension. The objective was explicit and entirely strategic: a rapid-deployment corridor that would let American forces enter and exit any theater of the war on Earth faster than any enemy could reposition to meet them — a door that let you leave from Texas and arrive in the Pacific before the other side's radar finished its sweep.

What the project's rift actually opened onto had been waiting, in some sense, for a very long time. It did not need to be discovered. It had simply never been reached.

The rift did not stabilize. It did not behave as a corridor. It behaved as a mouth, and for the seconds it remained open, it swallowed four living people out of the facility and closed itself around them.

They are, as far as anyone left in the world above knows, dead. A containment failure buried under a classification most of the surviving research staff never had clearance to name. Four notifications sent to four families, care of a government that could not tell the truth about Phantom Corridor even if it wanted to.

They are not dead. They are simply the only four living people in a world built entirely from the dead.

---

## III. THE FOUR

They did not arrive together. That is the first and most important fact of their story, because Oroboros does not deliver anything gently, and it did not deliver them as a unit. It scattered them the way it scatters everything — across a warzone the size of a planet, with no compass that points toward each other, only toward the next fight.

All four are American, drawn from the same classified roster at the same Phantom Corridor test site. That shared origin is the only thing Oroboros left intact.

**THE SOLDIER — Staff Sergeant Marcus Deshawn Reeves**, twenty-eight, U.S. Army infantry, assigned to Phantom Corridor's security detachment. Black, raised in a mid-sized American city by a single mother who worked two jobs for most of his childhood and was rarely home before dark. He does not talk about his father, and there is nothing to say — the man left before Marcus could form a memory of him, and by the time Marcus was old enough to notice the absence, he had already decided it wasn't a wound worth reopening. What filled the space his mother's work hours left behind was the street, and by thirteen he had learned it well enough to survive in it — the arithmetic of who to avoid, what to carry, how to make himself look like more trouble than he was worth to anyone thinking about starting something. He never once used any of it as a reason to put distance between himself and his mother. Whatever he learned to do outside her door, he still came home to her every night he could, still gave her most of what he made even when what he made came from things he'd rather she not ask about, still put himself between her and anyone or anything that looked like a threat to her — a habit that started before he was old enough to understand what he was actually protecting her from and never once stopped. She fought him on the life he was building for himself the entire time, every year of it, and never once succeeded, and never once stopped trying either. He turned eighteen three months before his second arrest, and that one held — two years served, most of it spent doing the kind of quiet, disciplined work that gets noticed by the right people for the wrong reasons. One of the corrections officers on his block, a former Army NCO who saw something in him worth not writing off, spent those two years pushing him toward a door most of the men on that block never got shown: a service program that could turn a sentence into an enlistment, if Marcus could prove the discipline was real and not just good behavior with an expiration date. He proved it. The Army took the version of him the street had already built — watchful, loyal past the point of self-interest, incapable of leaving people behind — and gave it a uniform and a purpose that finally matched what he'd already been doing his whole life without anyone calling it that. He is the most physically capable of the four, and the one for whom survival is the smallest problem he has on Oroboros. What he cannot do is stop protecting people who don't need protecting anymore, in a world with no rear echelon, no home to report back to, and no mother waiting up for him this time. On a world built entirely from soldiers who cannot stop fighting, the thing that once made him a good son and a better soldier — the refusal to ever, under any circumstance, let the people near him go undefended — is the first thing that starts to look less like virtue and more like a mirror.

**THE SCIENTIST — Dr. Elena Farro**, thirty-two, Phantom Corridor's lead field theorist on rift stability. She did not volunteer for Phantom Corridor. Her father, a two-star general with his own career staked on the project's results, brought her onto it over her explicit objection — she had seen enough of the preliminary containment data to believe, correctly, that whatever the rift actually connected to was not going to behave like a corridor. She said so. She was overruled, and then quietly informed, through channels she was never meant to trace back to her father directly, that a scientist of her caliber declining a program of this classification would not simply end her own career — it would draw exactly the kind of attention her family, and specifically her five-year-old son, could not afford her father's enemies to take an interest in. She is a widow; her husband died in a highway accident two years before Phantom Corridor, and her son, Jack, is the only reason she has ever needed for anything since. She said yes afraid, not persuaded, and has carried that fear onto Oroboros with her, sharper now than it was in any lab. She is a practicing Christian, quietly and without performance, and it is the one part of herself Oroboros cannot narrow down to a battlefield function no matter what else it takes from her. She is the worst-equipped of the four for physical survival and the best-equipped for understanding what is actually happening to them. Her arc is discovery in the most dangerous sense: every answer she finds makes her more certain that Phantom Corridor did not create Oroboros, and more afraid of what that means about who — or what — has been waiting on the other side of human history the entire time. What she carries into this world is love that costs her something to keep giving — to her son, held at a distance she cannot close, and increasingly to the other three, who did not ask to need her and do anyway. On a world that runs on nothing but appetite, a person who keeps choosing to give something away is the strangest thing in it.

**THE ENGINEER — Thomas "Whit" Whitfield**, thirty-nine, civilian contractor attached to Phantom Corridor's hardware division, the oldest of the four. His family was never the kind that held together on its own. Money was tight for most of his adult life, tighter after his wife, Carol, was diagnosed with a chronic illness that never quite goes into full remission and never quite gets bad enough to stop being something they manage rather than something they solve. They have three kids — two boys and a girl, the oldest fourteen — and none of the ordinary stability other families seem to get handed for free. What they had instead was Whit, holding all of it together year after year the way he holds everything together: quietly, competently, without much acknowledgment and without ever once asking for it. He picked up every shift he could get, learned to fix anything that broke before it cost money they didn't have to replace it, kept Carol's treatment schedule straight in his head better than any of her doctors did, and made sure his kids never once had to know exactly how close to the edge the family actually lived, because that was his job, the same as any of the actual construction and fabrication work that paid the bills. Nobody taught him to be the load-bearing wall of his own household. He simply became it, the way he becomes it for anything else that's coming apart, because someone always has to and it was never going to be anyone but him. He understands machines, fortification, and the physics of holding a structure together under stress — all of which turn out to be exactly the skills a person needs to survive somewhere that has been at war since before recorded history. His arc is reconstruction: shelter, salvage, eventually the horrifying temptation to try to rebuild a version of the very device that stranded them here, because it is the only path home he can conceive of, and because he does not yet understand that the rift was never really Phantom Corridor's to control in the first place. What he carries into this world, more than any specific skill, is the same instinct that has kept a struggling family standing for over a decade — the refusal to let anything he cares about come apart on his watch, whether it's a marriage, three kids, or three strangers who have every reason to fall apart under pressure and mostly don't, because he is quietly the one making sure the joints hold. He knows where each of the others fits, what each of them needs from the group to keep functioning, and he builds that the same way he has always built everything: load-bearing points identified, weight distributed, nothing left to chance that can be reinforced instead. On a world defined by things falling apart forever, a person who has never once been allowed to stop holding something together is the closest thing to a miracle Oroboros has ever hosted.

**THE OFFICER — Colonel Desmond Ashgrove**, forty-six, U.S. Army, Phantom Corridor's ranking officer and the man who signed off on the live test. He grew up in a household where letting anything slip out of control was not a mistake you were allowed to make twice. His father, a career officer himself, ran the family the way he ran everything else — a household with a chain of command, a household where chaos was the one thing that was never, under any circumstances, tolerated, because chaos was what happened to people who hadn't planned well enough to prevent it. Desmond was taught to lead before he was taught much else — how to read a room and take charge of it, how to make a decision under pressure and never let anyone see the pressure, how command is less about giving orders than about being the one person in any given room who has already decided what happens next before anyone else has finished being afraid. He is married, with two children mostly grown, and he has run that household the same way his father ran his — competently, protectively, and with a level of control his wife has spent two decades gently, and unsuccessfully, trying to talk him down from. He is a good husband and a good father by any measure that matters to him, and he has never once had to ask himself whether the two are the same skill as the one that makes him a good officer, because on the world he came from, they never had to be tested against each other. He is the man who signed off on the live test, and nothing about him lets him off easily on Oroboros. His first instinct — correct, in its own cold logic — is that a dimension containing every army in human history is not a horror to escape. It is the largest strategic resource that has ever existed. His arc is the slow, resisted realization that "resource" is the wrong word for something that used to be people, and the slower, more dangerous realization that even after he understands that, some part of him still wants to use it anyway. What he carries into this world is the same instinct that raised his children and ran his household and built his entire career — the conviction that anything can be brought under control if the person in charge simply refuses to let it slip — and on a world where every army that has ever imposed order on anything did so through conquest that never ends, that instinct is the one that should worry him most, right up until the moment it is the only thing left holding the other three together.

None of the four know, at the outset, that the others survived. Each of them, waking alone in a ruined and burning world, believes — reasonably — that they are the only one who made it through.

Only Farro arrives on Oroboros already a believer. Marcus was raised in a house that went to church twice a year and stopped entirely once he enlisted; Whitfield thinks about God the way he thinks about weather in a country he has never visited — real, presumably, and none of his business; Ashgrove treats the question as a category error, something for men with less to actually control. None of that is fixed. It is where they start, not where the story leaves them, and each of the three moves toward whatever they come to believe by a different road, at a different pace, for different reasons — none of it engineered by Farro on purpose, all of it, eventually, traceable back to nothing more deliberate than who she keeps choosing to be around them.

Something about the four of them, taken together, resembles an old and terrible shape none of them would ever name out loud. It is not what they were sent here to become, and it was never guaranteed. Whatever pulled them through the rift wanted the structure they represented in that instant — soldier, scientist, engineer, officer, a war's whole shape folded into four people — and nothing about that moment decided anything that happens to them afterward. What comes next is not destiny finishing a shape it already drew. It is a world that has spent longer than human history learning exactly how to take endurance and starve it toward obsession, take order and starve it toward tyranny, take the instinct to hold a group together and starve it toward the need to control it — and four people who are, every day they survive there, still choosing, in ways too small to notice most of the time, whether to let it. Four riders were promised to this world once, in a story much older and much smaller than Oroboros. These are not them, and whether they ever become anything close to that shape is not written anywhere before it happens. It is decided the same way every choice on Oroboros is decided: badly, under pressure, one decision at a time, by people who mostly do not know yet which version of themselves they are choosing.

## IV. THE PHYSICS OF THE PLACE

### 4.1 Geography

Oroboros shares the Earth's geography. Its Italy is Italy-shaped. Its Pacific is exactly as wide as the real Pacific. This is deliberate, and it is the thing that makes the world *survivable* as fiction rather than an abstraction — Marcus can read a real map and understand, in general terms, where he is standing, even as everything about who controls that ground and what waits in it obeys none of the rules the map used to promise.

The landscape does not stay still, however, in the way the living world's landscape does. Cities are not preserved as museum pieces of their worst day. They are actively, continuously fought over, which means that the physical terrain of Oroboros is a palimpsest: a Roman road cutting through a WWI trench line cutting through the collapsed shell of a building leveled in a war fought decades into the future of the century that built it. Ground gets fought over so many times, by so many different armies from so many different centuries, that its surface geology is less like a battlefield and more like a wound that has scarred over a thousand times without ever fully healing.

### 4.2 The Rule of Endless Front

There is no rear on Oroboros. There is no safe zone maintained by any army, because no army on Oroboros is capable of *winning.* This is the central mechanical law of the place, and every other piece of its logic follows from it.

An army can take ground. A Roman legion — its soldiers have come, informally, to call themselves **the Ninth That Would Not Die**, referencing an old and half-true legend about a lost legion from the real world — held the Italian peninsula for what its soldiers experienced as centuries, turning back wave after wave of other pre-gunpowder forces drawn into Oroboros from other corners of antiquity and the early medieval world. Spears, discipline, and superior numbers can hold ground for a very long time against other spears. But holding ground is not the same as winning the war, because the war has no terminal condition. There is always another army arriving, drawn from a different moment of human history, entering Oroboros through the same mechanism that brought every army before it: the accumulated dead of a real war, somewhere, sometime, in the world above.

**Every war fought on the living Earth feeds Oroboros a new army.** This is the engine that makes the endlessness of the place literal rather than merely thematic. As long as humanity keeps making war, Oroboros's stockpile of soldiers is inexhaustible, replenished from every century simultaneously, arriving with the weapons and tactics and technology of their moment of death — up through the newest arrivals of 2045, wearing the same war still being fought on the living Earth the moment the four cross into Oroboros. A phalanx does not fight another phalanx forever. Eventually the front reaches it, carrying rifles, or tanks, or whatever the twenty-first century's wars have most recently built. Numerical attrition, technological mismatch, and sheer accumulated momentum are what actually turn ground over — not victory, because victory, in the sense of an ending, does not exist here. Only turnover. Only the next occupation.

The Ninth That Would Not Die finally fell — lost the ground, not the war it could never stop fighting — not to another legion but to an infantry division out of the First World War, the first force to reach Italy carrying gunpowder at all, let alone rifles and artillery. Centuries of discipline meant nothing against six times the numbers and weapons the legion had no answer for, wielded by men who no longer feared their own deaths, having already died once already. Superior organization can hold a line against equal or lesser technology almost indefinitely. It cannot hold against a categorical leap, no matter how long it has had to prepare. What was left of the legion did not vanish with the ground. It scattered — reduced, deteriorating, absorbed piecemeal into whatever other broken units it could still recognize as kin — the way anything on Oroboros scatters instead of ending.

**Captured ground means captured arsenals.** When a force falls, what it was carrying does not vanish with it. The soldiers who take the ground take the weapons left on it, and Oroboros's dead do not need training manuals to understand how to use what they've taken — the same battle-instinct that lets a legionary read a firing line reads a rifle's function on sight. It is not unusual, deep in ground that has changed hands a dozen times over a dozen different centuries, to find Roman regulars in salvaged body armor working a captured autocannon, or a company of First World War infantry carrying swords they picked up off the men they replaced because a blade never runs out of ammunition. Nothing on Oroboros stays in its own century for long. The front doesn't just move the map. It cannibalizes it, and every occupying force looks a little less like the army it used to be with every ground it takes.

### 4.3 Consciousness and Coherence

Not every dead soul who wakes on Oroboros wakes the same way, and the difference between a soldier and a civilian here is not a difference of degree. It is a difference of *kind.*

**Soldiers** retain consciousness, but it is consciousness with exactly one shape. A soldier on Oroboros can read a battlefield with total, instinctive fluency — assess a firing position, operate a weapon he has never touched in life, coordinate with the unit around him, recognize rank and give ground to it, adapt to a captured piece of enemy equipment inside a single engagement. He speaks, but only the one language war ever gave him: orders, callouts, rank, position, threat. A soldier can bark a command, report a contact, demand a password, warn an approaching stranger off at gunpoint in words that are unmistakably words. What he cannot do is anything outside that vocabulary. He cannot explain himself. He cannot be reasoned with about anything that isn't the battlefield in front of him, cannot answer a question about who he used to be, cannot be talked out of an order once it's been given — not because the words don't reach him, but because there is nothing left behind them to be reached. What's left is total fluency inside war, including its speech, and total absence outside it — a mind that is, in the most literal sense available, *only a soldier now, and nothing else a person used to be.* It is not mindlessness or muteness. It is a mind narrowed to exactly one function, executed with total fluency — language included — and nothing beyond it.

**Civilians** retain something categorically wider. A civilian who dies in a bombardment, a famine, or any of the machinery of atrocity that surrounds every war humanity has ever staged wakes on Oroboros still able to speak, reason, remember, plan, lie, and fear in every way a living person can. They do not become soldiers. They do not fight, as a rule, and they do not gain any of the battlefield fluency a soldier has. What they keep, instead, is everything a soldier lost: the full, general, undiminished consciousness of a person, unmoored from any single function — reasoning, memory, calculation, self-interest, all of it intact. What none of them keep, no matter how long they have been there or how whole their minds otherwise remain, is anything resembling love. Oroboros has no capacity to produce it, and had none, in any civilian, before four living strangers carrying it with them ever crossed into the place. Grief exists — the raw fact of having lost someone still registers, the way any fact registers to a mind still capable of memory — but grief on Oroboros is closer to an old scar than an open wound: felt as loss, understood as loss, never softened by anything resembling the tenderness that made the loss hurt to begin with in the world above, because that tenderness is exactly the thing Oroboros never had to offer any of them in the first place.

What civilians build instead of communities, in the terrain no army bothers to hold, looks enough like community from a distance to fool anyone who hasn't lived close to it. A dozen people will gather around a burning barrel in the ruin of a building neither side wanted badly enough to garrison, sharing the one fire, the one patch of dry ground, because numbers improve the odds of finding food, water, or warning before a front arrives — not because any of them woke up that morning wanting the others to be safe. Ask why they stay together and the honest answer, if any of them bothered to give it, would have nothing to do with each other and everything to do with arithmetic: alone, the odds of finding the next meal, the next water source, the next warning before a front rolls through are worse. Together, they're better. That is the whole of it. They will share warmth without ever quite calling it kindness, watch each other's backs without ever quite calling it care, mourn a member lost to a front's arrival with something that looks like grief and moves, underneath, like subtraction — one fewer set of eyes on watch, one fewer pair of hands for the next scavenging run. It is cooperation with every part of what makes cooperation feel like love scraped off it: the shape without the warmth, kept running on nothing but the same arithmetic that built it, for however many centuries it takes before the front finally decides the ground beneath the barrel is worth taking after all.

Even the oldest of them, the ones who have run this same arithmetic for centuries, have built something that passes for culture out of it — informal trade in salvage and information, oral histories of which fronts hold which ground, folk religions assembled to explain a world where dying does not end anything. None of it required love to build. Fear built it, and habit, and the same cold calculation that fills a shelter with strangers instead of leaving them scattered and easier for the front to take one at a time.

They live between armies, in the terrain no force considers worth permanently garrisoning, in the ruins that change hands too often for any military to bother fortifying. They are not safe there. They are simply *less immediately valuable as ground*, which is the closest thing to safety Oroboros offers anyone. When a front does roll through a civilian settlement — and it always eventually does — the result is what it has always been, in every war in human history, when the front reaches people who were never soldiers to begin with. Oroboros does not soften this. It is the machinery of endless war that does not spare the people who never volunteered for it, and the four protagonists, particularly Ashgrove, will eventually have to stand inside the wreckage of that fact and decide what it means that a piece of him still sees a strategic asset where he should only be able to see the wreckage of a people.

### 4.4 What Suffering Means Where No One Dies

Nobody on Oroboros dies twice. A body can be shot, burned, broken, buried under a ruin that took its whole street down with it, and the person inside it eventually opens their eyes again somewhere close to where they fell, whole enough to keep going, the way they always do. Death, the one true exit the living world offers everyone eventually, is the one thing Oroboros never allows. What replaces it is not mercy. It is two entirely different shapes of suffering, one for each kind of soul the place holds, and each shape is nothing more or less than what that soul already is, worn down into its purest and most permanent form — including how long the return itself takes, and what it costs to get there.

For a soldier, suffering is the war itself, with no far side to it. Every living soldier who has ever fought carried the promise, however distant, that the war would end — through victory, defeat, a wound bad enough to send them home, or death, which ends everything a soldier can be asked to endure. A soldier on Oroboros has none of those exits. The battle he is currently fighting will end, the way any single engagement ends, but the war underneath it never will, and some diminished, still-aware part of him seems to know this, the way an animal knows weather is coming before it arrives — not as a thought he could put into the one vocabulary left to him, but as a texture underneath everything he does, a fight that already knows, at some register beneath language, that it has always been fighting and will never be allowed to stop. This is the suffering built into what a soldier's consciousness on Oroboros already is: endurance with the stopping point removed, war become total identity with no self left underneath it to eventually go home to. It does not need a wound to hurt. Simply continuing is the wound, indefinitely renewed.

For a civilian, suffering keeps the shape it always had in every war fought on the living Earth — except that here, it never resolves the way it always eventually would have. Hunger that never becomes starvation because starvation would require a body that can actually fail. Thirst that never ends in death, because there is no death left to end it. Disease that runs its full course, again and again, without the one outcome disease has always eventually offered the people it doesn't spare. Cold, exhaustion, grief, the particular fear of watching a home get overrun for the second or the two-hundredth time — all of it recurring, all of it real in every sense a civilian would recognize as real, none of it ever concluding, because the body carrying it cannot be un-made the way a living body eventually can. Where a soldier's suffering is the collapse of everything he used to be into one narrow, endless function, a civilian's suffering is the opposite: the full width of an ordinary human life's worst moments, kept fully conscious, fully felt, and never permitted the one relief every one of those moments would eventually have carried in the world above.

Both are, in their own register, the exact shape of what Oroboros keeps of each soul once it has taken everything else. A soldier suffers by being reduced to nothing but the war. A civilian suffers by being kept whole enough to feel every part of what the war costs, forever.

### 4.5 The Hierarchy Beneath the Silence

A soldier on Oroboros speaks — orders, callouts, warnings, the vocabulary war gave him and nothing more. What he cannot do is be formally commissioned, promoted, or given rank in any way a living military would recognize. And yet soldiers on Oroboros still recognize rank among themselves, instantly and without a word of it ever being declared.

It is not insignia. Rank markings rot, get lost, get taken as trophies by whoever wins the next ground, and mean nothing on their own to a mind narrowed down to war. What soldiers on Oroboros recognize sits underneath the words they do have — a structure of authority that seems to exist independently of any individual soldier's memory of the living military he came from, as if "army" itself survived the crossing as a shape, even after every soldier's ability to formally name its parts did not. Within any given unit, at any given moment, there is a corporal, a captain, a general, whether or not anyone alive would recognize the insignia on them — and every soldier in that unit *knows* it, instantly, the way a body knows which way is down, and obeys the orders that authority speaks aloud the same way a living unit obeys a living officer.

Nobody on Oroboros has ever been formally promoted into that structure. You do not receive the rank. The rank receives you — accumulated, unconsciously, out of nothing more than sustained proximity to the fighting and a pattern of decisions the surrounding soldiers begin, without any of them choosing to, treating as command. It happens slowly enough that the person it is happening to rarely notices until it is already done.

This is the mechanism by which Oroboros's oldest civilians have, in isolated and catastrophic cases, come to command armies of hundreds of thousands.

## V. THE ASCENDED

### 5.1 The Two Doors

Every civilian who has spent long enough on Oroboros eventually arrives, whether they ever say it aloud or not, at the same choice. This world is not going to end. The war surrounding their settlement is not going to stop, this decade or this century, because it cannot stop — the living world keeps feeding it. A civilian can spend that time as what Oroboros calls a **Margin-Dweller**: hiding, running, rebuilding, grieving, watching the front take everything they build every few decades and starting again, forever, at the edge of a war that will never once acknowledge they exist.

Or they can walk into it.

The oldest civilians on Oroboros — the ones who have watched this cycle for so many repetitions that the distinction between patience and madness has stopped meaning anything to them — are the only ones who ever choose the second door. Not out of ambition, in most documented cases. Out of exhaustion. There are only ever two options, on Oroboros, for a mind that refuses to simply stop: keep running from a war that cannot be outrun, or become something the war has to answer to.

### 5.2 What They Become

A civilian who commits fully enough, for long enough, to fighting alongside a soldier faction begins to be read by that faction's silent hierarchy the same way any soldier is — proximity, competence, decisions the surrounding dead start treating as command. But a civilian brings something into that hierarchy that no soldier ever could: a mind that never lost language, reasoning, memory, or will — the full, unnarrowed consciousness that sets every civilian on Oroboros apart from every soldier there. When that mind is folded into a structure built to recognize command instinctively and absolutely, what results is not a general. It is something Oroboros has no other name for than what the oldest Margin-Dwellers call them, in the tone usually reserved for weather that kills: **the Ascended.**

An Ascended is not a civilian who learned to command soldiers. It is the fusion of a soldier's total, single-purpose fluency in war with a civilian's total, unbroken personhood — two kinds of consciousness that were never meant to occupy the same mind, welded together by centuries of accumulated rank neither side ever consciously granted. They can speak. They can plan on a scale no ordinary soldier faction ever could, because they still remember, in full civilian detail, everything a person is capable of remembering — logistics, geography, the layout of a city that stopped existing four hundred years ago, the names of everyone they have ever lost. And they can command, absolutely, because every soldier under them reads their authority as instinctively and totally as they read any general's. An Ascended does not lead an army. An Ascended *becomes* the point where an army's entire silent hierarchy resolves, the same way a compass needle resolves toward north.

The largest Ascended commands, by the time the four protagonists' paths begin crossing theirs, number in the low millions — entire theaters of Oroboros's endless front reorganized around a single mind that used to be an ordinary person hiding in the margins, centuries or a lifetime ago, before they ran out of the will to keep running.

### 5.3 The Cost

Nothing on Oroboros is free, and the price of becoming Ascended is the last thing that made a civilian a civilian in the first place. The fusion is not stable. The longer an Ascended holds their command, the more the totalizing, single-function fluency of the soldier consciousness they've merged with bleeds into the civilian mind that used to be whole. They do not become soldiers — they never lose language, never lose the capacity to reason on a scale no ordinary soldier retains. What they lose, slowly and by every account irreversibly, is anything resembling the *proportion* an ordinary person carries: the sense that any single life, including their own, matters more than the front. An Ascended a century into command still sounds, in conversation, entirely human. What they say, with total and unshaken calm, is the kind of thing that stops being sayable by anyone still capable of being called a person.

The oldest Ascended are rarely spoken of by name, even by the soldiers under them. They are spoken of the way a Margin-Dweller speaks of weather. The four protagonists will not meet the oldest ones directly until deep into their time on Oroboros — and when they do, the horror is not that the Ascended is inhuman. It is that she remembers, in perfect and devastating detail, exactly what being human used to feel like, and has simply stopped being able to let that memory outweigh anything else.

## VI. WHAT ELSE LIVES THERE

### 6.1 The Historical Register

Oroboros is not populated exclusively, or even primarily, by named figures from any specific media property. Its overwhelming majority population is the vast, anonymous weight of ordinary human history's ordinary dead: Roman legionaries, medieval men-at-arms, Napoleonic conscripts, colonial-era soldiers on both sides of history's ugliest expansions, First and Second World War infantry from every theater and every flag, and the full weight of every conflict fought on the living Earth up through 2045, arriving with whatever weapons and doctrine they carried at the moment of their deaths.

This is deliberately built as an ocean, not a roster — a small number of specific, sharply drawn figures rising out of an overwhelming anonymous mass, some invented for this story and some drawn, with real specificity and real respect for their established history, from the wider Call of Duty canon of human (not supernatural, not Aether-adjacent) conflict.

### 6.2 Figures Drawn From Established Conflicts

The following figures are woven into Oroboros as fully realized inhabitants the four protagonists can meet, fight beside, or die to — never deployed for cameo pleasure alone. Each is used because their canonical death is a documented, in-universe combat or war-adjacent death, which is the only kind of death Oroboros's premise allows through its gate.

**Vladimir Makarov** commands what has come to be known, among those who fight near it, as **the Inner Circle Remnant** — a splinter force still fighting the ultranationalist war he died in the middle of, no closer to its objective now than he was the day Price killed him, and no less certain that he is owed a victory the world above has long since stopped caring about. Makarov is not a horror-movie monster here — he is the single clearest embodiment of Oroboros's central cruelty, and the starkest possible contrast to every other named figure who shares his territory: he offers, even centuries in, no attempt at self-justification beyond raw nationalism, no ideology beneath the ideology, nothing resembling the sympathetic wound that complicates almost everyone else on this list. He *believes* his war still matters. He has had all the time since his death to notice, dimly, that it does not, and to refuse the thought every time it surfaces. He is a mirror held up to Ashgrove long before Ashgrove is ready to look into it — not because Ashgrove shares his cruelty, but because both men have organized an entire self-conception around a war continuing to mean what they need it to mean.

**General Shepherd** commands with the same cold operational competence he had in life, and his presence dramatizes one of Oroboros's cruelest ironies: he still believes, or has convinced what remains of him to believe, that there is a chain of command above him worth serving, in a place with no command above anyone. What made Shepherd in life was never simple villainy — thirty thousand of his men died in a single afternoon in a war the public moved on from within a news cycle, and what Oroboros preserves of him is a general who decided, once, that no soldier under his command would ever again be allowed to die that unremembered, and who was willing to engineer a war large enough to make sure of it. On Oroboros, that conviction has had centuries to calcify into something with no living men left to protect and no war left to end, and he commands exactly the way he always did: certain that the scale of the sacrifice justifies the hand that orders it. His forces intersect briefly with the remnants of the men he had executed and burned in life — Ghost and Roach are present in Oroboros's population, killed on his order at Shepherd's own hand and their bodies set alight afterward to erase the evidence, not burned as the execution itself — and their reunion with him is one of the few genuinely *just* moments this world ever allows. Shepherd is, for Ashgrove specifically, the harder mirror of the two: not a fanatic to be dismissed, but a competent, sympathetic officer who convinced himself that unaccountable command, exercised in the name of the men under it, was the same thing as protecting them.

**Raul Menendez** does not command a faction so much as *infect* one — his gift in life for turning grief into ideology follows him into death, though what he commands now is not devotion, because nothing on Oroboros still has that to give him. He did not arrive a monster. He was, once, a war orphan who protected his twin sister Josefina through years of exploitation neither of them chose, and everything that came after — the cartel, the insurgency, the cyberterrorism, the decades spent turning a legitimate grievance against the systems that used them both into a justification for mass civilian atrocity — traces back to the day an American operative's raid took Josefina's life and left Menendez with nothing left to protect. What holds his pocket of the civilian margins around him is closer to doctrine enforced through fear and repetition than anything resembling belief: the same cold arithmetic that builds every other civilian gathering on Oroboros, dressed in the borrowed language of a cause most of them arrived long after it ended, decades after the living world stopped remembering what Cordis Die was. They repeat what he tells them to repeat because repeating it has kept them alive this long, not because any of them still feel what Cordis Die's actual believers once felt. Menendez is the clearest available proof, for anyone on Oroboros paying attention, of exactly the pattern Farro comes to fear in herself and in the other three: a real wound, left untreated long enough, does not stay a wound. It becomes a reason.

These three, taken together, sketch the same shape from three different distances. Makarov is what conviction looks like with nothing left to convince. Shepherd is what protection looks like once it stops answering to anyone who could tell it no. Menendez is what grief looks like once it has had centuries, instead of years, to decide that the whole world owes it a debt. None of them are played for cameo pleasure alone. Each one exists to demonstrate the same thesis from a different angle: that the wars of the living world do not end when the fighting stops. They end when the *reason* stops mattering to the people who fought them — and on Oroboros, the reason never gets the chance to stop mattering, because nothing here ever ends. It is not the soldiers who carried out these men's worst orders who Oroboros preserves as its clearest warnings. It is the men who were, each in his own way, once trusted with more authority than anyone should be trusted with unaccountably — which is the exact shape of the temptation Ashgrove spends his whole time on Oroboros learning to recognize in himself before it finishes hardening into one of theirs.

### 6.3 The Deep Past

Beneath the historically legible layers — Roman, medieval, gunpowder-era, modern — Oroboros's oldest strata are barely human anymore. There are things down in the deepest, most fought-over ground, the parts of the world that have changed hands more times than any single mind could count, that no longer remember which war they were part of, or whether they were ever really people in the way the word means anything. What they are close to, without any of them being able to say so, is older than any of them — a truth felt long before it is ever understood.

## VII. THE QUESTION UNDER THE QUESTION

### 7.1 Why the Four?

Phantom Corridor's rift did not take everyone in the room. It took four people, out of a research and security staff numbering in the dozens, and it did not take them at random.

The four were not chosen by Oroboros because of who they were as individuals. They were chosen because of what they collectively *represented* at the moment the rift opened: the four functional roles a war effort depends on to sustain itself — the soldier who fights it, the scientist who arms it, the engineer who builds its infrastructure, and the officer who directs it toward a purpose. Something old and patient, coiled at the bottom of everything Oroboros is, responds to that structure the way a magnetic field responds to iron, whether or not it was ever fully aware of doing so. Phantom Corridor's four-person nucleus was, however briefly, one of the most concentrated distillations of "an army's complete internal structure" that has ever existed in one place at one time.

Oroboros did not take four soldiers. It could have any number of those. It took a *war*, in miniature — the whole shape of what makes a war able to sustain itself — because something underneath that world has spent longer than human history exists studying exactly that shape, and four living people carrying it into its territory was the closest thing to an answer walking through its door in longer than it can measure.

This is not resolved cheaply. The four do not receive a clean explanation early. It surfaces in pieces — through Farro's research, through what the oldest inhabitants of the deep strata let slip without meaning to, through everything that comes together only once their time on Oroboros is nearly over — and even then, nothing spells out everything that is *aware* at the bottom of Oroboros, or how fully. One structural truth holds (the four were taken as a set, for what they represented, not for who they individually were), and the deeper question is left to arrive through implication rather than explanation, the way the unanswerable stays unanswered when nobody forces it otherwise.

### 7.2 What Was There Before the First War

Nothing on Oroboros predates the thing the oldest civilians will, if they trust you enough, only ever call **the First Light** — never by any of the names it has taken since, and never above a whisper.

It was not born on Oroboros. Every fragment the four eventually piece together — Farro's research, half-preserved oral histories passed down through Margin-Dweller communities older than any written language, the unwilling testimony of Ascended who have held command long enough to remember further back than any human should — agrees on one thing: it came from the same Earth the living world stands on, and it has been there since before humanity had a word for war.

It was present, according to every account, at the first violence — watching, not causing, the way a hunting scar of a species learning for the first time that another human being could be made to stop moving forever. For an age longer than recorded history, it did nothing but watch. It did not create the wars of the earliest humans. It attended them, the way a coastline attends a tide it does not command, and something in the repetition of it — killing answered by killing, grief curdling into the next killing — began, slowly, to give it shape.

It first showed itself in something like a body during a massacre between two prehistoric bands, appearing to the losing side as a beautiful, radiant figure that ended the slaughter in a single motion no survivor could afterward describe in any consistent way, other than that it happened, and then it was over, and every enemy who had been advancing on them was simply dead. The survivors approached it in terror and in something closer to worship, unable to look away from it and unable to explain why. One of them found the nerve to ask what it was.

It answered with three words that meant nothing yet to anyone standing there, because no human language existed to carry their full weight, and would not for tens of thousands of years. *The Lie. The Sin. The Death.* The only one of the three the survivors understood, in the way they understood anything in that moment, was the last — because they had just watched it happen three hundred times in front of them, and the word for that was already the oldest word they had.

It offered them a fraction of what it was. In exchange, it asked for the only thing it has ever asked for since: to be worshipped. The band did not understand what that word would come to cost, could not have understood it, any more than a child can understand the terms of a contract placed in front of them — and so, without understanding, they agreed. It taught them how. That is where all of it began.

Every act of worship since — every altar, every sacrifice, every prayer offered by a soldier to whatever they called on before a battle, every civilization that has ever built its gods, knowingly or not, in a shape that rewarded violence — has fed something that was already old before the first of them was born. It did not need every war on Earth to make itself powerful. It only needed enough of them, repeated long enough, by enough people who never knew what they were feeding.

There came a point, long before any war the living world keeps records of, when it stopped being present *at* wars and began, slowly and without any single moment marking the change, to take the world itself over. It did not create anything. There was already an Earth — the same Earth the living world still walks on, the only Earth there has ever been — and what the First Light did, across an accumulation of centuries no human record reaches back far enough to date, was corrupt it: fold it, layer by layer, war by war, into a second and permanent state laid beneath the first, until enough of that Earth's capacity for violence had been given over to it that it was no longer a presence haunting human wars from the outside, but the total shape those wars took once they crossed into what it had made of the ground beneath them. It is not a ruler who lives in that world. It is not a general commanding its endless armies from some hidden seat of power the four might one day find and burn down. Oroboros is not a place the First Light occupies. Oroboros is the Earth itself, corrupted rather than created, several hundred thousand years of human worship and human slaughter folded into the same ground that still, on the other side, holds up the living world's cities and rivers and unmarked graves. The hatred that turns soldier against soldier, the hunger and thirst that never resolve, the despair that keeps a Margin-Dweller running instead of resting — none of it is administered from anywhere. It is not a system the First Light runs. It is what the First Light did to a world that was never its to remake, and never stopped being the same world underneath what it became.

This is the fact the four will eventually have to sit with, and it changes the entire shape of what they came here believing they might do. There is no chamber to reach, no throat to cut, no single act of violence that ends this the way violence has, in every other war any of them were ever trained for, ended something. You cannot defeat a world. You can only, if you are very determined and very lucky, find a way to stop being inside it.

Nobody on Oroboros states outright what it is, and nothing forces the word. What the fragments add up to is a shape anyone paying attention will recognize — the same being, or something answering to the same hunger, that a much later and much smaller human religious tradition would come to call by another name, long after this one had already finished its work on the world underneath the world.

### 7.3 Why the War Never Ends

The mechanical answer is attrition and reinforcement, not resolution — what actually moves Oroboros's front lines day to day. But the four eventually confront a harder question sitting underneath the mechanical one, and it is the question that matters more than any other by the time they understand it.

Oroboros is not a closed system being fed from outside. It is fed *by* the living world, continuously, for as long as the living world keeps making war. Every death Marcus, Elena, Whit, and Ashgrove witness on the other side is, in some sense, a death that happened because human beings, on the world they came from — a world in 2045 that has itself been at war for three years and counting — have never once stopped fighting each other long enough to let the reservoir run dry.

The most damning fact underneath all of it is not that something ancient and monstrous built this. It is that it did not have to build anything at all — only corrupt what was already there. The First Light is not the architect of Oroboros, standing somewhere apart from its own creation, because Oroboros was never a creation. It is the same Earth, the only Earth, turned by several hundred thousand years of human worship and human slaughter, absorbed without pause, into something that stopped being separate from the war long enough ago that there is no longer a wall between what it wants and what the world does. But corrupting a world does not mean being its only cause. Every soldier who wakes on Oroboros, every civilian who dies in its margins, every fresh war that keeps its stockpile from ever running dry, is still made by living human choices the First Light never has to lift a hand to cause. It does not need to reach into the living world and start a war. It only has to exist, patiently, on the other side of every war humanity was always going to start on its own — and wait for what it is made of to arrive. Humanity has been filling it, unassisted, since long before Phantom Corridor ever opened a door into it, and keeps filling it now, in 2045, for reasons that have nothing to do with anything watching from underneath. The place does not have to trap the four. Reality does that on its own, every day, on the world they are trying to get back to — Oroboros simply makes sure that once it does, there is never again a way to stop.

### 7.4 What Rescue Actually Means

Every soul on Oroboros arrived there already dead in a state the book of Farro's faith would call sin — not because war made them wicked, but because death in the middle of hatred, terror, or violence is, in that faith's accounting, rarely a clean way to leave the world, and Oroboros is where every one of those unclean departures seems to collect. Farro is careful, once she understands what she's looking at, not to say this to anyone who isn't ready to hear it that way, and even more careful never to let it curdle into the belief that anyone she meets there deserved to end up in this place. She does not believe that. What she believes, quietly and without needing anyone else to agree with her, is that whatever is possible to do for the people trapped in Oroboros is not a reward for their conduct in life or in death. It is mercy extended past the point anyone earned it, the same as it would have to be for anyone, anywhere, including her.

What she and, eventually, the other three become capable of doing for a handful of the people they meet there is real, and it is also smaller than it looks. A soul freed from Oroboros — the rare case where something one of the four does manages to loosen an individual soul's grip on its own unending battle, whether that soul is a soldier still fighting or a civilian still enduring — is freed *from that place.* It stops fighting a war that was never going to end on its own. It stops being made to relive the worst moment of its existence forever. That is not nothing. But it is not the same claim as salvation in the fuller sense Farro's faith actually means by the word. Nothing the four do decides what happens to any of those souls afterward, in whatever waits on the other side of finally being allowed to stop. Farro is explicit with herself about this distinction long before she says it out loud to anyone else: she is not in the business of judging anyone, and she is not in the business of saving anyone in the way only God is understood, within her own faith, to save. What she can do — what all four of them, eventually, find themselves able to do in ways none of them expected going in, and only ever for a handful of people at a time, never for Oroboros as a whole — is open a door out of the suffering. What waits past that door is not theirs to decide.

And the four are not, themselves, the mechanism. What stays distinct throughout — because Farro herself keeps it distinct, every time the temptation to think otherwise creeps in — is that nothing about Marcus, Whitfield, or Ashgrove's growing willingness to help her, and nothing about Farro's own courage, is what does the freeing. It is grace, moving with them because they are living, because they came from a world where grace is still possible in a way it no longer is for anyone already inside Oroboros. The four are living proof, walking through a world built entirely from the dead, that the door Oroboros closed on everyone already there was never closed on people like them — not because they are better, but because they are not yet finished, in the way only the living can still be unfinished. Farro is where that possibility first takes root among the four, not because she causes it, but because she is the only one of them who arrives already knowing the door is there to look for. Marcus, Whitfield, and Ashgrove come to whatever they eventually believe by walking beside her long enough to see what she keeps choosing, not because she ever sets out to convince them of anything. Each of their three roads there looks nothing like the others, moves at its own pace, and is never guaranteed to arrive — the same as it was never guaranteed, for any of the four, that they would become anything at all like what this world tried to make of them.

## VIII. THE SHAPE OF THE STORY

### 8.1 Four Directions, One World

Marcus, Elena, Whitfield, and Ashgrove survive and discover Oroboros separately, across a shared territory none of them can map to the others' positions. Each knows, at most, that the other three might still be alive somewhere on the same world. None of them, for a long stretch of their time there, knows for certain. Near-misses happen constantly and are rarely recognized as such while they're happening: a facility one of them shelters in for the night, abandoned hours before by another; gunfire in the distance mistaken for a rescue that isn't coming; a message scratched into a wall or recorded on salvaged equipment, found by the wrong person, or the right person, much later than it was left.

Their convergence, when it finally happens, changes the scale of everything rather than resolving it. Before it, the driving questions are *how do I survive* and *how do I find the others.* After it, the driving questions become *how do we get home*, and — once it becomes clear the rift that stranded them no longer exists to be reopened — *what does going home even mean, and what is each of us willing to do to make it happen.*

### 8.2 The Fracture

The four's reunion does not resolve their differences. It exposes them. Whitfield wants to reconstruct the mechanism that stranded them, believing replication is the only path home, without yet reckoning with the deep-strata implication that Oroboros's original rift was never Phantom Corridor's invention to begin with — it was a door that had simply never been *found* before, and rebuilding the key does not guarantee anyone controls what it opens. Farro wants to understand the place fully before anyone touches it again, certain that acting on incomplete knowledge is what stranded them in the first place. Marcus wants, more than anything else, an exit that does not require anyone to build a weapon first. And Ashgrove — who has by now seen enough of Oroboros's inexhaustible supply of soldiers, and enough of what the Ascended are capable of commanding, to understand its scale — begins to say the temptation out loud: that a door into a bottomless reserve of combatants drawn from every war in human history up to the present is not a horror to seal shut. It is the largest strategic asset in the history of warfare, and it is sitting open behind them.

This costs something real: not a cartoon villain turn, but the slow, resisted, partially successful argument of a man who has spent his whole career believing that anything which can end a war faster is worth the price of using it — confronted, this time, by the fact that he has personally walked through the wreckage of what "using it" has already cost, for every army and every civilian population that ever got caught underneath this world's endless front.

### 8.3 The Ending

There is no final confrontation, in the sense the four were trained to expect one, and there is no victory in the sense the living world would recognize as an ending. The First Light cannot be found in a single location and cannot be killed, because it is not a combatant standing somewhere inside Oroboros — it is what Oroboros *is*, and nothing the four are capable of carrying through a rift can end a world the way a soldier ends an enemy. Oroboros does not fall. It cannot be unmade by four people, or by four hundred, or by any number the living world could ever send after it, because what would have to be undone is not a structure sitting on top of the world — it is the world, the same corrupted Earth it has been since long before any of the four were born, and nothing short of the same authority that first let the corruption take root could ever call it back. What ends Oroboros, if anything ever does, is not written into anything the four are capable of doing. That is not their story. Their story is smaller, and it is the one that was always actually theirs to finish: survive it, understand it as fully as four living people ever manage to understand a world built to outlast them, and find a way home before it finishes wearing them down into the shape it has been trying to press them into since the day they arrived.

What they do manage, in the time they have, is not an ending. It is a handful of exits, each one paid for and each one small against the scale of everything Oroboros still holds when the four are finally ready to leave it behind.

Marcus finds his in the soldiers nobody else on Oroboros ever bothers to look at twice — the stragglers, the deteriorated, the ones who lost their unit and their coherence along with it and have spent however long since as nothing but the war, endurance with no self left underneath it to eventually go home to. What he learns, watching them long enough and closely enough that the other three stop understanding why he keeps doing it, is that a handful of them can still be reached — not spoken to, not reasoned with, but *protected*, the same reflex that raised him rerouted toward men who have long since stopped being able to protect themselves. He cannot save the soldiers of Oroboros. He can, a few times, at real cost, put himself between one of them and the ending Oroboros has planned for them for centuries, and open, in that moment, whatever door briefly stands ajar for a soul that has just been shown, for the first time since it arrived, that someone still thought it was worth defending.

Farro finds hers among civilians willing to hear, for the first time since their deaths, that they are not being punished, that what happened to them was never a sentence, and that something resembling mercy is still possible even here. Not many. Belief cannot be forced into a mind Oroboros has spent centuries teaching to trust nothing, and most of the people she reaches this way never fully cross whatever threshold she's offering before the front finds them again. But some do, and for those few, grace opens the door, and does not pretend to know what waits past it.

Whitfield's gift is different in scale from the other three, because his was never about reaching one soul at a time. What he builds, more than once, is shelter solid enough and clever enough to hold an entire fracturing settlement together through a front that should have scattered it — not a rescue exactly, but enough structure, held together long enough, that a Margin-Dweller community gets to keep existing as a community instead of getting ground down piece by piece the way Oroboros usually manages it. It costs him every skill he has and, eventually, more of himself than he expected to spend, and it does not save anyone from Oroboros the way Marcus or Farro do. It only proves, a few times, that something built with enough care can hold against a world built entirely to prove that nothing can.

Ashgrove's is the costliest of the four, because it requires him to succeed at exactly the thing his whole arc has been about learning not to want. Late in their time on Oroboros, he reaches one of the Ascended — not the oldest, not the most powerful, but one close enough to the person she used to be that something in her still answers when he speaks to her the way an officer speaks to someone worth commanding rather than someone worth using. What he offers her is not an order. It is, for the first time in longer than she can measure, a choice — and the choice he helps her make is to stop. To let the fused, absolute command she has held for centuries come apart, and to take with her, when it does, every soldier whose entire authority structure resolved through her. It is the single largest thing any of the four manage to do for anyone else on Oroboros, and it is also the clearest proof this story ever offers that Ashgrove's instinct to impose order, the thing that should have worried him most from the beginning, was never poison on its own — only a matter of what he finally chose to point it at.

None of it is everything. Oroboros holds, when the four finally leave it, essentially everything it held when they arrived — the endless front still moving, the Ascended still commanding what's left of their millions, the First Light still exactly what it has always been, patient, unkillable, and fed by a living world that has not stopped making war just because four of its people found a way out. What the four take with them is not a victory. It is a small number of names, a small number of people who are no longer there because of what the four chose to do with the little time and the little power they actually had — and the knowledge, which none of them find easy to live with afterward, that they left behind uncountably more than they were ever able to save.

The four do not go home through the door that brought them. That one is gone, along with any hope of finding it again. They build a new one — smaller, cruder, held together with what Whitfield can salvage and what Farro can calculate with nothing left to test it against — and it works exactly once, which is exactly as many times as they need it to.

What the First Light does with the rest of eternity is not something any of the four ever learn, and not something anything short of the authority that first allowed its corruption to take hold will ever be able to answer. What settles, in the end, is smaller and more human: whether the four leave as the people who arrived, or as something the world tried, for the length of their whole time in it, to bend them into; what it costs each of them to choose, at the end, not to become that; and whether Elena Farro makes it back to a five-year-old boy who has already lost one parent, or whether love, in the end, does not always get to win just because it never stopped trying.
