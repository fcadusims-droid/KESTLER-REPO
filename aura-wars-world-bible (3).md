# AURA WARS — World Bible
### Genre: comedy / battle-shounen / status-system satire.
### Setting: contemporary Japan. Tone: deadly-serious rules governing profoundly stupid stakes.

---

## 0. DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

The work runs on one engine: **treat social status as a hard magic system.**

Every rule here is written straight — ranks, mechanics, governing bodies, forbidden techniques — exactly as a real battle-shounen would treat chakra, nen, or cursed energy. The comedy is never in the narration winking. The comedy lives in the gap between:

- **how seriously the system is treated** (life-or-death gravity, ancient lineages, tournament arcs), and
- **what the system actually measures** (whether your watch is cooler than his sneakers, and whether you looked like you cared).

Nobody in-universe finds this absurd. That is the joke. The moment a character says *"isn't this all kind of dumb?"*, the spell breaks — so that line is not a release valve. It is a weapon, and it is loaded exactly once per arc.

There is a second rule under the first, and it governs everything downstream:

> **Aura is not a substance. It is a verdict.**

Nothing in this world glows. Nothing is measured out of a person like blood pressure. Aura is what a room decides about you, and the decision is real because everyone acts on it. Get this wrong and the satire collapses into a power fantasy wearing a satire's clothes. Get it right and every rule in this document snaps into place without further argument.

---

# PART I — WHAT AURA IS

## 1. THE CONSTITUTIVE LAW

**Aura** is social presence rendered as a force. It is real inside the fiction: it can be seen, gained, spent, drained, farmed, stolen, and — critically — **lost in front of everyone**, which is the only death this world recognizes.

Aura does exactly one thing: it makes onlookers perceive you as someone who matters. Everything downstream — intimidation, admiration, romantic gravity, the silence when you enter a room — is the visible effect.

But the mechanism is specific, and the specificity is the whole system:

> **Aura does not exist inside a person. It exists in the gap between a person and the people watching. It is produced by the watching. Remove the watchers and there is nothing left to remove.**

This has consequences the Circuit treats as natural law:

- **Unwitnessed Aura is zero.** A perfect Technique performed in an empty corridor produces nothing. Not "a little." Nothing. The move did not happen.
- **The Gallery is not the judge of the truth. The Gallery is the truth.** There is no appeal to a higher standard, because no higher standard exists. If the crowd says the weaker duelist won, the weaker duelist won, and there is no sense in which they were "actually" beaten. This is the single most contested fact in the world and the source of most of its moral trouble (§9.4).
- **Aura is a flow, not a reservoir.** It arrives while eyes are on you and leaves when they leave. Which is why standing still on a sidewalk is a job (§8).

### 1.1 The Rendered View and the Flat View

Because Aura is a perceptual event, the world can be described two ways, and both are accurate.

**The Rendered View** is the world as the Gallery perceives it. In the Rendered View, a yo-yo's string carves burning arcs through the air, a canned coffee opens with the sound of a struck temple bell, and a man in sunglasses casts a shadow with more shoulders than he has. This is the default register of the narration. When the story is inside a Clash, the story is inside the Gallery's eye, and the Gallery's eye is not a camera. It is a crowd.

**The Flat View** is the same event stripped of perception. A student does a yo-yo trick. A man opens a coffee. Somebody stands there in sunglasses.

Neither view is a lie. The Rendered View is what the world *is*, because the world is made of the watching. The Flat View is what a bored janitor sees, what a Reader sees underneath the Rendered one at every waking moment (§9.3), and what the story cuts to — rarely, surgically — when it wants to hurt.

There are also whole places where the Rendered View simply does not run, and the world's argument with itself happens almost entirely inside them (§14). The Flat View is the most expensive instrument in the work and is never turned on anyone in contempt. It belongs to a character the reader loves, at the exact moment their belief in the system is costing them something, and it is spent no more than twice in an arc.

### 1.2 Decay, Trickle, Burst

Aura leaks. A duelist who does nothing for a week does not keep their standing; the Gallery simply stops rendering them at the level they last held. This is not punishment. It is the natural state, the way heat leaves a room.

Three income models exist, and a duelist's whole lifestyle is determined by which one they run on:

| Model | Source | Rate | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Trickle** | Continuous low-grade witnessing. Standing, walking, existing correctly in public. | Slow, steady, endless. | Very low. Also very slow. |
| **Burst** | A Clash, a public moment, a set-piece. | Enormous. | Catastrophic. Losing a Clash costs more than winning it pays. |
| **Yield** | Aura generated by *other people's* attention to your relationship with them — orbit, debt, patronage. | Moderate, parasitic. | Collapses when the host collapses. |

Most of the Circuit lives on Trickle and dreams of Burst. This is the economic spine of the whole subculture and the reason §8 exists.

### 1.3 The First Law

> **The First Law of Aura:** *No aura is absolute. The room always contains a ceiling you cannot see, and you are never it.*

This is not a proverb. It follows mechanically from §1: since Aura is produced by watchers, and there is always a watcher whose regard you have not yet won, there is always a room in which you are nobody. The Apex of a campus Circuit is a stranger three train stops away.

**The First Law has no exceptions and the story never grants one.** Any structure that would make a character permanently the top of everything — including the protagonist — is a violation of the premise, not a payoff. Sora is not an exception to the First Law (§12.1). He is a demonstration of it.

## 2. THE TWO AXES

Aura is measured on two axes, and confusing them marks an amateur.

### 2.1 Static Aura (存在感, *Sonzaikan* — "Presence")

What you are before you do anything. Fit, posture, resting face, the way you occupy space, the confidence of your stillness. Passive. Always broadcasting. Always leaking.

Static Aura is composed of two multiplied factors, and the multiplication is the point:

**STATIC = WEIGHT × DELIVERY**

- **Weight** — what the thing objectively is in the current frame. Rarity, cost, appropriateness to the setting, appropriateness to the Theme if one is declared (§7). A limited-run sneaker has weight. So does a scar. So does a haircut two days old.
- **Delivery** — how it enters the room. Ease, timing, and above all the appearance of not having thought about it.

Because these multiply, neither dominates:

- Enormous Weight × near-zero Delivery = near zero. The richest possible item, revealed with visible pride, produces almost nothing. The Gallery reads the pride and prices it in.
- Near-zero Weight × perfect Delivery = capped. Total indifference while holding nothing is not Sigma-Class. It is Unrendered. It is a guy standing there.

This kills the beginner's error — *"indifference always wins"* — with a formula. **You cannot be indifferent about nothing.** Detachment only reads as detachment when the Gallery can see what you are choosing not to care about. The Sigma's power requires an object to refuse.

### 2.2 Kinetic Aura (演出力, *Enshutsuryoku* — "Performance")

What you do. Active. Spent to be earned. Kinetic Aura is not one skill; it is four, and a duelist is usually strong in two and dying in the others.

| Component | In-world term | What it governs |
|---|---|---|
| **Body** | 型, *Kata* — Form | Movement, stance, economy of motion, the shape you cut. |
| **Vessel** | 器, *Utsuwa* | The object you deploy and how far you transcend it (§4.2). |
| **Voice** | 声, *Koe* | Delivery, timbre, cadence, silence. The deepest of the four (§3). |
| **Substance** | 中身, *Nakami* | Whether you actually know what you are talking about. See §3.4. |

The great mistake of the mid-tier duelist is training Body and Vessel and treating Voice as decoration. Every Apex in recorded Circuit history has been a Voice specialist. There are no exceptions to this and it is the field's open secret.

# PART II — THE VOICE

## 3. VOICE AS A WEAPON

Voice is the only component that operates in all three Rounds, including the silent one. Round I is wordless, and speech in it is a flinch — which means Round I is decided by whether a duelist can keep a silence they are constantly being invited to break, and keeping a silence is a vocal discipline before it is anything else. Round II is announced. Round III is nothing but voice. This makes Voice the most-trained and least-understood discipline in the Circuit, and the place where the difference between a Contender and an Apex actually lives.

The governing principle:

> **Volume is not power. Volume is a bid. The Gallery decides whether you can afford it.**

A duelist who shouts is asking the room to grant a large amount of attention immediately. If the room grants it, the shout is enormous. If the room does not, the shout is the loudest possible way to be small, and the recoil is worse than silence would have been. Loud is high-stakes. Quiet is high-skill.

### 3.1 The Vocal Techniques

These have formal names and are taught. They are not metaphors; in the Rendered View they produce physical effects, and the narration renders them.

**The Drop (落, *Otoshi*)**
Lowering the volume so that the Gallery must lean in. Power is inverse to volume. Executed correctly, a Drop makes a room of sixty people go quiet to catch a sentence not addressed to them. In the Rendered View the air pressure changes. The Drop is the first technique every coach teaches and the last one anyone masters, because it requires the room to already want to hear you — a Drop from someone the Gallery has not granted is inaudible, and being inaudible in front of a crowd is a small death.

**The Hold (間, *Ma*)**
The pause. Silence deployed as an attack. The Hold works by transferring the burden of the moment onto the opponent: every second the silence continues, the Gallery watches *them* to see if they will break it. A duelist who fills a Hold has answered a question that was never asked, and has just spent Aura to do it. Elite duelists can Hold for nine, ten seconds. The record on the Kōyō Circuit is nineteen, held by a third-year who said nothing at all and won the Round.

**The Flat (平, *Taira*)**
Total absence of inflection. The Sigma-Class vocal signature. Every sentence delivered at the same weight, so the Gallery cannot tell which parts you care about, so the Gallery concludes you care about none of them. Extremely powerful, extremely brittle: one inflection anywhere in a Flat sequence recontextualizes the entire sequence as an act, and the collapse is total. Duelists have been Drained by a single sentence rising at the end.

**The Declaration (宣, *Sen*)**
The announcement of a Technique name in the grand register (§4.3). The Declaration is a *contract*: you have told the Gallery what is about to happen and how impressive it will be. If the Technique lands, the Declaration multiplies it. If it does not, the Gallery has your own valuation to measure the failure against, and the shortfall is charged to you in full. Duelists who Declare and miss do not merely lose the Round. They are quoted afterward.

**The Echo (響, *Hibiki*)**
Not a technique you perform — a technique you *achieve*. An Echo occurs when the Gallery repeats your line back, to each other, unprompted. It cannot be forced and cannot be faked; a seeded Echo (§8.8) can be smelled by any competent Reader. A true Echo is the highest confirmation the system offers, and duelists who have produced one talk about it the way other people talk about the birth of a child.

**The Cut (断, *Dan*)**
Interrupting. The most disrespectful move in the vocabulary and the most likely to backfire. A Cut asserts that what the opponent was saying did not merit completion. If the Gallery agrees, the opponent is annihilated. If the Gallery was still interested in the sentence, the Cutter has attacked the Gallery's attention, and the Gallery is not a thing you can attack.

**The Name-Call**
Speaking the opponent's full name, family name first, mid-Clash, at conversational volume.

This is the cruelest instrument in the system and its mechanism is worth understanding precisely. A duelist in a Clash is not a person; they are a presence, an arrangement of Weight and Delivery that the Gallery is rendering. The Name-Call collapses that. It says: *I know your name, I know you from before this, and so does everyone here now.* The presence becomes a nineteen-year-old with a full name and a hometown, standing in a courtyard. The Rendered View flickers toward Flat for half a second, and half a second is enough.

The Name-Call is technically legal. It is also the reason two Kōyō duelists have not spoken since spring.

**The Overtone (重声, *Kasanegoe*)**
The doubled voice — the effect in which a single speaker is heard by the Gallery as two voices layered a fraction apart. It is not producible on demand. It appears when a duelist's conviction, timing, and Substance align so completely that the Gallery renders their voice as larger than one throat. Perhaps four people currently alive have produced one. It is the one event this world treats as a miracle, and it treats it exactly that way.

### 3.2 The Anatomy of a Line

A line in a Clash is scored on four properties, in descending order of weight:

1. **Accuracy** — is it *true* of the opponent? An inaccurate line, however clever, recoils. The Gallery does not laugh at a good burn aimed at the wrong person; it winces, and the wince is charged to the speaker.
2. **Brevity** — power is inverse to length. The deadliest Word is one word.
3. **Timing** — arriving one beat late is worse than not arriving.
4. **Register** — the vocal technique used to carry it.

Note what is *not* on the list: cleverness, volume, and how satisfying it felt to say. All three are the standard failure modes of the Contender tier.

### 3.3 What You Cannot Fake

The Voice discipline is the hardest to counterfeit because it is produced in real time under observation. Fit can be bought. Techniques can be rehearsed in an empty gym. But a Drop requires a room that already wants you, a Hold requires nerve measured in seconds, and a Word requires having *understood* the opponent while standing in front of them.

This is why the Circuit's coaching economy is almost entirely vocal, and why a coach who can teach the Hold can charge whatever they want (§12.5).

### 3.4 Substance (中身, *Nakami*) and the Puncture

**Substance is a stat.** It is the answer to a simple question the Gallery is always, quietly, asking:

> *Does this person actually know what they are talking about?*

A duelist can deploy perfect Register, perfect Timing, a flawless Drop — and be saying something factually hollow. Empty Substance does not reduce your Aura on its own. The Gallery often cannot tell. Confidence covers an enormous amount of nothing, and a great many careers are built entirely on this fact.

But hollow Substance creates an **exposed surface**, and exposed surfaces can be attacked.

**The Puncture (穿, *Ugachi*)**
A counter-move available to any duelist who genuinely knows the subject the opponent has invoked. The Puncture is not an insult and never sounds like one. It is a quiet, correct, specific piece of information delivered flat, which reveals that the opponent's grand statement was decorated air.

The Puncture is devastating out of all proportion to its content, because of what it does to the Gallery's memory. It does not defeat the sentence it answers. It retroactively converts *every previous sentence the opponent has spoken in this Clash* into suspected decoration. One correction, and the Gallery re-renders the last four minutes.

Its cost: a Puncture requires you to demonstrate that you have studied something, which is visible effort, which is Farmer behavior, which is a vector for a Beta verdict (§6.3). The Puncture is therefore a trade — you spend your own appearance of effortlessness to destroy their appearance of substance. Sigma-Class duelists avoid it for exactly this reason and lose to it constantly.

Substance is the one place where the system accidentally rewards a person for actually knowing something, and the Circuit has never been comfortable about it. A duelist who reads is dangerous to a duelist who does not, inside a discipline that otherwise has no use whatsoever for the contents of anyone's head. It is unglamorous, unfashionable, and the quietest form of dignity the world makes available.

# PART III — THE FIGHT

## 4. THE DOCTRINE OF IMPROVISATION

The Circuit's central technical belief:

> **Prepared material decays. Improvised material appreciates.**

The Gallery can smell rehearsal. A Technique that has been performed before produces a fraction of its original yield — not because the Gallery remembers it consciously, but because a rehearsed thing arrives with the wrong texture. It has no risk in it. The Gallery is not applauding the move; the Gallery is applauding the *chance the move might not have worked*, and a rehearsed move never had one.

This produces the fundamental tension of competitive Aura and the engine of every tournament arc in the setting:

**You must prepare, or you will have nothing. You must not appear prepared, or what you have will be worth nothing.**

### 4.1 The Three Sources

Material in a Clash comes from three places, in ascending order of yield:

**The Carry (持, *Mochi*)** — what you brought. Your fit, your Vessel, whatever is in your bag. Lowest multiplier, because the Gallery knows you chose it in advance. Essential anyway; a duelist with an empty Carry is improvising with nothing.

**The Field (場, *Ba*)** — what happens to be present. A vending machine. A puddle. A pigeon. A traffic light changing. Higher multiplier, because the Gallery watched the world hand you something and watched you use it in under a second. Field mastery is the mark of a Contender.

**The Take (奪, *Ubai*)** — using something the *opponent* introduced. Their Vessel. Their line. Their gesture, returned altered. Highest multiplier in the system, and the most dangerous, because a failed Take hands the opponent their own material back with your fingerprints of failure on it.

The greatest recorded Round II on the Kōyō Circuit was won on a Take: a second-year caught the opponent's dropped folding fan mid-fall and finished the opponent's own unfinished Technique with it, correctly, in the opponent's style. The Gallery Echoed. The opponent left school.

### 4.2 The Vessel Law

**The Vessel Law:** *The lower the object, the higher the ceiling.*

A legendary sword generates almost no Aura, because it is supposed to be impressive; transcending it means nothing. A convenience-store rice ball, made transcendent, breaks the Gallery.

This is the most important mechanical rule in the system and the source of most of the comedy. Its formal statement:

**YIELD = TRANSCENDENCE ÷ EXPECTED DIGNITY OF OBJECT**

Approved high-yield Vessel classes, in the register in which coaches actually discuss them: canned coffee. Convenience-store onigiri. Umbrella, collapsible. Umbrella, transparent, from a convenience store, in weather that does not require it. Yo-yo. Spinning top. Folding fan. Plastic shopping bag. Train pass. Single chopstick. Boiled egg. Traffic cone. Someone else's bicycle. A receipt.

**Reaching for the sword.** Deploying an object of high expected dignity — a katana, a luxury watch used *as a Vessel* rather than a Reveal, a musical instrument played competently — breaks no rule, because there is no rule to break. It is simply scored as an admission that you could not win with less, and the Gallery is merciless about it. It is the most common way a wealthy duelist loses to a poor one.

### 4.3 The Answer Law

A Technique must *answer* the opponent's Technique. It must engage its logic, invert it, complete it, or refute it. A Technique performed in isolation — however spectacular — is scored as a **non-sequitur** and takes a severe reduction.

This is what makes full pre-planning structurally impossible. You cannot script an answer to a move you have not seen. The Carry gives you a vocabulary; the Clash decides the sentence.

### 4.4 Technique Naming

Techniques are named in a fixed grammar:

**[Grand Adjective] + [Cosmic or Elemental Noun] + of the + [Mundane Vessel]**

- *Ascendant Meridian of the Yo-Yo*
- *Ninefold Severance of the Folding Fan*
- *Gravemarker Descent of the Spinning Top*
- *Unbroken Comet of the Canned Coffee*
- *Sovereign Dusk of the Convenience-Store Umbrella*
- *Final Testament of the Boiled Egg*

The name is announced with total conviction (§3.1, the Declaration). It is never played for laughs by the character. The reader laughs; the duelist ascends.

**Naming a Technique you then improvise differently is legal and prestigious** — it is called **breaking the name**, and it signals that the Field gave you something better than your plan. Breaking the name well is Apex behavior. Breaking the name badly is the worst outcome available in Round II, and the one duelists have nightmares about.

## 5. THE AURA CLASH (オーラ・クラッシュ)

The formal one-on-one confrontation. Two duelists, face to face, no physical contact permitted. Around them, a **Gallery**, which is the judge, the arena, and the law.

The Clash proceeds through three fixed **Rounds (段, *dan*)**, each judged and closed before the next begins. Reordering them is a foul. Each Round measures a genuinely different competence, and a duelist strong in one is not thereby strong in another.

| Round | Name | Axis | Competence tested |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | The Fit (装, *Yosooi*) | Static | Possession × Delivery |
| II | The Craft (技, *Waza*) | Kinetic | Invention under pressure |
| III | The Word (言, *Kotoba*) | Contested | Perception and precision |

### 5.1 Round I — The Fit

Pure presence. No movement beyond the minimum, no Vessels deployed.

Duelists trade **Reveals**: single, wordless assertions of status. A wrist turned to catch light on a watch. A foot angled to show a sole. A jacket held open half a second too long. A collar. A scar. A bandage on the correct knuckle.

Each Reveal is a move, and the opponent must **answer** it — not endure it, but counter with a Reveal of equal or greater product (§2.1: Weight × Delivery). Answering a Reveal in a *different category* than it was offered is legal and scores higher if it lands: a watch answered by a haircut is an argument that time is not the axis that matters here.

The Round ends when both duelists have exhausted their Reveals, or one **flinches** — laughs, looks away, adjusts something, or speaks. Speech in Round I is always a flinch, whatever is said and however well; the Round is conducted in silence and the silence is the test. Adjusting your own fit is an automatic loss of the Round, because it tells the Gallery the fit was assembled rather than inhabited.

Winner of the Fit takes a **lead**, not the match.

Round I is also where the money shows. Weight can be purchased, and a duelist who can buy it enters the Round with a lead that no amount of nerve entirely closes. Nobody in the Circuit disputes this and nobody proposes to fix it; it is not treated as a flaw awaiting a reformer, it is treated the way weather is treated. The claim that the two axes are equally available to everyone is the polite lie the Circuit tells about itself (§10).

### 5.2 Round II — The Craft

The set-piece. Each duelist deploys a **Vessel** and performs a **Technique** with it that escalates far beyond physical possibility — in the Rendered View.

**What actually occurs:** a student does something with a yo-yo. What the Gallery perceives, and therefore what the world contains, and therefore what the page describes, is *Ascendant Meridian of the Yo-Yo*. Both are true. The Flat View is available in Round II and is almost never taken, because nobody who has come to watch a Clash wants to be told what they are looking at.

Round II is governed by the Vessel Law (§4.2), the Answer Law (§4.3), and the improvisation multipliers (§4.1). A Round II performed entirely out of the Carry, however technically clean, will lose to a scrappier Technique built out of the Field.

### 5.3 Round III — The Word

Verbal. A single exchange: a claim and a **clapback**.

Round III does not test detachment. It tests **accuracy** (§3.2). The Word must be *true of this specific opponent*, and it must be arrived at during the Clash — a Word prepared in advance almost always misses, because it was aimed at a person who did not show up.

The classic Word structure: **recontextualization.** The Word does not attack the opponent's performance; it reframes it. It reveals that everything the Gallery just watched was *effort* — that the opponent was farming the whole time, that the ease was constructed, that the indifference had a price tag. The system's deepest taboo is caring visibly, and the Word is the instrument that exposes caring retroactively.

**The Reversal Rule.** A Word can overturn any deficit, including a clean 2–0. But it is a stake, not a multiplier:

> **A Word attempted from behind carries the whole Clash. If it lands, the deficit is erased and the Clash is taken outright, however the earlier Rounds went. If it misses, the speaker does not merely lose — their remaining standing transfers to the opponent, and they go Drained where they stand.**

An ordinary loss costs a tier. A missed Word from 0–2 costs everything at once and hands it to the person standing opposite. Conceding the first two Rounds on purpose to set up a Word is a real strategy, it is called **digging**, and it is regarded roughly the way a chess player regards going down a rook for a mating net: occasionally brilliant, usually somebody who has confused desperation with a plan.

This is why the Circuit takes the first two Rounds seriously despite the third's power. A lead is not only a lead. It is the thing that means you will never have to gamble everything on one sentence.

### 5.4 Resolution and Aftermath

- Each Round has a winner, judged by the Gallery's reaction, called formally by a Reader when one officiates (§9.3).
- Best of three Rounds wins the Clash, subject to the Reversal Rule.
- The loser drops one tier (§6.1) and enters **Drained** (§6.2). A loss by missed Word costs both at once, and more besides (§5.3).

There is no blood. The stakes are entirely humiliation, and humiliation is treated here with the weight other disciplines reserve for injury, because the fear of it is universal and honest and nobody has ever outgrown it.

# PART IV — THE TOURNAMENT

## 6. RANKS, STATES, AND VERDICTS

The Circuit distinguishes carefully between three different things people confuse constantly: what tier you hold, what condition you are in, and what the room has decided you *are*.

### 6.1 Rank tiers (ascending)

| Tier | Meaning | Entry condition |
|---|---|---|
| **Unrendered** | The Gallery does not perceive you as a participant. Not an insult — a description. | Default state of every human being. |
| **Rendered** | The Gallery registers you. People look up when you arrive. | One witnessed Clash victory, or sustained successful farming (§8). |
| **Contender** | You are considered a possible threat to anyone. | Three victories, at least one against a higher tier. |
| **Framed** | The Gallery composes itself around you. Rooms arrange to include you. | Defeat of a Contender in a formal, Reader-officiated Clash. |
| **Apex** | One per Circuit at a time. | Defeat of the sitting Apex. There is no other route. |

**Promotion** requires witnesses of sufficient number; a Clash with a Gallery under roughly fifteen does not count toward tier, regardless of how good it was. This is why duelists fight in courtyards at 12:40 and not in stairwells ever.

**Demotion** is automatic and quiet. Losing a formal Clash drops you one tier. Failing to be witnessed at all — a month of absence, an illness, an internship — drops you one tier through simple decay (§1.2). Nobody announces it. You just notice, one day, that the room no longer arranges itself.

**Apex is not a trophy.** It is a target painted on your back by the First Law, and its holder is the least-relaxed person in the Circuit by an enormous margin.

**The misframe.** The conditions above are the ordinary routes, not the definition. Tier is whatever the Gallery holds, and the Gallery does not consult the table. Occasionally it renders someone at a level they never fought for — a person who happened to be standing nearby when something enormous happened, and whom the crowd afterwards refused to accept as incidental. The Circuit calls this a **misframe**. It is the one occurrence the vocabulary handles badly, because a misframed duelist holds a rank with no performance underneath it, and there is no procedure for taking it back; the tier was never awarded, so there is nobody to appeal to. Misframes are rare, deeply resented, and almost always resolved the same way — somebody goes and finds the person, to see what they are actually made of (§11.1).

### 6.2 Drained (枯渇, *Kokatsu*) — a state

The condition following Aura collapse, usually after a public loss. The Gallery stops rendering you: eye contact slides off, conversations do not pause when you approach, your presence stops changing the temperature of rooms.

Drained is **temporary and mechanical**. It is not a judgment about your character; it is closer to a wound. Recovery is possible and the route is known: quiet Trickle farming, out of sight of the Gallery that saw you fall, until decay clears the memory and you can be rendered fresh. The standard recovery is six to ten weeks. Duelists in this period are called **the quiet** and are treated with a rough, awkward tenderness by the rest of the Circuit, because everyone has been there or expects to be.

This is the nearest thing the Circuit has to convalescence, and the only stretch of a duelist's life in which nobody is measuring them.

### 6.3 Beta — a verdict

**Beta is not a state. It is a finding.** It means: *the Gallery has concluded that your effortlessness was manufactured.*

You can be Apex-tier and Beta. You can be Drained without ever being Beta. They are unrelated axes and confusing them is the mark of somebody who learned the vocabulary from the internet rather than from a coach.

A Beta verdict does not remove your Aura. It **taxes** it, permanently, at every future evaluation, because the Gallery now discounts everything you do by the suspicion that you worked on it. It cannot be appealed, disproved, or waited out. It can only be outlived by becoming somebody the Gallery cannot be bothered to remember the verdict about.

Being *seen* farming is the primary route to a Beta verdict (§8.9). This is the central occupational hazard of the entire farming profession.

### 6.4 Types of practitioner

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Auralist** | Anyone who practices Aura combat seriously. The neutral, formal word. |
| **Sigma-Class** | The apex archetype: a duelist who generates Aura through detachment. Dangerous because indifference cannot be counter-farmed — there is no leverage on someone who is not asking for anything. Requires possessing something to be indifferent about (§2.1); Sigma without Weight is just a tired person. |
| **Farmer** | A duelist who builds Aura through sustained labor rather than innate presence. Not an insult. Most champions are Farmers. Sigmas look down on them, which is itself a farming technique (§8.8). |
| **Reader** | A rare non-combatant who cannot generate Aura but can read a Gallery with precision. Judges and coaches are drawn from Readers. See §9.3. |
| **NPC** | A person with no Aura signature. The system does not render them. Considered a non-combatant. |

**On the word NPC.** The word is a slur, and its logic is airtight inside the system and monstrous outside it. Since Aura is constituted by the Gallery, a person the Gallery does not render genuinely has none — so the term is accurate, and its accuracy is exactly what makes it obscene. It is right the way a great many cruel things are right, having first arranged the frame in which they are to be measured.

There is no version of this world in which the word turns out to be technically wrong, and no consolation available in the form of secretly hidden Aura. The only answer to it is the one nobody inside the Circuit is able to say out loud: **so what.**

### 6.5 The vernacular

The formal vocabulary above is what coaches and Readers use. It is not what anyone says in a corridor. The working language of the Circuit is faster, cruder, and considerably more precise than the official one, and a duelist who speaks only the formal register is marked as somebody who learned this from a book.

| Word | What it actually means |
|---|---|
| **W / L** | A win or a loss, applied to a Round, a Clash, a haircut, a sentence, or an entire person. "He's an L" is a verdict on a life, delivered in one syllable. |
| **the Cringe** | The *instant* of annulment — the half-second in which accumulated Aura goes to zero. Drained is the state that follows; the Cringe is the event that causes it. Duelists can describe their own Cringe years later, to the second. |
| **Aura and Ego** | The diagnosis when projection tips over into announcement. Not a compliment. The ego is the leak: the moment the Gallery can see you would like it to notice, the noticing stops being worth anything. |
| **cooked** | Visibly mid-collapse and not yet aware of it. The Readers know first, then the Gallery, then — always last — the duelist. |
| **crash-out** | Total loss of composure. Distinct from a flinch, which is small, and from the Cringe, which is an annulment. A crash-out is the moment somebody stops being a duelist and becomes a person shouting in a courtyard. Recoverable, socially, in about a year. |
| **glitch** | A moment when somebody breaks their own classification — the Sigma who laughs, the NPC who says something that lands, the Apex who answers a question honestly. The Gallery cannot process a glitch in real time and simply stalls. |
| **on script** | Running stock lines and stock moves. The worst thing that can be said about a Round III, and the most common. |
| **mog** | To overwhelm someone by presence alone, with no move at all. Round I vernacular. Passive, involuntary, and unanswerable — you cannot counter a Reveal that was never made. |
| **sprezzatura** | The coaching word for studied carelessness. Brought back from Italy decades ago by a coach nobody remembers, used constantly by people who have no idea it is a loanword and would be embarrassed to find out. |

**The vocabulary is dying and should be allowed to.** None of these words are old. Hideo's generation (§15.1) had a complete parallel set for all of it, none of which survives, and he finds the current terms ridiculous in a way he cannot keep out of his voice. By the third year of the story the arriving first-years use two words the second-years already find embarrassing, and one word in this table has quietly stopped being said.

This should happen on the page. A slang that ages is true and a slang that does not is a fantasy, and the difference is visible to any reader who has ever been nineteen. The formal register — everything in §1 through §5 — does not drift, because institutions preserve their vocabulary and courtyards do not. That split is why the formal language carries the systems and the vernacular carries the jokes: one of them is supposed to outlive the story and one of them is supposed to be embarrassing by the end of it.

**The Conviction Problem.** Separately, and awkwardly, there is the duelist who believes they are apex-tier on no evidence whatsoever. The Circuit is openly contemptuous of this and privately aware that conviction is an input the Gallery reads directly, and that the Gallery does not verify claims. A sufficiently unshakeable belief in your own standing produces some of the standing it asserts. It is not a trick and it cannot be taught, and roughly half of every generation's champions started out as somebody everyone agreed was deluded.

## 7. THEMED CHAMPIONSHIPS

Formal tournaments are run under a **Theme (題, *Dai*)**, announced a fixed number of days in advance. Every element of a competitor's presentation must be legible within it: the fit, the Reveals, the Vessel, the Technique names, and ideally the Word.

The Theme is not a costume requirement. It is a **constraint**, in the sense that a sonnet is a constraint, and the Circuit discusses it in exactly that register.

### 7.1 How Themes are scored

Three properties, though tournaments are won in the interference between the first two:

**Legibility** — can the Gallery tell you are in-theme? An unreadable interpretation scores zero regardless of how good the reasoning was. The Gallery does not grade your intentions.

**Obliquity** — how far you are from the obvious reading. Direct interpretation is legible and nearly worthless. The Gallery has seen the obvious reading four times before lunch.

**Integration** — does the Theme run through *all* your material, or only your fit? A duelist whose Vessel and Word are also in-theme is operating on a different level from one who wore a color.

The optimum is a reading that arrives as **oblique, then legible** — the Gallery does not see it, then sees it, and the beat between those two states is where the Aura is generated. Score high enough on this and it is called a **Transcendent Reading**, and it wins tournaments outright.

### 7.2 The Theme Paradox

The Theme is announced in advance, which means everyone prepares. Preparation decays (§4). Visible preparation triggers Beta verdicts (§6.3).

So the Theme creates the tournament arc's whole shape: **weeks of frantic, secret work whose entire purpose is to produce something that looks like it occurred to you that morning.** Training montages in this setting are montages of people practicing looking casual, and they are played completely straight.

### 7.3 Worked example — Theme: HALLOWEEN (万聖節)

Declared eleven days out. The Kōyō Autumn Invitational.

**The trap.** Costume. A full costume is maximum Legibility, zero Obliquity, and — fatally — maximum visible effort. A competitor who arrives in a complete, well-made costume has announced that they spent eleven days on this. Two of the last three Invitational first-round eliminations were people in excellent costumes.

**Failed readings observed at this tournament:**
- Full vampire. Eliminated Round I. The Gallery was polite about it, which was worse.
- Fake blood, applied to the face, visibly recent. Legible, obvious, and it read as *asking*.
- A pumpkin. Just carrying a pumpkin. The Gallery could not determine whether this was confidence or nothing, and the Reader called it *nothing*.

**Strong readings observed at this tournament:**
- Ordinary clothes, entirely ordinary — except for dried fake blood under one fingernail, on the hand used for Reveals. Invisible until the wrist turned. Oblique, then legible, on the exact beat of a Round I Reveal.
- A plastic jack-o'-lantern candy bucket, carried as an everyday bag, with actual belongings in it, with no acknowledgment whatsoever that it was unusual. Deployed as a Vessel in Round II. The refusal to explain it *was* the interpretation.
- A competitor who wore, as her only theme element, an item of clothing that plainly belonged to a much larger person. Nothing else. Legible only in Round III, when her Word revealed whose it was.

**The winning reading.** A third-year arrived in nothing at all — no theme element, no color, no object, standing in the corridor in the clothes he had worn to a lecture. The Readers' Table prepared to score an off-theme penalty. He lost Round I, narrowly, to a competitor in a very good coat. He lost Round II to a Take he had walked straight into. Down 0–2 with one Round remaining and nothing in his Carry, he did not concede, which the Table recorded as digging (§5.3).

His Word was four words long. No two transcripts of it agree, which is itself part of the record. What the corridor understood was this: that the oldest thing anyone does on this night is go out as somebody unrecognizable; that every competitor present had spent eleven days building a person to be seen as; and that the only one who had come as himself was therefore the only one in disguise.

The Gallery Echoed. He took the Invitational on a reversal from 0–2. Two competitors who were not in the Clash at all went Drained where they stood, because the Echo had re-rendered their eleven days of work in front of everybody, and they were still wearing it.

The bracket is remembered because the whole system arrived at once and interfered with itself — obliquity, integration, a Take, a dig, a reversal and an Echo, in one corridor, inside twenty minutes. Nothing on the Kōyō calendar has managed it since.

### 7.4 The Theme economy

Because the Theme is worth so much, everything around its announcement is corrupt.

- **Theme leak.** Advance knowledge is the most valuable commodity in competitive Aura. Committee members are courted, bribed, and slept with. A leaked Theme is worth more than any item of clothing money can buy.
- **False leak.** Deliberately seeding a wrong Theme to burn rivals' preparation weeks. Regarded as legal and disgusting.
- **Theme reading services.** People who claim to predict Themes from committee composition and previous years. Most are frauds. Two are not, and both are Readers, and both are rich.

### 7.5 Recurring Themes on the circuit

**Halloween** (万聖節) · **White** (白 — monochrome, brutal, exposes fit-shopping instantly) · **Rain** (雨 — umbrellas as mandated Vessels, and the only theme in which the weather decides who weathers well) · **Convenience Store** (コンビニ — the purest test of the Vessel Law and the poor duelist's tournament) · **Childhood** (幼 — yo-yos, spinning tops, everything sincere, the theme that produces the most weeping) · **Funeral** (喪 — black only, no Kinetic escalation permitted, the Vessel deployed without a Declaration; the most technically demanding event in the calendar) · **Uniform** (制服 — all competitors identically dressed, Static Aura reduced to posture alone; the Farmer's theme, and the one Sigmas most often lose) · **Summer Festival** (祭) · **New Year** (正月 — the annual national bracket).

# PART V — FARMING

## 8. THE FARM

Aura decays (§1.2). This single fact generates an entire economy, an entire profession, and an entire caste of people who spend their lives in public places doing almost nothing with enormous intensity.

**Farming** is the sustained, non-competitive generation of Aura through Trickle income. A duelist who only fights is Drained by their third loss and gone. A duelist who farms correctly can hold Contender for years without entering a single formal Clash.

The Circuit's competitive class disdains farming and lives on it entirely.

### 8.1 Static Farming — the Stand

**Practitioners: Standers (立ち, *Tachi*).** The largest and most visible farming discipline, and the one an outsider notices first.

A Stander selects a position — a sidewalk, a station exit, the top of a stairwell, a crossing — and holds a posture for hours.

That is the entire activity. He stands there.

The posture is precise: weight on one leg, shoulder against a wall or not, one hand in a pocket or not, eyeline fixed at middle distance approximately seven degrees above the horizon, jaw closed, no phone. Never a phone. A phone is the most Aura-destructive object a Stander can hold, because it announces that the Stander requires something to do, and a Stander who requires something to do is a person waiting for a bus.

The yield per passerby is minuscule. The yield over four hours at a station exit at rush hour is considerable. Standers describe the work the way fishermen describe fishing.

In the Rendered View, a good Stander at hour three is magnificent: coat moving in a wind nobody else is experiencing, shadow pooling, the crowd parting fractionally around a spot on a sidewalk. In the Flat View, a young man is standing outside a convenience store, not moving, for a very long time, and has been there since eleven.

**Technical vocabulary of the Stand:**

- **the Post** — the position itself. Posts are inherited, sold, defended, and fought over. A good Post is worth more than a good coat.
- **the Lean** — the wall-supported variant. Lower effort, lower yield, higher endurance. The beginner's form.
- **the Free** — unsupported. Higher yield, brutal on the lower back.
- **the Count** — total unbroken duration. Recorded, compared, and bragged about. Not the vocal Hold (§3.1), and Standers find the comparison insulting: one is measured in seconds, the other in weather.
- **the Break** — any movement that resets the Count. Checking the time is a Break. Sneezing is a Break. Being spoken to and answering is a Break; being spoken to and *not* answering is not a Break and is worth double, and this is why Standers are so unpleasant to talk to.
- **weathering** — holding a Post through rain, snow, or heat. Multiplier applies. Standers who weather are regarded with genuine awe and are frequently unwell.

**The legend:** the **Standing Man of Shibuya**, who has held the same Post outside the same exit for eleven years. Nobody knows his name. Nobody has heard him speak. Two separate documentary crews have failed to make him Break. Readers who have measured him report the highest sustained Trickle ever recorded and something else they have trouble describing, which is that he does not appear to be farming anymore, and may not have been for some years, and may simply live there now.

### 8.2 Route Farming

**Practitioners: Runners (動線, *Dōsen*).**

Rather than holding one position, a Runner walks a fixed path across a campus or district, timed precisely to peak foot traffic, engineered to maximize eyes-per-minute. A well-designed route crosses three high-density corridors at their fullest moments and arrives nowhere in particular.

Routes are surveyed obsessively, timed to the second, and defended violently. **Route collision** — two Runners arriving at the same corridor at the same moment — is one of the most common causes of a spontaneous Clash, because neither can yield without the Gallery watching them yield.

Runners never look like they are going anywhere and never look like they are not. The discipline is in the walk.

### 8.3 Set-Piece Farming

**Practitioners: Stagers (演, *En*).**

Engineering a witnessed moment. The catch of a falling can. The door held at the precise instant. The umbrella opened over a stranger. The correct answer given in a lecture hall without appearing to have prepared it.

High yield, high risk, and the highest fraud rate in farming: many set-pieces are arranged. A Stager with two accomplices can manufacture a heroic moment in a crowded corridor twice a week. Detection is a Reader specialty, and exposure is instant Beta.

### 8.4 Orbit Farming

**Practitioners: Orbiters.**

Yield income (§1.2): farming off proximity to a high-Aura individual. Being seen with. Standing slightly behind. Laughing at the correct moment, at the correct volume, half a beat after everyone else.

A second form of Yield runs on obligation rather than proximity: performing favours in a way that ensures the debt itself is witnessed, so that being publicly owed does the work of being publicly liked. Both are despised and both are effective. Every Framed or Apex duelist carries between two and six Orbiters and pretends not to have noticed them. When the host collapses, the orbit collapses with him, instantly and entirely, and the Orbiters are elsewhere by the following morning, standing behind somebody else.

### 8.5 Attribution Farming

**Practitioners: none formally. Everyone informally.**

Since the Gallery decides what happened, being present when something happens is often enough to be assigned its cause. Standing near a collapse. Being in frame. Arriving just before a resolution.

Attribution is the most passive income in the system and the least controllable. It is also the mechanism by which a person who has done nothing can ascend, which the Circuit finds so intolerable that it has produced almost no vocabulary for it beyond the misframe (§6.1). See §11.1.

### 8.6 Denial Farming

**Practitioners: Ascetics (拒, *Kyo*).**

The Sigma methodology. Aura generated by publicly and calmly refusing something desirable: a challenge, an invitation, a compliment, food, a seat, an opportunity everyone can see the value of.

Requires the refused thing to be visibly worth having (§2.1 again — you cannot refuse nothing). Its practitioners live in a permanent state of mild deprivation and describe this as freedom.

**The Refusal of a Clash** is the highest-value denial available and the most dangerous: refused correctly, it says *you are not worth the four minutes.* Refused a fraction wrong, it says *I am afraid of you*, and the Gallery is very good at telling the difference.

### 8.7 Silence Farming

**Practitioners: Mutes (黙, *Moku*).**

Speaking as little as possible in a group so that each word appreciates. A Mute who has said nothing for forty minutes and then says one sentence is heard by the entire table.

The trap is obvious and Mutes fall into it constantly: silence appreciates, but only until the group stops expecting you to speak at all, at which point you have farmed yourself into being furniture. The transition from Mute to Unrendered has no warning sign. Coaches describe it as the quietest way to disappear.

### 8.8 Predation

**Practitioners: Predators. The term is not used to their faces.**

Farming by lowering someone else. A public correction. A joke at the right target. An observation about somebody's fit, delivered flatly, to a group.

Predation works because Aura is relational: the Gallery is grading a comparison, and lowering the comparison raises you as reliably as improving yourself does, for a fraction of the effort. It is the only farming method with a victim, it is the most efficient method in the system, and it is entirely legal.

**Moral Predation** is the highest-yielding form and the least examined. Rather than mocking a target, the Predator publicly *condemns* one — naming somebody's foul, somebody's Seeding, somebody's treatment of an Unrendered first-year, loudly and in front of everybody. The Gallery grants moral attention faster than it grants any other kind, and the yield is enormous. What makes it genuinely difficult is that it is indistinguishable from actually caring, including to the person doing it. Nobody in the Circuit has ever successfully told the two apart from the inside, and the Yard's position is that you should assume you are doing both.

**The Tax** is the small form: taking something of somebody's openly and without asking — a chip from their bag, the seat they were walking toward, the end of their sentence. Too minor to contest, too public to ignore. It happens outside Clashes, in corridors, all day, and it is how the tier system is enforced between formal matches.

**The Staged Fall** is the dark one. A duelist lowers themselves in public — on purpose, visibly, badly — so that the room will rise to defend them, because a defence is a form of being valued that can be produced on demand and being valued cannot. It works. That is the horrible part. The Yard watches for it, does not name it out loud when they see it, and quietly sits down next to the person afterwards.

Related methods:
- **Seeding** — placing confederates in a Gallery to shape its reaction. Detectable by good Readers, catastrophic if exposed.
- **Turning** — swaying a Gallery mid-Clash against the technically superior opponent. Legal, and the subject of the argument the Circuit has never settled (§9.4).

### 8.9 Overfarm and the Exposure

Every farming method carries the same terminal risk. **The Gallery must not see the labor.**

- **Saturation** — a Post, a route, or a technique loses yield through repetition. The Gallery habituates. Forces rotation, which forces territorial conflict, which is why the Yard (§8.10) has borders.
- **Overfarm** — farming so visibly that the activity itself becomes legible as work. A Stander who holds the same Post nine hours a day for a month is no longer generating presence; he is generating a rumor about a guy who stands outside the station.
- **the Exposure** — the moment a Gallery consciously recognizes a farm as a farm. Immediate Beta verdict (§6.3). Permanent tax.

**The Farmer's Paradox, stated plainly:** *this is labor that ceases to function the instant it is recognized as labor.* Coaches call the required condition sprezzatura (§6.5) and treat it as a skill; farmers, who have to live inside it, describe it as being on shift permanently and never being paid. Everything about the profession — its secrecy, its solidarity, its bitterness toward Sigmas who inherited what Farmers build — comes out of this one structural fact.

### 8.10 The Yard

Farmers are not organized, exactly, but they know each other. **The Yard** is the informal community of working farmers in a district: Post ownership, route schedules, saturation warnings, and a genuinely warm mutual-aid culture that exists in total contradiction to the competitive ideology everyone in it claims to hold.

Yard customs:
- Posts are inherited, not taken. Taking a Post from a working Stander is the closest thing the Yard has to a crime.
- Nobody Exposes another farmer to a Gallery. Ever. This is the one rule.
- Drained duelists are quietly given low-traffic Posts to recover on, and nobody discusses it.

The Circuit is glamorous and vicious. The Yard is four exhausted people in a stairwell at two in the morning comparing back pain. It is also, structurally, the class it sounds like: the people who do the actual labor of the Aura economy, who are looked down on by the people whose status they generate.

# PART VI — INSTITUTIONS AND SOCIETY

## 9. GOVERNING STRUCTURES

### 9.1 The Circuit

The informal but rigidly hierarchical ladder of duelists in a given institution or district. Kōyō University's Circuit is the story's home ground. Circuits nest: campus, regional, national. Each has its own Apex, and each Apex is a nobody one level up, which is the First Law expressed as an org chart.

### 9.2 The Gallery

Any crowd witnessing a Clash. Its collective reaction is not evidence of the judgment; it **is** the judgment.

The Gallery has properties that duelists study the way generals study terrain:
- **Density.** Under roughly fifteen, no tier movement (§6.1).
- **Composition.** A Gallery of first-years scores spectacle. A Gallery of upper-years scores restraint. Choosing where to Clash is choosing your judge.
- **Temperature.** Galleries arrive warm or cold and can be moved. Warming a Gallery before a Clash is legal, common, and the reason duelists arrive twenty minutes early.
- **Turnability.** A Gallery can be swung mid-Clash by a duelist who is losing on the merits, which the system does not recognize as a foul, because it does not recognize "the merits."

### 9.3 The Readers' Table

**A Reader does not measure a person. A Reader reads a room.**

Readers cannot generate Aura — this appears to be constitutional and is not a choice. What they possess is the ability to perceive a Gallery's state and trajectory with precision: where the attention actually is, where it is about to go, which reaction is genuine and which is seeded, and the exact instant a Round has been decided, usually two or three seconds before the Gallery itself knows.

They see the Flat View and the Rendered View simultaneously, all the time, and cannot switch it off. Most find this exhausting. Many find it lonely.

Readers officiate formal Clashes, call Rounds, detect Seeding, and certify tier promotions. Elite tournaments cannot run without them.

The fault line runs directly through this: the only people who can see the system clearly are the only people structurally barred from benefiting from it. Readers hold all the certification power and none of the status. A Reader's word makes and unmakes careers, and no Reader has ever been Framed, or Apex, or looked at across a courtyard the way a Framed duelist is looked at.

This produces, reliably: Readers who sell certifications. Readers who shade a call for a friend. Readers who become coaches and live through their students. Readers who refuse to officiate at all, on principle, and are treated as traitors by an entire subculture that requires them. And a quiet, generations-deep resentment that the Circuit has never once had to take seriously, because what is a Reader going to do — challenge someone?

### 9.4 The problem of the Gallery's law

If the Gallery is the law, then Turning a Gallery — winning the crowd away from a technically superior opponent — is not cheating. It is *legislating*. There is no fact of the matter about who "really" won that the Turner has falsified, because there is no fact of the matter at all. The superior duelist who loses to a Turner has not been robbed. There was nothing to rob.

Every honest person in the Circuit finds this intolerable and cannot say why, because the only available objection — *but he was actually better* — is a sentence the system has no meaning for.

The Circuit's answer, so far, has been to produce men like Jun Sasabe (§12.4) and to dislike them without ever managing to say what they have done wrong.

## 10. MONEY, CLASS, AND THE POLITE LIE

Static Aura can be purchased. Kinetic Aura cannot.

This produces a class structure the Circuit never acknowledges and everybody navigates:

- **Wealthy duelists** dominate Round I, buy Weight, and are structurally pushed toward the Sigma path, because effortlessness is much easier when the effort was money. Their characteristic weakness: they reach for the sword (§4.2). They lose Round II to people who have had to be inventive.
- **Poor duelists** are pushed toward the Farmer path and toward Kinetic specialization. Their characteristic strength: Field mastery, Vessel discipline, and a Round III that comes from actually noticing people, which is a skill you develop by needing to.
- **The Convenience Store theme and the Uniform theme** exist in the tournament calendar as the two events where this reverses, and the Circuit treats them as charming novelties rather than as the indictments they are.

The polite lie is that the two axes are equally available to everyone and that the Vessel Law equalizes the field. The Vessel Law does help. It does not equalize anything. A rich duelist can also buy a rice ball.

None of this is ever said aloud in the Circuit, and it does not need to be. Everybody navigates it from the first week, in the way people navigate a thing they have agreed not to have a word for.

## 11. THE CONFLICT ENGINE

A world about an apathetic protagonist in a competitive genre has one structural problem, and it must be solved at the level of mechanism rather than motivation: **what drags a person who does not care into an arena, repeatedly, against his will?**

Three mechanisms, running simultaneously. All three are engines of *plot*. The engine of *change* is separate and is the spine of the entire work (§17); these three exist to put Sora in rooms where it can operate on him.

### 11.1 Attribution — the ascent he never consented to

Since the Gallery determines what happened (§8.5), a person present at a collapse is assigned its cause. Sora is present at collapses. Not because he does anything — because Drained duelists keep collapsing in front of the one person in the courtyard who does not react to them, and a Gallery that has just watched a Framed duelist fall apart in front of a motionless stranger will not accept *nothing happened* as an account of what it saw.

So he ascends. Rendered, then Contender, then Framed, without having entered a single Clash. Each tier is a target painted on him by other people's need for a story. He does not want it, cannot refuse it — refusal is itself a farming move and reads as one (§8.6) — and cannot explain it, because explaining is over-explaining and the Gallery prices that in too.

The engine, stated plainly: the world keeps promoting him for things he did not do, and every promotion sends somebody stronger to find out why.

### 11.2 The tether — the one thing he answers

Sora does not care about status, ranking, or being perceived. He cares, in a low and grumbling way, about roughly two people. When the Circuit's machinery reaches one of them, he shows up. Not heroically. Irritably, and late, and having already decided this is going to take forever.

This is not the only lever and must not be the only lever, or it wears out by Arc Three. It is the lever for the moments the plot cannot otherwise reach; the ordinary work of moving him is done by Attribution (§11.1), and the work of *changing* him is done by the Contamination (§17), which requires nothing from him at all.

### 11.3 The hunt — Kaito's terror

The reigning Apex can feel that someone in this university outranks him and cannot locate them. He is correct that something is wrong and wrong about what it is. His search is an active, escalating force that generates plot without requiring Sora to want anything at all. It also, crucially, moves nothing internally — Kaito is looking for a duelist, and looking for the wrong thing is a kind of inaccuracy, and inaccurate attention does not advance the Contamination by a single step (§17.2). He can hunt Sora for two years and leave him exactly as he found him. The person doing the damage is the one who is being kind.

All three engines share one property: they are external. Nothing here requires Sora to develop an ambition, and he must not be allowed to develop one, because the moment he wants to win he is playing, and the moment he is playing he is beatable. That is the climax, not the setup.

## 12. PRINCIPAL CAST

### 12.1 Sora Ibaraki — the Unreadable

Reads to the world as an Unrendered NPC: two backpack straps, no fit, konbini rice ball, eating alone under the ginkgo at the north end of the courtyard, every day, at the same time, for reasons that are not mysterious. He is tired. He has a shift later — one in the morning to six, four nights a week, at a convenience store where nobody is looking at anybody (§14). He has been doing it since his first year. Nobody on the Circuit has ever thought to ask what a person becomes after several hundred hours a year of not being measured, because the Circuit cannot see rooms it does not attend.

**What he is not:** the hidden strongest. He is not secretly Apex, he does not have concealed Aura, and he is not the ceiling. The First Law has no exceptions (§1.3).

**What he is:** a **null**. Sora cannot be rendered by a Gallery, because rendering requires the subject to be participating in the transaction — to be, at some level, offering themselves to be seen. Every other person in this world is transmitting something, including the Standers, including the Ascetics, including the NPCs, who transmit *nothing* and are therefore correctly measured at zero.

Sora does not transmit nothing. He transmits *not applicable*. The Gallery reaches for him and closes on air, and a crowd that cannot resolve a person does not conclude *he is nobody* — it becomes obsessed. The eye returns to the place it could not focus. In the vernacular he is a permanent, walking glitch (§6.5) — except that a glitch is a moment and stalls the Gallery for a second, and he does it all day, every day, sitting under a tree with a rice ball. This is not power. It is a malfunction, and it happens to be the one malfunction this particular system cannot metabolize.

Which is why his climb through the tiers (§11.1) is a misframe (§6.1) and not a promotion. Unable to produce an image of him, the Gallery produces a *position* for him instead — a rank with nothing inside it, filed under a name almost nobody has bothered to learn. He holds standing and no rendering at the same time, a combination the vocabulary was never built to carry, and the Circuit's response to a category error has never once varied: send somebody to break it.

He does not perform detachment. He *is* detached, because he genuinely does not care, because he is mostly just tired, and because the thing everyone here is competing for has never once looked to him like a thing.

The danger escalates from there. As the Circuit fixates on him, he begins — slightly, involuntarily — to notice. And noticing is the beginning of participating, and participating makes him legible, and legible makes him rankable, and rankable makes him beatable. The story's real threat is not a stronger duelist. It is Sora starting to care, which the Circuit's attention is manufacturing in him at a rate he cannot control.

He does not know any of this. Neither does anyone else. One person is close (§12.3).

### 12.2 Kaito Amasawa — the Apex

Reigning campus Apex. Sunglasses indoors, fresh cut on a four-day cycle, sports science major, three Orbiters he pretends not to have.

Believes he is Sigma-Class. He is, in truth, the greatest **Farmer** of his generation — the effortlessness is the product of relentless, invisible, exhausting work, which by the system's own logic makes him one Exposure away from a permanent Beta verdict (§6.3). He wakes at 4:40. Nobody knows this. He would rather be Drained than have it known.

His arc is terror, and it is worth being precise about its shape: he is not afraid of losing. He is afraid of the First Law, which he understands better than anyone on this campus because he is standing at the top of it and can feel how thin the floor is. He can sense that someone here outranks him completely and he is looking for a duelist, which is why he will not find them for a long time.

He is not a villain. He is the most hardworking person in the story, and the system he has won has no vocabulary for that except as an accusation.

### 12.3 Yui Hoshikawa — the Reader

Law major. Has been in Kaito's orbit for a year and a half and reads, to a careless observer, as the girl who thinks he is the king. She is not an Orbiter in the technical sense (§8.4) and could not be — Readers generate nothing, Yield included — which leaves her the only person standing near Kaito who is not taking anything out of him. Neither of them has noticed this.

She is a latent Reader (§9.3) — she sees both Views at once and has never had a word for it. She fell for Kaito because she read him accurately as apex-tier, and she was not wrong at the time, and part of her arc is the discovery that being right about someone is not the same as being right about wanting them.

She is the first person to look at Sora and understand what is happening — not that he is strong, but that the Gallery is *failing*, that the room keeps reaching for him and coming back empty, and that this is not a measurement problem. She can see the malfunction because she is the only person who can see the machinery.

And she is the mechanism (§17.2). Accurate attention is the only thing that advances the Contamination, she is the most accurate instrument in the story, and every time she looks at him correctly she moves him one step closer to being an ordinary person who can be beaten and hurt. She works this out roughly halfway through. She cannot stop, because reading is not something a Reader elects to do, and because the alternative is looking away from the only interesting thing that has ever happened to her.

She is also the first person who can see what Kaito costs himself, and she has to decide what to do with that, and there is no version of that decision that is kind to everyone.

### 12.4 Jun Sasabe — the Turner

Contender tier, undefeated, and stalled there deliberately.

Sasabe wins by Turning Galleries (§8.8, §9.4). He arrives early, warms the crowd, seeds it lightly, reads the room's mood better than his opponents read their own material, and beats duelists who are straightforwardly better than him at everything except the only thing that counts. He has never challenged Kaito. Ask him why and he will tell you without embarrassment that the Apex's Gallery has already made up its mind, and that he does not fight rooms he cannot move.

**He has committed no foul.** He will say so, calmly, to anyone who raises it, and he will be right, and nobody will be able to explain why they want to hit him. He is the setting's argument with itself, walking around in a nice jacket. If the story gives him a comeuppance that proves he was cheating all along, the setting has flinched.

### 12.5 Rei Mizoguchi — the coach

Retired Reader, runs the Kōyō Readers' Table, teaches the Hold to anyone who can pay and a few who cannot. Thirty years of watching other people be looked at.

Cynical in the specific way of someone who understands a system completely and has never been allowed inside it. She explains this world to anyone who will listen and is grieved by it constantly, and she is the one person who will say out loud what §9.3 only implies: that she has spent her life certifying other people's worth in a currency she is not permitted to hold.

### 12.6 Tetsu Karakida — the Yard

Second-year, Rendered tier, working Stander with a mediocre Post outside the west gate. Earnest, unfashionable, extremely bad at the Flat, and Sora's only real friend.

Tetsu believes in the Circuit completely and will never be good at it. He is one of Sora's two tethers (§11.2), the story's entry point into the Yard (§8.10), and the character whose ordinary, un-transcendent dignity is the argument the whole work is quietly making. When the story needs to show what the system costs, it should not show an Apex falling. It should show Tetsu, in the rain, weathering a Post for four hours, for almost nothing, because he wants it so much.

He carries Arc One (§19). This is not a supporting role temporarily promoted; the reader has to love somebody before they can be shown a machine, and Sora at the opening of the story wants nothing, and a story about a person who wants nothing must be carried by a person who wants something badly and cannot have it.

The four adults who carry the critique — Hideo, Michiko, Ryū and Sae — are in §15, because they belong to the argument rather than to the Circuit and putting them here would file them as mentors, which is exactly what they must not be.

Still unwritten, and load-bearing when they arrive: the regional Apex above Kaito, the rival Circuit, the leaked-Theme scandal, and the Standing Man of Shibuya — who should stay off the page for a very long time.

# PART VII — THE OUTSIDE

## 13. THE TWO NUMBERS

Every room runs two hierarchies at once. The Circuit has built a complete instrumentation for one of them and has no instrument at all for the other.

The first number is **Aura**: who is watched. Who the room orients toward. Who is central, visible, dominant, remembered. This number is real, it is measurable — Readers measure it professionally — and everything in this document is a technology for raising it.

The second number has no name in Circuit vocabulary, which is itself the finding. It is: **who would come.** Who is asked for help and gets it. Who people are quietly glad to see. Who three separate people would drive to a hospital at four in the morning without being asked twice.

The two numbers are not opposites and not the same, and their overlap is very much smaller than anyone inside the Circuit assumes. Roughly a third of the most-watched people in a room are also among the most-wanted; roughly a third of the most-wanted are among the most-watched. The rest of both groups are invisible to each other. Most Apexes in Circuit history have been in the first group and not the second, and none of them found out in time, because the Circuit's entire apparatus is built to keep the question from being asked.

This is not an argument that the second number is superior. It has its own cruelties: people can be beloved and powerless, and being liked has never once stopped a room from arranging itself around somebody else. And Aura does real work — it is how a nineteen-year-old who arrived at this university with nothing discovers that he can affect a room at all, which is not a small thing to discover and cannot be discovered any other way.

The criticism is narrower and harder to dismiss than "status is meaningless":

> **The Circuit is not wrong that Aura is real. It is wrong that Aura is the only number. A system with a single gauge will eventually mistake the gauge for the engine, and this one did that some time in the last century.**

Nobody can measure the second number. There is no Reader for it, no Table, no certification, no tier. Which means nobody trains for it. Which means the people who have it usually do not know they have it, and the people who lack it usually find out at a funeral.

## 14. THE FLAT PLACES

The Rendered View requires a Gallery. Remove the Gallery and there is nothing to render, and there are places in this world where that removal happens structurally rather than by choice. The Circuit's young avoid them by instinct and cannot say why. The Circuit's old live in them.

**The sentō.** A public bath is the most complete negation of the system that exists, and it achieves this with no philosophy whatsoever. There is no fit. There is no Weight. There are no Reveals available, because everything with which a Reveal could be made is in a basket by the door. Static Aura in a sentō is structurally zero for everybody present, simultaneously, and what remains is a room of wet tile containing several men, some of whom are old, none of whom are being scored. A first-year who wanders into one usually leaves within four minutes and describes the experience afterwards as *weird*, which is the closest available word for the sensation of the machinery being switched off while you are still inside it.

**The night shift.** Between one and six in the morning, a convenience store's Gallery density sits permanently below the rendering threshold. Five hours a night, four nights a week, of drunks, taxi drivers, one other tired person, and nobody looking. This is not a spiritual practice and nobody involved would describe it as one. It is a job. It is also, cumulatively, several hundred hours a year in which a person is not being measured, and it changes people, and the Circuit has never noticed because it cannot see what happens in rooms it does not attend.

**The end of a Yard shift.** Four exhausted farmers in an izakaya at two in the morning, off the clock, generating nothing. The ideology says this should not be comfortable. It is the most comfortable hour any of them have.

**Illness, injury, and funerals.** The Circuit has no rule covering a person with a fever. It does not need one. Nobody has ever challenged somebody who was visibly unwell, nobody has ever explained why not, and no coach has ever been asked to justify the exemption. The system has an enormous hole in it exactly where the human body is, and everybody steps around the hole without discussing it.

The in-world term for entering such a place deliberately is **going flat**. Young duelists experience it first as fear — the specific fear of not being rendered, which is indistinguishable from the fear of not existing — then as relief, then as something they cannot explain to their friends without sounding like they have given up.

**The location rule, which matters more than any line of dialogue:** the elders' argument only works inside a flat place. The same sentence spoken in a courtyard, in front of a Gallery, is not the same sentence. In the courtyard it reads as cope, it reads as Beta, and the Gallery prices it accordingly — and the Gallery is not wrong to, because a person defending their own worthlessness at volume is doing something else. Every scene in which the critique lands must be located somewhere the system is not running. Every scene in which it is delivered in the open must fail.

## 15. THE ONES WHO LEFT

The critique of the system does not come from inside the story's own narration, which never winks (§23). It comes from people who used to be in the Circuit and are not anymore, and it comes badly, in fragments, inside anecdotes about knees.

**How these characters speak.** They never say that status does not matter. That sentence is unavailable to them, because they know it is false and they lived through the proof. They say specific things about specific bodies at specific times. They are allowed irony — this is the single clearest marker of being outside — while the young are not, and the young hear the irony as disrespect for two years before they hear it as freedom. They do not lecture, they are frequently wrong, and at least two of them are still competing in ways they would deny.

**How the advice lands.** It does not. Nobody in this story is talked out of anything in a conversation. The purpose of these scenes is not persuasion; it is deposit. The sentence goes in, is rejected on the spot, sits there for two years, and surfaces the night the character finally breaks — at which point they will not remember who said it.

### 15.1 Hideo Tsuchiya, 68

Owns the Kanda-yu sentō, which he inherited. Regional Apex in 1979, back when the thing had a different name and the word for a Clash was a word nobody uses now.

He is not wise. He is a man with a ruined hip who won everything available to him and who, at his father's funeral, counted the room and got to nine. His argument is the one in §13 and he did not reason his way to it; it was administered to him.

He is also still competitive at sixty-eight. He checks his reflection in the changing-room mirror. He knows he does it, he hates it, and he has not stopped, and if a young duelist points this out he will not deny it. He is not free of the system. He is further along in it, which is a different thing and much less inspiring, and the story should never let him be mistaken for the first.

His position, when he can be got to state it, is not that it did not matter. It is: *it mattered. That is the problem. It mattered enormously and it was still the wrong thing to be measuring.*

### 15.2 Michiko Arai, 54

Shift manager at the konbini where Sora works nights. Never entered the Circuit, was never rendered by any Gallery, and is — precisely, technically, by the definition in §6.4 — an NPC.

She is also the happiest person in the book. This is not a twist and is never presented as one. She has a husband who is fine, a dog with a bad ear, four women she has known since she was fifteen and speaks to weekly, and no idea whatsoever that any of this constitutes an argument about anything.

**She never gives advice.** She talks about her weekend. The devastation is structural rather than rhetorical: an entire life is being described, in detail, in front of a boy whose world has no instrument capable of registering that it is there. The moment she explains herself, or delivers a moral, or says anything resembling *you know, none of that stuff matters* — she stops being a person and becomes a device, and the argument she embodies collapses into the thing it was supposed to refute.

### 15.3 Ryūnosuke Kanda, 41

Did not leave. Twenty-two years on the same Post outside the west station. Talks about a comeback constantly and specifically, with dates.

He is not a joke and must never be written as one. He is what the elders' advice is for, and he is standing evidence that the advice is not automatically taken by the people who need it most. On a good day he is better at Static Aura than anybody currently on the Kōyō Circuit, and this is exactly the trap: he possesses one genuinely extraordinary skill, it is worth nothing anywhere outside a two-hundred-metre radius, and he knows this, and knowing it is the reason he cannot put it down.

He is also the strongest counter-argument to Hideo, in living form. Leaving is not equally available to everyone. Hideo left with a bath house. Ryū has a Post.

### 15.4 Sae Fujimoto, 34

Framed tier at Kōyō twelve years ago. Now a pharmacist. Comes back once a year for the New Year bracket and watches from the back with her coat on.

She disagrees with the other two, out loud, and she is not obviously wrong. Her position: the Circuit taught her to stand in front of a room and not flinch, she would not have her job or her marriage without that, and the men who tell you it was all worthless are, in her experience, mostly the ones who lost. She would do it again. It also ate her twenties, and she will say both of those things in the same breath without appearing to notice the contradiction, because there isn't one.

She exists so that this section is an argument rather than a sermon. If she is removed, the elders become correct, and the moment the elders become correct the story has started lying.

## 16. WHAT THE ELDERS GET WRONG

Everything in §13 to §15 is the case against the Circuit. It is a good case. It is also incomplete in five specific ways, and the story is obliged to know all five, because a critique that cannot survive its own counter-argument is decoration.

**They are describing a system that has changed.** Hideo's Circuit was ephemeral. You lost, and in a month the room had genuinely forgotten, because rooms forget and there was no mechanism preventing it. The permanence of the modern Gallery — the way a Cringe is repeated, recounted, and available to be recounted again three years later by somebody who was not present — is a real structural difference and not an exaggeration by the young. When Hideo says *it passes*, he is describing a mechanism that no longer works the way it did, and the young are right to notice, and they cannot articulate what they have noticed, so they lose the argument while being correct.

**They confuse having survived with having understood.** Hideo did not solve anything. He got older, and the specific capacity to sit in a room and not require it from the room is very largely a function of having gotten older. Dispensing that as advice to a nineteen-year-old is close to advising somebody to be taller. He offers it as wisdom because the alternative is admitting that the difference between him and Ryū is partly a bath house.

**They have the causation backwards more often than they think.** The elders' account is that the Circuit damages people. Frequently the truth runs the other way: a place with explicit rules, a visible scoreboard, and a clear answer to the question *am I doing all right* is an enormous relief to somebody whose life outside contains none of those things. Damaged people find the Circuit. Hideo cannot see this, because his own story requires the Circuit to have been the villain rather than the shelter.

**They discount what the system actually delivers.** Nerve. Real skill. The Yard, which is the warmest institution in this world and could not exist without the cold one that produced it. And most seriously: for a particular kind of person — the ones the university's ordinary hierarchies had already sorted out and set aside — the Circuit is the first room that ever rendered them at all. Telling that person the number is meaningless is telling them that the only place that ever let them in was a lie, and it is not obvious that this is a kindness or that it is true.

**They speak as though the damage were universal, and it is not.** The great majority of people pass through the Circuit and come out unmarked; it was four years, some of it was humiliating, they are fine. A small minority is permanently reshaped by it. That minority is not randomly selected: it is overwhelmingly the people for whom their own worth was already contingent on the room before they ever walked into it, who arrive with the fault already present and find, in the Circuit, the one environment perfectly designed to load it. Which means the honest statement is not *the Circuit destroys people* and not *the Circuit is harmless*, but something considerably less quotable than either.

### 16.1 The separation, stated properly

The advice the elders are groping toward, and mostly fail to say cleanly, is not *status does not define you*. Inside a Clash that sentence is not even wrong; it is meaningless, because inside a Clash status is the only thing there is, and a duelist who believes otherwise will lose and deserve to.

The accurate version is mechanical:

> **Inside the Clash, Aura is real and the rules hold and you should play to win. Outside it, no Gallery is rendering, and a measurement that is not being taken is not a measurement. The error was never caring about Aura. The error is Trickle-farming your entire waking life — which means never once being off the clock, which means never finding out who you are in a room where nobody is scoring.**

And underneath it, the one thing an elder can actually hand to somebody, which is not wisdom and not consolation but a permission:

> **You are allowed to be unwitnessed.**

The young hear this and understand it to mean *you are allowed to be nobody*, which is why it takes two years, and why it has to have been said early enough that it is already in them when they need it.

Even this is not general advice. It is the correct advice for a specific person in a specific condition, and the elder who cannot tell which person he is talking to will do damage with it. Hideo, most of the time, cannot tell.

# PART VIII — STORY ARCHITECTURE

## 17. THE CONTAMINATION

This is the mechanism the entire work rests on, and it is the one thing in this document that must be built rather than asserted.

Sora is a null because he is not participating in the transaction (§12.1). The Gallery cannot render him because rendering requires the subject to be, at some level, offering themselves up. The story's actual physics is that **this condition is destroyed by being looked at**, slowly, without his consent, and by nothing else.

The mechanism is not psychological and does not require him to want anything. It is this:

> **You cannot be looked at continuously without eventually forming a model of how you appear. The instant a model exists, a preference about it exists. A preference is participation. Participation is legibility. Legibility is a rank, a Cringe, and a way to lose.**

The Circuit's attention is therefore not a threat to Sora in the ordinary sense. It is a solvent. It is dissolving the only thing that makes him what he is, at a rate he does not control and cannot perceive, and the people doing it are not his enemies — they are curious.

### 17.1 The five stages

Each stage has an observable symptom, because a mechanism that cannot be drawn is not a mechanism.

**One — Registration.** He notices that he is being looked at. This costs nothing and changes nothing internally. The symptom is that he moves. Not to be seen better — to be seen less. He shifts to the far side of the ginkgo. It is the first deliberate act he has ever performed in relation to a Gallery, and it is already a move, and he does not know that.

**Two — Modelling.** An involuntary picture of how he appears begins to assemble. The symptom is prediction: he catches himself knowing, half a second early, how a room is about to react. This is the Reader's faculty (§9.3) arriving in somebody who is also renderable, which is a combination the system has no name for.

**Three — Preference.** He finds he prefers one prediction to another. The symptom is repetition: he does a small physical thing a second time, because the first attempt felt wrong. He has never in his life done anything twice.

**Four — Investment.** Something lands, and he feels the small warm thing that everybody else in this world has felt every day since they were fourteen. He recognises it. It frightens him more than any defeat could, and he cannot describe why to anybody, including himself.

**Five — Legibility.** The Gallery can now render him, because he is participating. He acquires a tier that means something, an Aura that can be measured, a Cringe that can happen to him, and an opponent who can beat him. He becomes, for the first time, ordinary.

### 17.2 The rate control

The Contamination does not advance with crowd size. A courtyard of two hundred people looking at Sora moves nothing, because a crowd looks at a shape.

It advances when somebody looks at him **accurately** — when a single person holds a correct model of him and he can feel that they do. General attention is weather. Specific attention is a solvent.

**This makes Yui the accelerant, not Kaito.** Kaito is hunting a duelist and will keep failing to find one; his search generates plot and does not advance the mechanism by a single step. Yui reads Sora correctly the first time she looks (§12.3), and every subsequent accurate reading moves him a full stage, and she is the only person in the story who can watch it happening in real time.

She therefore has to decide whether to stop looking. She cannot. Reading is not something a Reader elects to do, and the one thing she is better at than anybody alive is the thing that is destroying him.

### 17.3 It is not a fall

The Contamination must never be written as a tragedy of corruption, and Sora must never be treated as having been pure.

He becomes a person. He becomes vain in small ways, and hurt by small things, and pleased when a room turns toward him, and afraid of Thursday. He joins everybody. Whether that is a loss or an arrival is the question the entire work exists to hold open, and it must be held open — the moment the story decides, it becomes either a fable about the corruption of innocence, which is a story that has been told, or a fable about the joy of connection, which is a lie about the Circuit.

It is also **irreversible**. You cannot un-know how you appear. The proof is already in this document: Hideo is sixty-eight, has left, owns a bath house, and still checks the changing-room mirror (§15.1). Nobody gets the null back. This is the strongest reason the story is not a redemption arc and cannot become one.

## 18. WHERE THE CATHARSIS LIVES

This world contains four structural refusals, all deliberate and all correct:

1. Sora cannot want to win (§17.3, §11).
2. The critique never wins an argument on the page (§15).
3. Nobody is the ceiling (§1.3).
4. Nobody is converted in a conversation (§15).

Stacked without a counterweight, these starve a reader on principle. A work that refuses satisfaction four different ways is not rigorous, it is withholding, and the difference is invisible to the author and obvious to everybody else.

The counterweight is already in the system and simply has to be authorised.

### 18.1 The payout rule

> **The first number generates the plot. The second number generates the catharsis.**

Aura victories (§13, the first number) are always taxed. You win and the room will have forgotten by Thursday, and now you are a target, and the First Law is standing behind you. Every first-number win in this work arrives slightly sour, and that souring is not cynicism — it is accuracy.

Second-number events are never taxed. They are the only unqualified emotional payouts the work contains, and they are always the same shape: **somebody comes.** Somebody is waiting at the bottom of the stairs. Somebody sits down next to somebody without saying anything about it. Somebody drives to a hospital at four in the morning and does not mention it afterwards.

### 18.2 Pacing and placement

- **One per arc.** Not more. The second number is scarce by definition; making it frequent would turn it into a currency, which is the exact error the Circuit made with the first one.
- **Never at the climax of a Clash.** Fusing the two numbers cheapens both — it says the crowd's approval and being loved are the same event, which is the lie the entire work is written against.
- **Always in a flat place (§14).** After the match, in the quiet, off the clock.
- **Never named.** No character observes that this is what matters. The instant somebody says it out loud it becomes the moral of an episode, and §15 already forbids that.

The budget for the whole work: comedy continuous, first-number wins frequent and always taxed, second-number payouts rare and never taxed. That ratio is the reader's entire diet, and it is enough, and nothing else is.

## 19. ARC ONE — TETSU

The first arc does not belong to Sora. It belongs to Tetsu Karakida (§12.6), because a reader has to love somebody before they can be shown a machine, and because Sora at the start of the story wants nothing and a story about somebody who wants nothing has to be carried by somebody who wants something badly.

**What Tetsu wants:** to be picked. The Kōyō Autumn Invitational takes eight duelists from the Circuit. He has been Rendered tier for two years, he has never been picked, and he wants it once — not to win it. To be on the list.

**Why he cannot have it:** his Static is hopeless. He is bad at Delivery, worse at the Flat, and his Post outside the west gate is mediocre and always will be. He has one genuine faculty and it is invisible on a scoreboard: he notices people. Which is Round III competence (§3.2, §5.3), which is the thing nobody trains, which he has because he needed it (§10).

**The shape:**

1. He is not picked. Again. He takes it well in public, which is its own kind of terrible.
2. He does what the Circuit says to do, and buys Weight he cannot afford. His Round I improves measurably. Somebody notices him trying, and the word *Beta* is used about him for the first time, at a distance, by people who will not remember saying it.
3. He asks Sora for help, which is ridiculous — Sora is Unrendered, Sora is an NPC, Sora is eating a rice ball. Sora, irritably and mostly to end the conversation, says one accurate sentence about Tetsu's next opponent. Tetsu wins on it. Neither of them understands what happened.
4. The Gallery understands, incorrectly (§8.5). It attributes the win to the motionless person who was standing there. This is the first misframe (§6.1) and the beginning of everything.
5. Tetsu is picked. He goes out in the first round, badly, on the Theme, in a costume he worked on for nine days.
6. At the bottom of the stairs, the Yard is waiting. Nobody mentions the match. Somebody has already ordered.

That last beat is the arc's whole payload and it is a second-number event (§18). It is also the first time the reader is shown that this world contains something the Circuit's instruments cannot see, several hundred pages before anybody in the story says so.

**What Arc One establishes without explaining:** the three Rounds, the Theme system, the Vessel Law, the Beta verdict, the Yard, Attribution, the misframe, and Sora's first step into the Contamination (§17.1) — all of it as consequence rather than exposition, and none of it named.

## 20. THE RENEWAL ENGINE

The premise — a serious system measuring stupid things — is a single joke, stated on page one. A single joke does not serialise. What serialises is a *generator*, and this world has three, all of them already built and none of them dependent on the absurdity increasing.

**The Theme (§7).** Every tournament imposes a new constraint set, and constraint is the renewable comedic resource. Funeral is not the same comedy as Convenience Store; Uniform is not the same comedy as Childhood. Each Theme produces its own failure modes, its own trap, its own cast of people who read it wrong. This generator is effectively bottomless and it is the primary engine of every arc after the first.

**The Vessel Law (§4.2).** The world contains an inexhaustible supply of undignified objects and the comedy is object-specific. This is the texture generator: not what carries an arc, but what makes any given page funny.

**The vernacular drift (§6.5).** Words go out of date on the page. A term that was devastating in Arc Two is embarrassing by Arc Six, and the people still using it are marked by it.

**The anti-escalation rule.** The absurdity never increases. The *gravity* does. Chapter eighty is not funnier because the Vessel is more ridiculous than the yo-yo was; it is funnier because a convenience-store rice ball now carries eighty chapters of accumulated seriousness, and the reader has been trained. The joke is supposed to deepen, not widen. Widening it — bigger, sillier, more — breaks the Deadpan Doctrine (§23) and turns the work into the parody it was written to avoid being.

**The honest limit.** Comedy is the texture and cannot be the spine. Deadpan at this register is not load-bearing across a hundred chapters on its own, and any plan that assumes it is will produce a magnificent first twenty and a hollow fifty. The spine is the Contamination (§17). Everything else is what the pages are made of while it happens.

## 21. MEDIUM AND VISUAL GRAMMAR

**This is a manga.** Fixing that is not a formality: half of this system is a perceptual double-exposure, and a device that lives in narration will not survive contact with a page that has no room for narration.

So the Rendered View and the Flat View (§1.1) are not narrated. They are **drawn differently**, and the reader learns the difference in one page in Chapter 1 and is never told it again.

| | Rendered View | Flat View |
|---|---|---|
| **Line** | Heavy, varied, brush-weighted | Thin, uniform, mechanical |
| **Tone** | Dense screentone, deep blacks, speed lines | None. Bare white. |
| **Panels** | Bleeding, breaking the gutter, irregular | Rigid rectangles, generous even gutters |
| **Figures** | Drawn tall — roughly one and a half heads above the model sheet | Drawn at correct height. The same person, smaller. |
| **Backgrounds** | Abstracted into pattern and light | Accurate. A courtyard. A vending machine. A drain. |

The cut between them needs no caption and must never receive one.

**The Gallery is drawn in the margins.** Faces in the gutter, outside the panel borders, watching. When a Gallery Turns (§8.8), the faces in the margin change *first* — before anything inside the panel does. The reader learns to read the edges of the page before they learn to read the duel, which is exactly the skill a Reader has (§9.3).

**Sora is the only character in the book with no tone.** Not as an effect and not at dramatic moments — always, in every panel, from page one. Flat line, white fill, correct height, in scenes where everybody around him is rendered. It should read initially as a stylistic quirk, then stop being noticed by Chapter 4, and then come back the first time Yui looks directly at him and the reader remembers that they have been looking at a hole this whole time.

And as the Contamination advances (§17), tone begins to appear on him. Very slightly. A single gradient on a sleeve. Nobody comments.

**Technique and Word.** Round II Techniques take the double spread — that is what double spreads are for. Round III Words take one small panel with a great deal of white around it, because power is inverse to length (§3.2) and a page can say that better than a sentence can.

## 22. FORBIDDEN SHAPES

There are two ways this work fails, and they are opposite, and every constraint below prevents one or the other.

The first failure is collapse into a story that already exists: the tired unremarkable boy who turns out to be the strongest, in a world that measures status. The second failure is collapse into an illustrated essay: a beautiful machine that turns over perfectly and moves nobody.

**Against the first:**
- Sora is never revealed to be secretly powerful. There is no concealed capacity, no dormant tier, no late reveal.
- Sora never trains. He has nothing to increase. There is no power-up in this work and no arc structured around him getting better at anything.
- Sora never enters a tournament in order to win it.
- Aura never becomes physically real. The instant it can lift an object, this is a different genre and a considerably worse one.
- The absurdity never escalates (§20).
- No antagonist is cheating. Sasabe (§12.4) is playing correctly, and the discomfort is the point.
- No adult is correct (§16). Anybody who arrives with the answer is wrong on arrival.

**Against the second:**
- Every arc contains somebody who wants something concrete and can be prevented from having it. In Arc One that is Tetsu (§19); it is never Sora.
- Every arc contains exactly one second-number payout (§18), unnamed and unremarked.
- No character is introduced to occupy a position in the argument. If a character's only function is to be right about something, they are cut. Sae (§15.4) survives this test because she is wrong about half of what she says and knows it; Michiko (§15.2) survives because she never argues at all.
- The comedy never comments on itself, and nothing in the finished pages ever acknowledges that any of this is a satire (§23).

# PART IX — DOCTRINE

## 23. THE DEADPAN DOCTRINE (tone rules — non-negotiable)

1. **The narration never winks.** Aura combat is described with the gravity of a war epic. Always.
2. **No character inside the Circuit finds the system absurd** — except as a deliberate, rare, devastating move (§5.3). Sincerity is the default there; irony is a finisher, and it is spent once an arc.
3. **Outside the Circuit, irony is free.** The elders (§15) joke about all of this constantly and it costs them nothing, because no Gallery is rendering them. This asymmetry is the generational divide made mechanical: the old have a shield the young are forbidden. It is also why the young hear the joke as contempt long before they hear it as an exit.
4. **The stakes are social and therefore real.** Nobody dies. Drained is written as genuinely devastating, because public humiliation *is* genuinely devastating, and pretending otherwise makes the comedy hollow.
5. **The lower the object, the higher the drama.** Always reach for the most mundane Vessel. Grandeur applied to grandeur is nothing.
6. **The Flat View is a weapon, not a joke.** It is never deployed to mock a character the reader does not love, and never more than twice per arc.
7. **Normal people exist and are fine.** The world is full of people who never enter the Circuit and live good lives. The Circuit believes it is the world. It is a subculture in a university district in a city of nine million. That is the widest joke and the quietest truth.
8. **No one is exempt from the First Law**, including Sora, including the Standing Man, including whoever turns out to be above them.
9. **Nothing in the finished pages acknowledges that this is a satire.** No character, no caption, no visual joke aimed over their heads at the reader. The satire is the machine, not a remark about the machine (§22).
10. **The critique never wins an argument on the page.** Nobody is talked out of the Circuit in a scene (§15). The elders deposit sentences; the sentences surface years later, and by then nobody remembers who said them.

## 24. THEMATIC SPINE

The work asks one question: **what is it worth, to be perceived as more than you are?**

The system rewards the performance of not-caring. The only person it cannot touch is the one who genuinely doesn't. Which means the game, played to its end, can only be won by refusing to play — and the instant you notice you have won, you are performing, and you have lost.

But the sharper edge is underneath that, and it is a class argument rather than a spiritual one:

**Aura is produced by labor, and the system's highest value is the concealment of labor.** Kaito wakes at 4:40 and must never be caught at it. Tetsu stands in the rain for almost nothing. The Standers, the Runners, the Yard, and the Readers do all the actual work of a status economy that treats work as the one unforgivable thing. Everyone is exhausted and everyone must appear to be resting.

Underneath even that is the instrumentation problem (§13). The Circuit built one gauge, calibrated it beautifully, and forgot to ask what it was failing to read. Everybody here can tell you exactly how watched they are. Not one of them can tell you how many people would come.

And Sora is not the hero because he is the strongest. He is the hero because he is the only person in the courtyard who is not being *charged* for existing, and the reason he is not being charged is that he is too tired to have ever opened an account.

The First Law, restated as the story's heart: **you are never the ceiling.** The comedy is watching everyone farm frantically toward a top that does not exist. The soul is the tired kid under the ginkgo who never looked up — not because he was above it, but because he was never in it, and because a thing that only exists when you look at it stops existing if you simply, exhaustedly, don't.

## 25. OPEN FRONTIERS

Everything below extends from the existing rules without breaking them.

- **The national ladder.** Nested Circuits, each Apex a nobody one level up. The New Year bracket. What an Apex looks like at twenty-nine.
- **Forbidden techniques.** Not fouls — the system has almost no fouls. Things that are legal and that nobody does: sustained Predation of an Unrendered person; Exposing a Farmer; the Name-Call against someone already Drained.
- **The Reader question.** What happens the first time the Table refuses, collectively, to officiate.
- **Aura in the working world.** Interviews, sales floors, politics. The Circuit thinks it invented this. Nobody in an office has ever needed a name for it.
- **The Standing Man.** Eleven years. What he was before. Whether he can Break. Whether he wants to.
- **The Flat View, extended.** A whole chapter written in it, once, at the worst possible moment.
- **Kanda-yu.** The sentō as a recurring set. Who else is in there. What Hideo was like in 1979, told badly, by somebody who was there and disliked him.
- **The second number, measured.** What happens the first time somebody tries to build an instrument for it, and why the attempt is both moving and doomed.
- **Ryū's comeback.** He has a date. The date arrives.
- **The exemption nobody explains.** Why a sick person cannot be challenged, and what happens the first time somebody does it anyway.
- **Stage Five.** What Sora is like once the Contamination completes (§17.1) — which is the ending, and which should be written before anything else is, so that the whole run is aimed at it.
- **The first tone on his sleeve.** The chapter where a single gradient appears and nobody in the story notices (§21).
