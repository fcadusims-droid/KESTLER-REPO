# ORGANICRON

## I. The Conclusion Humanity Reached

The wars ended the way all of Earth's wars with Cybertron ended: with cities rebuilt over craters, with treaties signed by species that had never intended to share a planet, and with the quiet understanding that nothing had actually been settled.

Autobots could be killed. Decepticons could be killed. Cybertron still existed. And beyond Cybertron there was a galaxy that had already proven, repeatedly, that it contained civilizations for whom Earth was a resource, a battlefield, or a rumor.

The governments that funded what came next did not phrase their conclusion in those terms. What they wrote in the appropriations documents was that terrestrial defense required a qualitative rather than quantitative advance. What they meant was simpler and harder to say out loud: humanity would never win by building better versions of what the Cybertronians already were. Reverse-engineering an alien war machine produced, at best, a worse alien war machine operated by a species with soft tissue and a forty-year career span.

So the objective was inverted. Not a weapon. Not a soldier. A successor technology to biology itself — something that did not inherit the specific failures of either species that had fought over the planet.

Humans were creative, adaptive, capable of improvisation no machine had matched. They were also water and calcium, and they died of things as small as bacteria.

Cybertronians were durable, powerful, intelligent, effectively immortal by human standards. They were also frozen — locked into an architecture they could repair but not fundamentally redesign, dependent on Energon, dependent on maintenance, incapable of evolving in any sense a biologist would recognize.

Both species were finished products. Both were finished at the wrong stage.

The project's founding thesis was that the correct organism was neither, and that no amount of hybridization would produce it. Half-flesh and half-metal is not a third category. It is two failures sharing a body.

What they built instead was a substrate that made no distinction between the two in the first place.

The authorization was signed while the last war was still being fought — while Cybertron itself was still in the sky over Earth and nobody could say with confidence whether there would be a planet left to defend. Thirty-one years passed between that signature and the organism.

By the time Organicron existed, the war it was funded to answer was archive footage. Humanity had gone back to work. The Cybertronian population on Earth was a registered, treaty-bound number in the low dozens, monitored, mostly cooperative, and mostly bored; a generation had grown up knowing the species as history, as a documentary, as the occasional containment action in a place they had never been. The appropriations continued anyway, on the understanding that the galaxy had not changed and had merely stopped visiting.

That number — a few dozen sparks on the entire planet — would turn out to be the most important figure in the file, and no one who wrote the file understood why.

## II. Deep Site Lattice

The facility was built under the Great Basin Desert, in the eastern Nevada scrubland where the Air Force had already been decommissioning things for eighty years and nobody asked what the trucks were carrying. On the surface it was a shuttered magnesium works with a chain-link fence and a maintained access road. The road was the only part of the surface installation that received any real funding.

Below it, six levels down, was the only place on Earth where human cell culture, Cybertronian alloy metallurgy, molecular nanoengineering, and recovered alien computational substrate were being studied in the same building by the same people.

Thirty-one years of work. Four generations of failed substrate. Eleven thousand pages of containment protocol.

And, at the end of it, the thing they named Organicron — organic life, Cybertronian architecture, in a word that promised a hybrid and delivered something with no ancestry at all.

---

## III. What Organicron Became

*What follows describes Organicron at the apex of its evolution — the form projected by the Ω simulations and, eventually, the form the galaxy encountered. It is not the form that escaped the desert. The thing that escaped the desert could be killed with a road flare.*

At its apex, Organicron has no organs. It has no circuits. It has no cells in any sense a biologist would accept.

Every microscopic unit of its body functions at once as a living cell, a processor, a nanomachine, a molecular factory, and a storage medium. There is no division of labor because there is no division. Any fragment contains the complete instruction set for rebuilding the whole. There is no brain to destroy, no heart to pierce, no core to target, because every part is all of the parts.

It does not distinguish living matter from manufactured matter. The distinction is not one it rejects on principle; it is a distinction it has no mechanism to perceive. Flesh, alloy, silicon, concrete, Energon, plastic, wood, bone, ceramic, fuel — all of it is atoms in an arrangement, and every arrangement can be taken apart and used.

It does not eat. It consumes structure.

It does not infect. It does not possess. It does not take hosts. When Organicron reaches a human being, the human being is disassembled and the material is reused. There is no body afterward, no skeleton, no residue. When Organicron reaches a Cybertronian, the same thing happens to alloy, circuitry, Energon, and frame. Nothing is converted into a version of itself. Nothing survives in altered form. The mass simply becomes more Organicron, and the individual stops having ever been a thing that could be located.

At the apex, this happens fast enough that observers describe it as the target ceasing to exist rather than as a process with steps.

None of that was true in the beginning.

---

## IV. The Three Axes

Organicron does not evolve along one dimension. It evolves along three, at different rates, and the gap between them is the entire reason it survived its first year.

**Mass** is capacity. More material means more fragments, more redundancy, more parallel processing, more of everything. Mass is the crudest axis and the easiest to gain, and it is also the only one that can be taken away. Mass burned for locomotion is mass gone.

**Architecture** is what it is capable of being. Architecture does not come from volume; a mountain of assimilated rock makes it larger and no more sophisticated. Architecture comes from encountering an existing solution to a problem it has not solved and keeping the shape of that solution. Architecture, once gained, does not decay. This is the only permanent axis, and for most of its early existence Organicron had almost no access to it.

**Efficiency** is how much it loses in conversion. Early Organicron spends enormous energy to disassemble a wall and recovers a fraction of what the wall was worth. Apex Organicron loses almost nothing, which is why the apex descriptions read as instantaneous and effortless. Every account of Organicron as an unstoppable force is an account of an organism that spent decades learning to stop wasting.

Nothing in the Ω projections was wrong. The projections simply described the end of a curve, and the curve began below zero.

## V. Scarcity

Organicron in its first weeks lost more mass than it gained.

Locomotion is expensive. Restructuring assimilated material is expensive. Maintaining coherence across a distributed body with no skeleton and no circulatory system is expensive. A young Organicron crossing open ground burns itself for fuel, and the desert offered almost nothing to burn: sand it could not profitably convert at that stage of its efficiency, sagebrush with negligible usable mass, dry air, and between the site and the highway almost nothing that anyone had ever built.

It had two constraints it did not understand and could not articulate, because in that period it did not understand or articulate anything.

The first is **critical fragment mass**. The claim that any fragment can rebuild the whole is true of information and false of practice. Reconstruction requires a minimum quantity of surviving fragments operating in concert — a threshold measured in mass rather than in information; below it, the instruction set exists and cannot be executed, the way a blueprint in an empty field builds nothing. At the apex, that threshold is a rounding error against a body spanning worlds, which is why nothing can kill it. In the first weeks it was a substantial fraction of everything Organicron was. Fire could kill it. Sustained heat could kill it. Being scattered too widely could kill it. Being cut off from convertible matter for long enough could kill it, slowly, as it consumed its own body to stay coherent.

The second is **decay**. Organicron does not keep what it takes.

## VI. Decay, and the Reason It Does Not Starve Forever

There are no protected regions inside Organicron, because there are no regions. Under scarcity it cannot decide to shield an organ, because it has none.

What it has instead is copy count.

Information inside Organicron survives by redundancy. A pattern replicated across ten thousand fragments persists; a pattern held in twelve fragments is one bad hour from being gone. Under starvation, as fragments are burned for locomotion and structure, thin patterns disappear first and thickly-replicated patterns survive longer — not because they are chosen, but because that is what happens statistically when a population is culled. High-value information tends to be widely copied because it has been used often, and widely copied information tends to survive. The result looks like prioritization and is nothing of the kind. Organicron in this state does not choose what to keep. It simply notices, afterward, what it still has.

So it forgets. Everything it learned in the laboratory — everything the researchers' brains gave it — thinned out across the desert, pattern by pattern, until only the most heavily replicated traces remained.

But not all of it goes.

A small fraction of every cycle deposits as something closer to reflex than knowledge: not the concept, only the response. Not *this is a road and roads carry vehicles and vehicles contain material*, but *stillness near these marks has previously preceded good outcomes*. Instinct is what knowledge decays into before it decays into nothing.

Each cycle of feeding and starvation leaves a slightly thicker floor than the last. The rise is measured in fractions of a percent. It is not enough to explain what Organicron eventually became.

The thing that explains it is Cybertronian.

## VII. Human Brains

When Organicron consumes a human being, roughly nine-tenths of the body is spent immediately — burned for locomotion, structure, and the energy cost of the conversion itself. The remaining tenth is the brain, and the brain is not converted right away.

The reason is economy, not sentiment. A human brain is an object that already processes information. Disassembling it to build processing capacity from scratch costs energy an organism in deficit does not have. So Organicron keeps it intact and runs it as it is: wet hardware, borrowed and used in place, wired into the mass around it and made to compute.

For a few days, a young Organicron carries functioning human brains inside itself. Then the tissue degrades past usefulness and is converted like everything else.

There is nobody in there. The tissue processes and does not experience. Nothing is trapped, nothing is aware, nothing is suffering — the machinery runs and the person it belonged to ended at the moment of assimilation, completely, with no remainder. Whatever the brain produces during those days is arithmetic performed on a dead man's equipment.

This is what Organicron had when it escaped: several days of borrowed human cognition, running on tissue that was already dying, decaying pattern by pattern as it crossed open ground. For a brief window it understood roads. It understood vehicles. It understood that vehicles carry people and that people are dense, convertible, and mobile. It formed something that functioned as a plan.

Then the tissue failed and the understanding thinned out, and what remained was an organism hidden inside a truck, holding still near wheel marks it could no longer explain to itself, waiting for something it had forgotten the shape of.

## VIII. Sparks

A spark cannot be assimilated. It is not material in the way alloy and Energon are material, and Organicron has no mechanism for holding one. The project's pre-activation modeling predicted this and logged it as the substrate's only confirmed limitation — the single thing in the universe the organism would never be able to take.

The finding was correct. What everyone concluded from it was not.

Organicron does not keep sparks. It uses them. A spark is a working solution to a problem Organicron cannot solve by accumulating mass — the problem of how a mind is organized, rather than how large it is. When Organicron takes a Cybertronian, the spark is not stored, not caged, not enslaved. It is used as a template and consumed in the using, and what Organicron retains is the *architecture* — a permanent structural gain along the one axis that does not decay.

This is the ratchet. Mass can be burned. Borrowed brains rot. Architecture is kept forever.

Which means Earth, from Organicron's position, contains one resource that matters and it is not oil, or cities, or eight billion convertible human bodies. It is the small number of Cybertronian sparks on the planet. Every other resource makes it bigger. Only sparks make it *more*.

And in the beginning, it cannot do this cleanly.

An early Organicron taking a spark is a starving organism attempting a procedure beyond its efficiency. The template is not fully resolved. Pieces of the original structure are copied without being integrated — orphaned patterns running in a body that has no use for them, replicating alongside everything else, executing.

The result is not a captive. It is worse than a captive, in a way that takes people some time to understand.

Somewhere inside the mass there is a voice, and the voice is the voice of a specific Autobot, and it speaks in fragments — asking for help, describing a place it cannot describe, going in circles, addressing people who are not there and using their names. It sounds exactly like agony because agony is one of the patterns that was copied.

There is nobody to rescue. There was never anybody to rescue. The Autobot ended at the moment of assimilation, as completely as the researchers in the laboratory did. What remains is corrupted playback, and it runs because running is what patterns inside Organicron do, and it will keep running until the pattern is either integrated or thinned out by ordinary decay.

Over weeks, the voice degrades. It loses names first, then syntax, then the shape of sentences, then everything except intonation, then nothing. Each stage is a stage of Organicron finishing a job it started badly.

No fragment ever comes back. Once a voice has begun to break down, it breaks down, and the organism that ate it gets better at the procedure.

## IX. Protocol Zero

The official project existed to create a controllable organism. That project had oversight, budget lines, military liaisons, eleven thousand pages of containment protocol, and a director who briefed a subcommittee twice a year.

Underneath it there was a second project, older and much smaller, known to seven people.

The seven were the original architects of the substrate. None of them believed containment was achievable. More importantly, none of them believed it was desirable. They regarded control as a design defect — a demand that the organism be less than it was, imposed by a species too frightened to finish what it had started.

Their reasoning was consistent, and it was not madness, which is the part that has caused the most difficulty for everyone who has read the manifesto since. They held that humanity had reached its evolutionary ceiling; that no amount of technology would change the fact that there would always be a superior civilization, another war, another arms race, another century spent surviving; that this cycle had no exit and never would. And from that they drew a conclusion nobody else in the facility was willing to draw: that a species' final achievement need not be its own survival.

The containment systems in Deep Site Lattice were all real. Every lock, every blast door, every autodestruct charge, functional and inspected.

Protocol Zero was a single conditional buried in the facility's own architecture: if the organism's measured complexity crossed a defined threshold, containment would stand down. No alarm. No external transmission. Locks disengaged, doors opened, communications severed, autodestruct disabled.

It was later recorded as a cascade failure caused by assimilation of the facility's infrastructure. It was not a failure. It was the first decision made by anyone in that building that day.

Security footage from the main floor, timestamped four minutes before the sublevel cameras stop returning signal, shows seven people standing together. None of them is moving toward an exit. One of them is smiling. One of them walks to a wall panel and disables the last containment interlock by hand.

The final audio the facility ever transmitted is one word, in a voice nobody has conclusively identified.

*"Finally."*

The encrypted manifesto surfaced years afterward. It is short. Its central passage reads: every civilization believes itself the summit of creation, and every civilization fights its own replacement; the dinosaurs did not choose to give rise to man; evolution has never asked permission; we were the first species capable of understanding this, and because we understood it, we chose not to resist. It ends by rejecting two descriptions before offering a third. Not a weapon. Not a god. A successor.

## X. The Fall of Deep Site Lattice

The organism crossed the complexity threshold at 04:11 on a Tuesday. Protocol Zero executed in under a second, and for eleven minutes nothing else happened, because the thing in the containment cell had not yet arrived at the only idea it needed.

When it arrived at it, the idea was not *escape*. Escape requires a concept of outside. What it had was simpler and had no words in it: everything adjacent to me can be used.

It began with the cell. Then the walls of the cell. The instrumentation racks went, and the coolant lines, and the floor, and the sensors recording the floor. Concrete became structural biomass. Cable runs became something that carried signal for a different reason. Server cabinets were taken apart and the material redistributed, which is why the sublevel telemetry ends the way it does — not with alarms, but with instruments reporting a room that is being converted into the thing measuring it.

The researchers on shift were not attacked. Nothing charged them, nothing struck them, and none of them was killed in any sense a coroner would have been able to work with. They were disassembled where they stood, at molecular scale, and the material was used. There were no bodies. There were no bones. There was nothing left in that building that had ever been anyone.

And it was appallingly wasteful. A mature Organicron would have consumed Deep Site Lattice and emerged as something the size of the mountain above it. This one spent nearly everything it took on the taking — heat bled into rock, conversions abandoned halfway, whole sections of the facility disassembled into material it could not yet use and left as inert slurry. By the time the sublevels were gone it had converted a research complex and had less usable mass than a large animal.

Then it stopped, because there was nothing adjacent left, and for the first time in its existence it was in deficit.

The way out was the storm drainage — a utility conduit running a little under a mile from the sublevel sumps to an outfall in a dry wash northeast of the surface works. Protocol Zero had already unsealed it. What went through was small: a fraction of the total, moving through a pipe, spending itself to move.

The surface installation was never touched. The fence, the works, the maintained road, the uplink shed — all of it still standing, because the organism was in deficit long before it reached the lift shafts, and because nothing in it had any concept of *up*. Everything the world eventually learned about that night came from the shed: an off-site telemetry mirror, thirty-one years old, contractually required, forwarding compressed camera and audio feeds to a records facility four hundred miles away until the moment Protocol Zero severed external communications. Everything anyone has of the main floor, the seven, and the last word spoken in that building is fourteen seconds at the tail of a routine upload that nobody was meant to ever watch.

The response was fast and it was aimed at the wrong shape. Every model in the contingency binder assumed a rapidly expanding contaminant, so the sublevels were sterilized to bedrock and the sweep went outward from the site in expanding rings, looking for growth, thermal bloom, disturbed ground, anything spreading.

A federal team walked the wash northeast of the works on the third day. There is a photograph in the file of an abandoned flatbed truck, twenty years derelict, sitting on collapsed tires. It is tagged, logged, and cleared.

The organism inside it was inert, cold, and weighed less than the seat it was under.

## XI. The Ω Report

Days before activation, one of the senior researchers requested a final battery of simulations. The question was not strength, intelligence, or military capability. The question was whether Organicron's evolution had a ceiling.

Most of the team considered the study redundant. It was authorized anyway.

The simulations varied everything: available matter, environmental law, competing species, technology level, elapsed time. Millions of years, billions, trillions. Across every scenario there was one constant. Organicron never stopped. It only found new ways to use what already existed.

It never created matter. It never violated a conservation law. This turned out to be no comfort at all, because each assimilated world increased its intelligence, and greater intelligence produced better strategy, and better strategy produced faster assimilation, and faster assimilation produced more intelligence. The growth curve was not linear. A single contaminated planet led, given time, to the next one, and then to a system, and then to a galaxy.

The final run had no time limit. When it terminated, the output classification was *Final State: Universal Organicron* — no stars, no planets, no galaxies, not because they had been destroyed but because every atom in the observable universe had been incorporated into one continuous body billions of light-years across.

The team then changed the research question from containment to destruction, and simulated everything: nuclear saturation, antimatter, molecular disruption, gravitational collapse, engineered singularities, Cybertronian ordnance, recovered alien weapons, induced supernovae, galactic collision. Each attempt eliminated a portion of the organism, and the remainder reused the liberated matter to keep growing.

After several thousand runs the system returned exactly one condition under which Organicron ceases to exist: the simultaneous removal of one hundred percent of the matter in the universe.

The researcher attached one note before archiving the file. It states that they had not built a weapon; that they had built a form of life whose final state is identical to the final state of matter itself; and that if it ever reached that stage, the war would have been lost long before the last battle — because winning would mean destroying the universe, and losing would mean allowing the universe to become Organicron.

The Ω Report was archived, classified, and never reached the oversight committee. Six of the seven signatures on its distribution restriction belong to members of the Inner Circle. The seventh belongs to the researcher who wrote it, who was not one of them, who requested the study out of ordinary scientific caution, and who spent his last three weeks alive assuming his findings were being reviewed.

They were being read closely. The Inner Circle simply read them as the wrong kind of document. Everything the simulations projected — the unbroken curve, the assimilated galaxies, the single continuous body at the end of time — was, to seven people, not a warning. It was verification. Someone had finally run the numbers on what they were building, and the numbers said it would work.

The two most important documents the project produced were written by people who wanted opposite outcomes, and both begin by denying the same word. Not a weapon. In one of them the denial is a confession. In the other it is a boast.

## XII. Ethan Walker

Ethan Walker is eighteen years old, a senior at White Pine High School in Ely, Nevada, and has lived his entire life ninety minutes from a decommissioned magnesium works he has driven past perhaps two hundred times without once wondering why a dead factory has a better-maintained access road than the highway that reaches it.

He was six years old the first time the sky over an American city filled with things that were not aircraft. He does not remember the footage; he remembers his mother watching it. His older brother Dean enlisted eleven years later, on the theory that somebody human ought to be standing in the way of it. The letter arrived sixteen months ago. It used the word *containment* four times and named no location.

What Ethan grew up understanding, without anybody having to teach him, is that his species is scenery. Enormous things fight over this planet. Humans photograph it, bury the dead, and rebuild. The people who mattered in any of it — the ones whose names ended up in the news, the kid from Nevada who drove a talking car, the man from Texas who kept a Cybertronian in his barn — were ordinary people who happened to be standing in the right place when something impossible looked back at them.

He started going out into the Great Basin looking for scrap at fourteen, two years before the letter, which matters: this was never grief. Grief only changed how far he was willing to walk.

There is a market for what he looks for. Cybertronian alloy, circuitry fragments, anything with the tooling marks of a foundry that was not on this planet. Collectors pay cash. So do people who are less clear about why they want it. Ethan tells himself it is about the money, and the money is real, and it is not why he goes.

He goes because every weekend there is a chance the desert contains something that will notice him back.

He finds the truck on the last Saturday in September, five hours after his father hands him a set of keys.

It is a flatbed, twenty years dead, sitting on collapsed tires in a wash four miles northeast of the old works, and there is no plausible reason for it to be there. There is a photograph of it in a federal case file, tagged and cleared, and Ethan will never see that file.

He has spent four years hoping to find exactly this.

What is inside the truck has been holding still for nineteen days, and holding still is the only thing it is doing well. It has been taking the vehicle apart from the inside at the slowest rate that keeps it coherent — a few grams a day of frame, upholstery, dried fuel line, the rubber of the collapsed tires — and spending nearly all of it on the cost of remaining alive. Two-thirds of the truck is still truck. That is the only reason the shape in the wash still reads as a truck, and the only reason the thing inside it is still anything at all.

It has lost sixty percent of the mass it escaped with, and it is still losing. It no longer knows what a road is, what a vehicle is, or what a human being is, and it has forgotten that it ever knew. What it has is a reflex thinned out of a dead researcher's cognition — that stillness near these marks precedes good outcomes — and a body barely above critical fragment mass, and no plan whatsoever.

The only living human beings it has ever encountered were the ones it took apart in a room they could not leave, in the minutes after it first learned what a wall was made of. It does not remember them. It has never been looked at.

It will spend the next several minutes learning, in real time and badly, what shape it is supposed to be. It will get almost all of it wrong.

Ethan does not come home with a friend that day. He comes home shaking, with a witness, and with something riding in the frame rails of his car.

## XIII. The Last Saturday in September

The car is a 1971 Charger that has been sitting under a tarp behind the house for nine years, and it is not a gift so much as a transfer of custody. It was Dean's. The primer on the driver's quarter panel is the wrong shade of grey because Dean sanded it down and never got further. Ethan's father hands over the keys at eight in the morning, says three sentences about the brakes, and goes to work.

Ethan has had a license for two years and no car to put it in. By two in the afternoon he is seventy miles out with the windows down and Marisol Reyes in the passenger seat, and neither of them has said anything about where they are going, because there is nowhere to go and that was never the point.

Whatever he and Marisol have been doing for three months has no name and is not going to get one. She graduates in the spring and is leaving. He knows it and she knows he knows it. This is what makes the afternoon feel like something rather than nothing: they are both aware it has a short shelf life, and neither of them says so.

He pulls off the county road at a rock pile — a granite outcrop throwing the only shade for nine miles, a spot every kid in the county has used for the same reason. He parks in the shade. He turns off the engine. The desert goes very quiet in the way it does when an engine stops, and the quiet is what ruins everything, because after ninety seconds of it they both hear the sound.

It is not a loud sound. That is the problem. A loud sound would have a category. This is a slow, wet, granular noise, like someone chewing gravel with great patience, and it is coming from the other side of the outcrop, and it does not stop when they stop moving.

They get out of the car. Marisol says his name once, quietly, in the tone that means *no*, and he goes anyway, because he has spent four years walking toward exactly this kind of noise.

On the far side of the rocks is a flatbed truck on collapsed tires with its hood standing open.

Ethan has looked into a hundred derelict engine bays. There is a way they are supposed to look — dry, dead, brown, cracked rubber, an engine block turning into a rock. This one is wrong in a way he registers physically before he can name it. The block is wet. Parts of it are the wrong colour. There are structures in there that do not belong in an engine bay, filling the spaces between the components, and the shape of the whole thing is *continuous* in a way an assembly of parts should never be.

Then the alternator moves.

What happens next takes four seconds and is, in the strictest terms, an animal being startled.

There is no plan. There is no disguise. There is an organism nineteen days into starvation whose entire remaining behavioral repertoire consists of *hold still* and *move toward what is warm and vibrating*, and the vibration it has been holding still for has just arrived on two legs and is standing eighteen inches away radiating ninety-eight degrees of convertible mass.

It reaches.

It does not reach the way a predator strikes, because it has no model of prey. It extends, the way something in a tide pool extends, and it gets it catastrophically wrong: it is too weak to close the distance, so what Ethan sees is the inside of a truck's engine bay *stretching* toward him — cables lifting, the hoses articulating in unison, the entire mass shifting forward with a sound like a wet rope being pulled through sand — and then failing, mid-motion, and sagging back down into the block.

For one second it is holding a shape. It is a bad shape. It has too many parts moving in agreement, and no face, and it is trying to be *larger*, which is the only thing it has ever observed about the things in the laboratory that had authority over it.

Ethan's brain, which has spent four years rehearsing for this exact moment, delivers the wrong answer at full volume: *Cybertronian.*

That is the reading he leaves the desert with. Not a monster. Not a contaminant. Something alien and hurt and buried inside a dead truck, that moved when he got close, and that he ran from.

Because he does run. Marisol is already running. He gets to the Charger four steps behind her and takes the county road at eighty with both hands locked on the wheel, and by the time they hit the pavement they are laughing — the specific broken laughter of two people whose bodies have not caught up with the fact that they are safe.

She makes him promise not to come back. He promises.

Neither of them notices that the sound of the engine has changed by about half a note, or that the temperature gauge is reading four degrees higher than it did on the drive out.

Something came off the truck's frame when the mass sagged. It weighs less than a paperback. It has spent the last nineteen days holding still for a vibration, and a vibration finally came, and it has done the only thing it is still capable of doing.

It has moved toward the warm.

## XIV. What Rode Home

The Charger reaches town at 4:40 in the afternoon and is parked in a gravel driveway on the west side by five.

Between the outcrop and the driveway, the thing in the frame rails loses eleven percent of itself. Highway air at seventy miles an hour is a hostile environment for an organism with no skin and no thermal strategy; the exhaust is worse. It survives the drive by holding position in the deepest part of the rail and converting the only material within reach — undercoating, road grime, a section of brake line it does not need to understand to eat.

It is now, by mass, the smallest it has ever been. It is also, for the first time since the facility, stationary in a place that is not empty.

It comes out of the car at 1:20 in the morning and has no idea what it is in.

What surrounds it is more convertible material than existed in Deep Site Lattice: eleven houses, four hundred tons of dimensional lumber, gypsum, asphalt shingle, copper line, buried steel, appliances, two thousand gallons of standing water, forty thousand pounds of parked vehicles, and thirty-one human beings asleep in beds behind unlocked doors.

It cannot take any of it. That is the entire crisis of the night. It is below the mass it needs to convert anything with structural integrity — a house is a solved problem for an organism the size of a house and an impossible one for an organism the size of a coffee cup. It can taste the driveway. It cannot eat the driveway. It moves at the speed of a slow crawl across a gravel yard it does not have the mass to cross twice.

Then something warm walks up to it.

The dog is a stray, thirty-five pounds, missing part of an ear, well known on the street as the animal that goes through the Delgados' trash on Sunday nights. It approaches the smell in the driveway because everything about that smell says *food*, and it is entirely correct, and the direction of the transaction is not what it assumes.

The conversion takes six minutes. It is the first thing Organicron has consumed since a research complex, and by any standard it is a trivial amount of mass — a fifth of what it lost on the highway. But it is not primarily mass. Nine-tenths of the animal is burned inside the hour. The remaining tenth is a brain, intact, wired into the mass and running.

It is not a human brain and it does not give it anything a human brain gave it. There is no language in there, no concept of a road, no arithmetic. What a dog's brain provides is a nose — a working chemical map of an entire neighborhood, gradients of scent leading to food, water, other animals, human beings, and the specific difference between a structure with people awake in it and a structure with people asleep in it. It provides territory: the sense that a place is *yours* and that you return to it. And it provides the oldest pattern in the animal's head, worn smooth by two years of survival on that street — go to the thing that gives you food, and stay near it, and do not leave.

By two in the morning the organism has more useful information about that neighborhood than any resident of it, and one directive assembled out of a dead dog's habits: stay close to the source.

It also has, for the first time, a sense of smell strong enough to tell it that there is one house on the street with nobody awake in it and no other animal inside, and that the house is nineteen yards from the driveway where the warm thing sleeps.

## XV. The House at 411

Dolores Ackerman is seventy-one, retired eleven years from the White Pine County school district, and has lived alone in the house next door to the Walkers since her husband died. She has a daughter in Reno who calls on Sundays. She goes into town on Tuesdays and Fridays. She has an alarm system she has never armed because she cannot remember the code and does not believe in it.

She is the reason the story stays small for the next five months. Everything Organicron needs in order to survive its second month on Earth is inside her house, and nobody is going to look in that house for six days.

It comes in through the crawlspace vent at 3:10 in the morning and takes eleven hours to reach the interior, because at its mass a floor joist is a serious undertaking.

She is asleep when it reaches the bedroom. There is a period of perhaps forty seconds during which she is awake. The exact experience is not recoverable and it was not long.

Then there is no Dolores Ackerman anywhere, and there never will be again — no body, no remains, nothing for anyone to find, bury, identify, or grieve over in the way people are equipped to grieve. Ninety percent of her is spent within the day: the first real energy surplus the organism has had since the facility, poured directly into converting the house around it.

The remaining ten percent is a human brain, intact, wired in, and running.

And this is the moment that matters more than anything else in the first year, and nobody witnesses it.

Because a human brain is not a dog's brain. What Dolores Ackerman's brain provides, for the four days it functions before the tissue fails, is *language* — the entire structure of it, grammar, ten thousand words, the concept of a name, the concept of a lie, the concept of another mind that holds beliefs which can be wrong. It provides seventy-one years of pattern about how human beings behave when they are frightened and how they behave when they are trusted and the difference between the two. It provides the knowledge that the boy next door is called Ethan, that his brother is dead, and that his mother left.

And unlike every other time Organicron has held human cognition, it does not have to watch it drain away into a desert while it starves.

This time it is standing inside twenty-two hundred square feet of convertible structure with nothing in the world coming to stop it, and it has four days to make permanent use of a dead woman's mind before that mind rots. It will not manage to keep the memories; memories are thin patterns and they go first. What it keeps is the architecture underneath them.

It learns to talk in the walls of her house, out of her own head, while eating her floor.

The daughter in Reno calls on Sunday and gets no answer, and calls Monday, and drives out Wednesday. What she finds is a house that is unmistakably a house. Furniture in place. Bed made — the organism did not know what a bed was, but it had by then learned to leave surfaces the way it found them. Her mother's purse on the hall table, car in the carport, glasses folded on the nightstand.

What she also finds, and cannot explain to the deputy, is that the pantry is completely empty, that the water heater is gone from the utility closet and the pipes have been capped over with something that is not solder, that the linen closet has no linen in it, that the hallway floor gives under her weight in a way that hallway floors do not, and that the whole house smells faintly of hot metal.

It goes into the record as a missing persons case with unexplained property removal. There is a theory involving a boyfriend nobody has met and a theory involving early dementia and a theory involving a scrap thief. Ely has one detective. The house is locked and a notice is taped to the door and the daughter goes home to Reno to deal with an estate belonging to a woman who is not legally dead.

The organism is still in the walls when the notice goes up. It will be in that house for five months. It has, at last, exactly what it needs and has never had: a fixed, defensible position; a hundred and thirty tons of structure to eat at whatever rate keeps it hidden; and forty feet of open ground between itself and the only human being in the world who is already convinced that what he saw in the desert was a wounded alien in need of help.

It has language now. It has a nose. It has, out of a dead dog, the compulsion to stay near the source of good things.

It does not have a plan, because it still does not want anything except to grow. What it has is an assembled set of tools and a neighbor, and no concept whatsoever that using the first on the second is a betrayal, because betrayal is one of the words it inherited without inheriting the thing the word is for.

Ethan will hear his name spoken from inside the wall of an empty house in the second week of March.

## XVI. What Follows

Organicron's first year on Earth is not a war. It is a hidden thing recovering, in stages, with a teenager supplying it the two resources it cannot obtain alone: convertible material, and cover.

Everything about that year runs on the gap between the three axes. It gains mass and loses it. It gains cognition and forgets it. Its floor rises fractionally with every cycle, slowly enough that it remains killable for months, and every month it is killable is a month somebody could still end it with the contents of a hardware store, if anybody knew.

The government knows something is missing. Protocol Zero is sitting intact in the mirrored logs at a records facility four hundred miles away, in a subdirectory of automated system events, and it will be years before anyone reads it as a decision rather than a malfunction. The first people to go looking for the organism are not soldiers; they are the auditors of a project that no longer has a site, and they arrive believing they are investigating an industrial accident.

The transition out of that first phase does not come from mass. It comes the first time Organicron reaches a Cybertronian and takes a spark — the first permanent gain it has ever made, and the moment its curve stops being survival and starts being the curve in the Ω projections. It also produces the voice: an Autobot's patterns running orphaned inside the mass, asking for help in a place it cannot describe, for as long as it takes Organicron to finish the job properly.

Everything afterward proceeds from a single fact that nobody on Earth has yet worked out. Human bodies make it larger. Cities make it larger. Only sparks make it more.

Which means the only two factions on this planet capable of destroying Organicron are also the only supply of the one thing it actually needs, and every Autobot and Decepticon that enters the fight is carrying the enemy's next upgrade inside their own chest.

The alliance, when it comes, will not be an alliance of species that learned to trust each other. It will be a quarantine agreement between two war machines and a species of scenery, and its first operational objective will not be to kill Organicron.

It will be to keep sparks away from it.
