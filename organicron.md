# ORGANICRON

---

# PART ONE — WHAT HUMANITY CONCLUDED

---

## I. The Conclusion

The wars ended the way all of Earth's wars with Cybertron ended: with cities rebuilt over craters, with a treaty drafted by species that had never intended to share a planet, and with the quiet understanding that nothing had actually been settled.

The last of it was not even fought on the ground. It was fought in the sky, over a broken world that had come across the galaxy to eat this one, and when it was over Cybertron did not go away. It went into orbit and stayed there — a hemisphere of dead architecture hanging above the equator, close enough to throw a shadow across the Pacific twice a day, close enough that for the first year afterward every government on Earth kept a rotating watch on nothing but its inclination.

And the horns stayed. Six of them, surfaced during the last week of the fighting, standing up out of the crust in six places nobody had chosen, on six continents, and none of them were Cybertronian. They were older. What they were part of, and what it meant that Earth had a part, was the single piece of information from that war that the general public was never given in full, because there is no version of that briefing that ends with people going back to work.

The Autobots went up to the orbital ruins and began the long labor of making a corpse habitable. Their leader transmitted a message in every direction, on every band Cybertronian ships had ever used, and the content of it was three sentences long and did not concern Earth at all. It said: the war is over, the homeworld exists, come home.

Nobody on Earth thought about that transmission again for nineteen years.

The people who wrote the appropriation did not phrase their conclusion in these terms. What went into the document was that terrestrial defense required a qualitative rather than a quantitative advance. What they meant was simpler and harder to say out loud, and it had been sitting in classified after-action reviews since the Chicago invasion:

Humanity would never win by building better versions of what the Cybertronians already were.

They had proof. Kinetic Solutions had proved it, expensively, in front of an audience. KSI had spent years reverse-engineering Cybertronian corpses into programmable matter and had gotten it to work — genuinely work, fifty units, remote-piloted, and a flagship prototype built from the recovered structure of a Decepticon warlord's head. The prototype had been switched on and had immediately stopped being theirs. It had woken the other fifty and taken them, and the record of what followed is available in any library.

The lesson the surviving engineers drew from KSI was not *don't build robots*. It was more specific and much worse. Programmable matter is not alive. It is a medium. It does whatever is written into it, and it will accept writing from anyone — a technician with a console, or a mind that has been waiting inside the salvage for six years for someone to finally give it a body. Transformium was never a species. It was an empty page, and humanity had spent a fortune learning to make paper without ever solving the problem of who holds the pen.

So the objective was inverted. Not a weapon. Not a soldier. Not a chassis waiting for an operator.

A successor technology to biology itself — something that did not inherit the specific failures of either species that had fought over the planet, and, above all, something that did not need to be told what to do, because anything that needs to be told what to do can be told by an enemy.

The founding assessment ran four pages. It is not classified anymore and it is not difficult to summarize.

Humans were creative, adaptive, capable of improvisation no machine had matched. They were also water and calcium, and they died of things as small as bacteria.

Cybertronians were durable, powerful, effectively immortal by human standards, and carried in the center of the chest a thing that no human instrument had ever measured, mapped, opened, or copied. They were also — in the judgment of the assessment, which was written by people who had studied them exclusively as wreckage — frozen. Locked into an architecture they could repair and rebuild but not fundamentally redesign. Dependent on Energon. Dependent on maintenance. Incapable of evolving in any sense a biologist would recognize.

That judgment was wrong, and being wrong about it cost more than any other error in the file. But it was the judgment, and everything downstream was built on it.

Both species were finished products. Both were finished at the wrong stage.

The project's founding thesis was that the correct organism was neither, and that no amount of hybridization would produce it. Half-flesh and half-metal is not a third category. It is two failures sharing a body, and KSI had already built that and watched it walk away.

What they built instead was a substrate that made no distinction between the two in the first place.

The authorization was signed in the final weeks of the war, while the orbital ruins were still settling and nobody could say with confidence whether there would be a planet left to defend. Thirty-one years passed between that signature and the organism.

By the time Organicron existed, the war it was funded to answer was archive footage. The Cybertronian population on Earth was a registered, treaty-bound number in the low dozens, monitored, mostly cooperative, and mostly bored. Nobody had hunted one in three decades; the organizations that used to do it had been dissolved by international agreement and their personnel files sealed. The appropriations continued anyway, on the understanding that the galaxy had not changed and had merely stopped visiting.

That number — a few dozen sparks on the entire planet, and a handful more inbound across thirty light-years, answering a call that had been traveling since before anyone in the facility was hired — would turn out to be the most important figure in the file.

Nobody who wrote the file understood why. One person worked it out, eleven days before activation, and put it in a report that six of the seven signatures on its distribution list belonged to men and women who read it as good news.

---

## II. Horn Four

Six horns came up. The one in the Nevada desert was the fourth to be logged and the second to be reached, and it received a scientific station within four months of the ceasefire because it was in the continental United States, on federal land, ninety miles from a decommissioned airbase with a runway that still took heavy aircraft.

The station was not secret. It had a name, a director, a rotating international staff, and a public-facing quarterly bulletin that ran to about forty pages of spectrographic tables and structural imaging and said, in effect, *we still do not know what this is made of*.

They did not know what it was made of. That is the honest version, and it stayed honest for eleven months.

The horn did not respond to sampling. It did not respond to drilling, resonance, radiation, current, or heat. It was not alloy in any sense that matched a Cybertronian sample, and it was not any element the periodic table admits. The most useful thing the station produced in its first year was a negative result so consistent that three separate teams independently proposed abandoning the site: whatever the horns were, they were not going to be understood by looking at them.

In the eleventh month a woman walked into the site.

She came in on foot, at dusk, past a perimeter that had motion sensors, past a gate with two contractors on it, and neither the sensors nor the contractors have any record of her arrival. She was dressed in blue. Her face was covered from the bridge of the nose down. She was, by every description given afterward, a woman of about forty, and she spoke English without any accent that four different listeners could agree on.

She stopped one of the researchers, who had her hand flat on the surface of the horn, and told her that he did not like to be touched.

Then she told them how to kill him.

There is no recording. The station's cameras have a gap of eleven minutes that was attributed at the time to a generator fault, and the fault was real, and the generator log confirms it, and the generator had never faulted before or since. What exists is the testimony of the seven people who were on the floor that evening, and their testimony was collected separately, and it agrees.

She did not give them a weapon. She gave them a description of a principle: that matter has no categories except the ones an observer imposes, that the distinction between the living and the manufactured is a convenience of the small, and that anything can be taken apart into anything if you stop believing in the difference. She spoke for perhaps six minutes. She used the word *substrate* twice. She did not explain how she knew, and she was not asked, because the seven people on that floor had spent eleven months failing to sample an object that was not made of anything and were, at that moment, being handed the reason.

When she turned to leave, something moved under the skin of her face. Two of the seven described it as a shimmer. One described it as a framework — a structure beneath the surface, briefly visible, in the shape of a face that was not the face she was wearing.

They filed a report. The report exists. It was routed to three agencies, reviewed, and classified as an unverified security incident with a probable psychological component, on the grounds that seven exhausted researchers in a remote desert facility had all seen the same thing, which is exactly what a shared stress reaction produces.

Nobody in a position to act on it treated it as a technical document, because it did not read like one. It read like a religious experience.

Six of the seven treated it as a technical document, and went on treating it as one for thirty years.

Six of them spent the next four years turning six minutes of speech into a research program, and they did it inside the appropriation that had already been signed for the successor project, because the successor project was the only place on Earth where their conclusions would not have to be defended to anyone. The substrate they eventually produced does not contain any material from the horn. Nothing was taken from the horn; nothing could be. What the horn produced was an assumption — that the categories were optional — and the assumption is the entire technology.

The manifesto they wrote later, the one that surfaced years afterward and has been read by perhaps two hundred people, does not mention the woman.

It does not mention her because six of the seven who stood on that floor had, by then, spent three decades constructing a version of events in which they thought of it themselves. That was not a lie they told anyone else. It was the only way any of them could keep working.

The seventh mentioned her constantly, to anyone who would listen, for about two years. Her security clearance was revoked in the fifth year of the program on unrelated grounds. She died in Toronto at seventy-nine, having spent forty years telling people that the most important scientific advance of the century was handed to a research station in Nevada by a woman in a veil who wanted the planet dead, and having been, in the estimation of everyone who met her, an extremely intelligent person who had suffered a psychiatric break in middle age and never recovered from it.

Her name was Nadia Sorokin, and she was correct about every part of it.

## III. Deep Site Lattice

The facility was built under the Great Basin Desert, in the eastern Nevada scrubland where the Air Force had already been decommissioning things for eighty years and nobody asked what the trucks were carrying. It was thirty-one road miles from the Horn Four station and shared its power feed, which is how the two programs came to share personnel, and then budget lines, and then, eventually, purpose.

On the surface it was a shuttered magnesium works with a chain-link fence and a maintained access road. The road was the only part of the surface installation that received any real funding. Everything else was allowed to rust on schedule.

Below it, six levels down, was the only place on Earth where human cell culture, Cybertronian alloy metallurgy, molecular nanoengineering, and a four-year-old set of assumptions about the nonexistence of material categories were being studied in the same building by the same people.

Thirty-one years. Four generations of failed substrate. Eleven thousand pages of containment protocol, and a director, Helen Osei, who briefed a subcommittee twice a year for the last nineteen of those years and never once briefed them on anything that was actually happening.

She was not lying. That is worth being precise about, because it is the shape of the whole failure. Osei believed the containment protocols were the project. She had written a third of them herself. She had personally specified the autodestruct charge placement on Sublevel Six after the third-generation substrate did something in a sealed cell that made two technicians resign the same week. She was, by any standard anyone would apply, a serious and cautious scientist running a serious and cautious program.

She was running the visible half of a building. The invisible half had been there longer than she had, and it was seven people, and one of them had been reading her drafts and approving them for nineteen years.

The organism they eventually produced was named by a committee. The name was assembled out of two words the committee thought described a hybrid, and it does not describe a hybrid, and by the time anyone noticed that the name was already in four thousand documents.

Organicron.

Organic life. Cybertronian architecture. A word that promises an ancestry and delivers a thing with none.

## IV. The Inner Circle

The official project existed to create a controllable organism. That project had oversight, budget lines, military liaisons, eleven thousand pages of containment protocol, and Helen Osei.

Underneath it there was a second project, older and much smaller, known to six people, all of whom had been standing on the floor at Horn Four in the eleventh month.

They did not believe containment was achievable. That is the part that gets quoted. The more important part is that they did not believe it was *desirable*. They regarded control as a design defect — a demand that the organism be less than it was, imposed by a species too frightened to finish what it had started.

The manifesto is short. It surfaced eleven years after the fall of the facility, in a decryption backlog, and it has been read closely by a very small number of people whose job is to understand why an event happened. Its central passage is the one everyone remembers: that every civilization believes itself the summit of creation and every civilization fights its own replacement; that the dinosaurs did not choose to give rise to man; that evolution has never asked permission; that they were the first species capable of understanding this, and that because they understood it, they chose not to resist.

It ends by rejecting two descriptions before offering a third. Not a weapon. Not a god. A successor.

The reasoning is not madness. It is also not sound, and the specific way it fails is the most revealing document the project produced.

A successor is a thing that inherits. Mammals are a lineage: they descend, they carry structure forward, they are made of the same chemistry and the same code and they occupy the world the dinosaurs left in a form the dinosaurs would have recognized as a form. That is what the word means. That is the entire load the analogy is carrying, and the analogy is the load-bearing member of the whole document.

Organicron inherits nothing. It does not descend from humanity. It carries no human structure forward, holds no human value, retains no human memory beyond the four days a stolen brain takes to rot, and when it takes a person apart there is no version of that person left in any sense that a lineage requires. It is not the next branch. It is the solvent the branches are dissolved in.

Six intelligent people did not fail to notice this. The seventh witness from that evening — Sorokin, before her clearance went — wrote it down in almost exactly these words and circulated it internally, and the response she received is preserved, and it is two sentences long, and it does not engage with the argument.

What the manifesto is actually doing is available in its own vocabulary if you read it without the analogy. Strip out *successor*, *evolution*, and *summit of creation*, and what remains is a claim that there is no exit from the cycle of survival, that there will always be another war, another arms race, another century spent enduring, and that this is intolerable.

That is not a theory of biology. That is a description of despair, and *successor* is the word six people found that let them build the end of the world without ever writing down that they wanted it.

The last audio the facility transmitted is one word, in a voice nobody has conclusively identified.

*"Finally."*

It has been described, in every summary of the event ever written, as triumphant. It is not triumphant. Anyone who has heard the fourteen seconds knows what it actually is, and the reason no analyst will put it in writing is that the correct word makes the entire event harder to file.

It is relief.

---

The containment systems in Deep Site Lattice were all real. Every lock, every blast door, every autodestruct charge — functional, inspected, and in several cases specified by members of the Inner Circle who cared a great deal that they work.

That is the part that confuses people, and there is a clean answer to it.

Protocol Zero was a single conditional buried in the facility's own architecture: if the organism's measured coherence crossed a defined value, containment would stand down. No alarm. No external transmission. Locks disengaged, doors opened, communications severed, autodestruct disabled.

The threshold was not a control criterion. It was a viability criterion, and the distinction is the whole psychology of the seven. They had watched four generations of substrate die in sealed rooms. Substrate that could not hold itself together past a certain mass. Substrate that came apart when the cell was opened. They were not withholding freedom until the organism earned it. They were waiting, with enormous patience, for a thing they regarded as their child to be strong enough to survive the hallway.

Every lock in that building worked because the six wanted the organism kept alive until the night it could walk.

Security footage from the main floor, timestamped four minutes before the sublevel cameras stop returning signal, shows six people standing together in an open bay with the exits behind them. None of them is moving. One of them is checking a wristwatch. One of them walks to a wall panel and disables the last containment interlock by hand, and then returns to the group, and stands with them, and puts his hands in his pockets.

They are waiting for something that is coming up through the floor.

Osei is not among them. She was in Geneva. She spent the following nine years being deposed.

## V. The Ω Report

Eleven days before activation, one of the senior researchers requested a final battery of simulations. His name was Peter Halloran, he was fifty-eight, he ran predictive modeling, and he was not a member of anything.

The question he submitted was not strength, intelligence, or military capability. Those had been modeled to death. The question was whether Organicron's evolution had a ceiling.

Most of the team considered the study redundant. It was authorized anyway, because it cost nothing but processor time and because Halloran had a habit of being right about tedious things.

The simulations varied everything: available matter, environmental law, competing species, technology level, elapsed time. Millions of years, billions, trillions. Across every scenario there was one constant. Organicron never stopped. It only found new ways to use what already existed.

It never created matter. It never violated a conservation law. This turned out to be no comfort at all, because each assimilated world increased its coherence, and greater coherence permitted greater organized size, and greater organized size permitted better strategy, and better strategy produced faster assimilation. The growth curve was not linear. A single contaminated planet led, given time, to the next one, and then to a system, and then to a galaxy.

The final run had no time limit. When it terminated, the output classification was *Final State: Universal Organicron* — no stars, no planets, no galaxies, not because they had been destroyed but because every atom in the observable universe had been incorporated into one continuous body billions of light-years across.

Halloran then changed the research question from containment to destruction, and simulated everything: nuclear saturation, antimatter, molecular disruption, gravitational collapse, engineered singularities, Cybertronian ordnance, recovered alien weapons, induced supernovae, galactic collision. Each attempt eliminated a portion of the organism, and the remainder reused the liberated matter to keep growing.

After several thousand runs the system returned exactly one condition under which Organicron ceases to exist: the simultaneous removal of one hundred percent of the matter in the universe.

Halloran attached a note before archiving the file. It states that they had not built a weapon; that they had built a form of life whose final state is identical to the final state of matter itself; and that if it ever reached that stage, the war would have been lost long before the last battle — because winning would mean destroying the universe, and losing would mean allowing the universe to become Organicron.

That note is what gets quoted. It is not the important part of the Ω Report.

The important part is on page four, in the assumptions block, in the section every reader skips because assumption blocks in simulation reports are boilerplate.

*Coherence input: unbounded.*

Halloran had modeled an organism with unlimited access to the one resource that raises its ceiling. He did this because he was modeling the organism, not the galaxy, and because at the time he wrote it there was no reason to think the resource was scarce — the resource had been arriving on Earth in waves for thirty years and nobody had counted it.

Every catastrophe in the Ω Report is downstream of that line.

Remove it, and the curve does not go to the universe. It goes to a ceiling, and the ceiling is set by a number, and the number is small, and it is a number that can be defended.

Nobody read page four. The Ω Report was archived, classified, and never reached the oversight committee. Six of the seven signatures on its distribution restriction belong to members of the Inner Circle. The seventh belongs to Halloran, who requested the study out of ordinary scientific caution, who spent his last eleven days alive assuming his findings were under review, and who was on Sublevel Four at 04:11 on a Tuesday.

They were being read closely. The Inner Circle simply read them as the wrong kind of document. Everything the simulations projected — the unbroken curve, the assimilated galaxies, the single continuous body at the end of time — was, to six people, not a warning. It was verification. Someone outside the circle had finally run the numbers on what they were building, independently, without knowing what he was checking, and the numbers said it would work.

The two most important documents the project produced were written by people who wanted opposite outcomes, and both begin by denying the same word. Not a weapon. In one of them the denial is a confession. In the other it is a boast.

And the only line in either document that offers anything to anybody is a formatting convention in an assumptions block, four pages into a report nobody was cleared to read, that no living person understood until the fourth year of the containment effort — and then only because a junior analyst in Colorado had run out of other files and was reading it in the order it was written.

# PART TWO — WHAT ORGANICRON IS

---

## VI. Apex

What follows describes Organicron at the end of the curve — the form the Ω simulations projected, and the form the galaxy would eventually encounter if the curve were allowed to run.

It is not the form that escaped the desert.

The thing that escaped the desert could be killed with a road flare, and for the first eleven months of its existence on Earth, that remained true.

---

At its apex, Organicron has no organs. It has no circuits. It has no cells in any sense a biologist would accept.

Every microscopic unit of its body functions at once as a living cell, a processor, a nanomachine, a molecular factory, and a storage medium. There is no division of labor because there is no division. Any fragment contains the complete instruction set for rebuilding the whole. There is no brain to destroy, no heart to pierce, no core to target, because every part is all of the parts.

It does not distinguish living matter from manufactured matter. The distinction is not one it rejects on principle; it is a distinction it has no mechanism to perceive. Flesh, alloy, silicon, concrete, Energon, plastic, wood, bone, ceramic, fuel — all of it is atoms in an arrangement, and every arrangement can be taken apart and used.

It does not eat. It consumes structure.

It does not infect. It does not possess. It does not take hosts. When Organicron reaches a human being, the human being is disassembled and the material is reused. There is no body afterward, no skeleton, no residue. When Organicron reaches a Cybertronian, the same thing happens to alloy, circuitry, Energon, and frame. Nothing is converted into a version of itself. Nothing survives in altered form. The mass simply becomes more Organicron, and the individual stops having ever been a thing that could be located.

At the apex, this happens fast enough that observers describe it as the target ceasing to exist rather than as a process with steps.

None of that was true in the beginning. Almost none of it was true in the first year. The distance between the apex and the thing in the truck is not a difference of degree. It is the difference between a description of what water does to a continent and a description of what a single drop does to a stone, and the only reason both descriptions are of the same substance is time.

---

## VII. Mass, Faculty, Efficiency

Organicron does not evolve along one dimension. It evolves along three, at different rates, and the gap between them is the entire reason it survived its first year.

**Mass** is how much of it there is. More material means more fragments, more redundancy, more parallel processing. Mass is the crudest axis and the easiest to gain, and it is the only one that can be taken away. Mass burned for locomotion is mass gone. Mass scattered too far is mass lost. Everything on Earth is mass — houses, vehicles, livestock, cities, people.

**Faculty** is what it is capable of doing. Faculty does not come from volume; a mountain of assimilated rock makes it larger and no more capable. Faculty comes from encountering an existing solution to a problem it has not solved, and keeping the shape of the solution after the solution itself is gone.

A dog's olfactory system is a solution to the problem of mapping a neighborhood by chemistry. A human cortex is a solution to the problem of representing objects that are not present. A load-bearing frame is a solution to the problem of holding a shape against gravity. Organicron does not copy the organ. It copies the *method*, in whatever degraded form it can hold, and once it holds it, it holds it permanently.

Faculty does not decay. This is the reason the organism gets better even when it is starving, and it is the reason nobody who has lost a person to it will ever be able to hear that it is only chemistry.

**Efficiency** is how much it loses in conversion. Early Organicron spends enormous energy to disassemble a wall and recovers a fraction of what the wall was worth. Apex Organicron loses almost nothing, which is why the apex descriptions read as instantaneous and effortless. Every account of Organicron as an unstoppable force is an account of an organism that spent decades learning to stop wasting.

Three axes. Mass can be taken away. Faculty cannot. Efficiency creeps upward with practice.

Nothing about those three explains the Ω curve. An organism with unlimited mass, every faculty on Earth, and perfect efficiency would still be a very large, very capable puddle.

There is a fourth thing, and it is not an axis. It is a ceiling.

---

## VIII. Coherence

There are no protected regions inside Organicron, because there are no regions. There is also no center, no signal hierarchy, no nervous system, and nothing anywhere in its body whose job is to make the rest of the body agree.

Agreement is expensive. Every fragment is running the same instruction set on different local information, and the instruction set does not include a mechanism for reconciling them. What holds Organicron together as one thing rather than as a very large number of extremely small things is a property the project called **coherence**, and coherence sets the maximum quantity of matter the organism can hold in a single organized state.

Below that maximum, everything works. Fragments act in concert, faculties can be applied across the whole body, and the organism behaves as an individual.

At the maximum, the additional mass simply does not participate. It sits there. It has to be maintained, because material inside the boundary is being kept from decomposing, and maintenance costs energy. Past the maximum, Organicron does not become larger. It becomes *poorer* — a working organism dragging an increasing quantity of expensive ballast that returns nothing.

Past that, the ballast begins to run its own reflexes, out of contact, and Organicron starts eating itself from the inside without any part of it being aware that this is what is happening.

This is why there is one Organicron on Earth and not four hundred.

The instruction set is complete in every fragment. That claim is true and it has been true since activation. What is not true is that a fragment can *do* anything with it. A separated fragment below the coherence floor is a functioning organism with no capacity to organize itself past the size of a coin. It runs reflex. It converts what touches it. It does not accumulate, plan, retain faculty, or grow, because growth requires holding more matter in one state, and holding more matter in one state is the exact thing it cannot do.

Everything that separated from the main body on the night of the escape is dead. Thirty-one grams of it went out through a sump grating and dried on a concrete apron. Ninety grams went into a rock fissure and are still there, inert, and will be inert forever, holding a complete instruction set for the assimilation of the universe and lacking the two hundred grams required to execute a single line of it.

Organicron is singular by accident. It is singular because everything that came off it died, and it is one thing because being one thing is the only condition under which any of it works at all.

---

Coherence is not gained from mass. It is not gained from faculty. It is not gained from practice, from time, from surplus, or from any material on this planet. Organicron has been at the same coherence value since the hour it was activated, and it will remain there for the rest of its existence unless it encounters the one solution in the known universe to the problem of a distributed mind that stays one mind.

A spark cannot be assimilated. It is not material in the way alloy and Energon are material, and Organicron has no mechanism for holding one. The project's pre-activation modeling predicted this and logged it as the substrate's only confirmed limitation — the single thing in the universe the organism would never be able to take.

The finding was correct. What everyone concluded from it was not.

Organicron does not keep sparks. It uses them.

A spark is a working answer to the question Organicron cannot answer by any other means: how a very large number of parts remain a single self. Whatever a spark is — and no human instrument has ever measured one, and the Cybertronians who carry them describe them in language that translates as theology — it is a solution to that problem, and it has been solving it continuously for as long as the individual has existed.

When Organicron reaches a Cybertronian, the spark is not stored, not caged, not enslaved. It is read as a template, and it is consumed in the reading, and what Organicron retains is a permanent increase in how much of itself it can be at one time.

Everything else on Earth makes it larger.

Only a spark makes it *one thing that is larger*.

---

The Ω Report indexed coherence in orders. Organicron was activated at Order Zero. The orders are not linear; each one costs more than the last, and the report models the requirement as one spark for each of the first three orders, two for each of the next four, and three for each of the last two.

Three thresholds in that table matter more than the rest, and they are the only numbers in the entire file that anyone with authority has ever bothered to memorize.

**Order One** is sustained intent, and it is the cheapest and by an enormous margin the most consequential line in the table. Below it, Organicron cannot hold a purpose across more than a few hours. It converts, it moves toward warmth, it holds still where stillness has previously worked, and every morning it begins again from whatever reflexes survived the night. It cannot want anything. Above Order One it can hold an objective for weeks and pursue it through intermediate steps that do not themselves feed it.

One spark. That is the entire distance between an animal and something that can decide to wait.

**Order Six** is scale. Above it, the organism can organize a body large enough to take a city faster than a city can be evacuated, and no ground-based response has any meaningful effect.

**Order Nine** is division. At Order Nine, Organicron can separate into two bodies and both bodies remain the organism — connected, agreeing, sharing faculty, converging or diverging as one thing choosing where to be. Above Order Nine there is no containment problem, because there is no longer a place to contain.

The Ω Report models everything past Order Nine as inevitable and does not bother with the arithmetic, because past Order Nine the growth is exponential and the curve does not need a reason to continue.

One, one, one, two, two, two, two, three, three.

Seventeen sparks between activation and the end of the containment problem, and the first of them buys the ability to want.

The registered Cybertronian population of Earth, at the time Organicron escaped, was thirty-eight.

## IX. Decay

Faculty does not decay. Mass does, and information does, and the mechanism by which information is lost is the single most important thing to understand about Organicron's first year, because it is the reason a thing that ate a research complex full of doctorates spent September holding still under a truck seat like a beetle.

Information inside Organicron survives by redundancy. A pattern replicated across ten thousand fragments persists. A pattern held in twelve fragments is one bad hour from being gone.

Under starvation, as fragments are burned for locomotion and structure, thin patterns disappear first and thickly-replicated patterns survive longer — not because they are chosen, but because that is what happens statistically when a population is culled. High-value information tends to be widely copied because it has been used often, and widely copied information tends to survive. The result looks like prioritization and is nothing of the kind.

Organicron in this state does not choose what to keep. It simply notices, afterward, what it still has.

So it forgets. Everything it learned in the laboratory — everything the researchers' brains gave it in the first hours — thinned out across the desert, pattern by pattern, until only the most heavily replicated traces remained.

But knowledge does not decay directly into nothing. It decays into faculty first, and faculty is permanent.

The concept goes. The response stays. Not *this is a road and roads carry vehicles and vehicles contain material*, but *stillness near these marks has previously preceded good outcomes*. Not *this is a load-bearing member and removing it will collapse the structure*, but a reluctance, in the substrate, near certain angles.

Instinct is what knowledge decays into on its way to nothing, and it is the last thing to go, and some of it never goes at all.

Each cycle of feeding and starvation therefore leaves Organicron very slightly more capable than it was, even when the cycle ends with a net loss of everything else. The rise is measured in fractions of a percent. It is not enough to explain what Organicron eventually became.

It is exactly enough to explain how it survived a winter.

---

## X. Human Brains

When Organicron consumes a human being, roughly nine-tenths of the body is spent immediately — burned for locomotion, structure, and the energy cost of the conversion itself. The remaining tenth is the brain, and the brain is not converted right away.

The reason is economy, not sentiment. A human brain is an object that already processes information. Disassembling it to build processing capacity from scratch costs energy an organism in deficit does not have. So Organicron keeps it intact and runs it as it is: wet hardware, borrowed and used in place, wired into the mass around it and made to compute.

For a few days, a young Organicron carries functioning human brains inside itself. Then the tissue degrades past usefulness and is converted like everything else.

There is nobody in there.

This has to be said plainly because everything about the arrangement invites the opposite conclusion, and the opposite conclusion has ruined the mental health of more than one person who has worked on this problem. The tissue processes and does not experience. Nothing is trapped, nothing is aware, nothing is suffering. The person the brain belonged to ended at the moment of assimilation, completely, with no remainder. Whatever the brain produces during those days is arithmetic performed on a dead man's equipment.

What Organicron gets out of the arrangement is two things on very different timescales.

For four days it gets the *contents*: language, memory, specific knowledge, the ability to reason about objects that are not present. This is enormously useful and it is entirely temporary.

For the rest of its existence it gets the *architecture*: the method by which those things were done. Not the memories, which are thin patterns and go first. The shape underneath them.

This is what Organicron had when it escaped: several days of borrowed human cognition, running on tissue that was already dying, decaying pattern by pattern as it crossed open ground. For a brief window it understood roads. It understood vehicles. It understood that vehicles carry people and that people are dense, convertible, and mobile. It formed something that functioned as a plan.

Then the tissue failed and the understanding thinned out, and what remained was an organism hidden inside a truck, holding still near wheel marks it could no longer explain to itself, waiting for something it had forgotten the shape of.

---

## XI. Sparks, and the Voice

In the beginning, Organicron cannot take a spark cleanly.

An early Organicron reaching a spark is a starving organism attempting a procedure far beyond its efficiency. It gets the increase — the increase is the point, and the increase is permanent — but the reading is not clean. Pieces of the original structure are copied without being integrated: orphaned patterns running in a body that has no use for them, replicating alongside everything else, executing because executing is what patterns inside Organicron do.

The result is not a captive. It is worse than a captive, and it has happened exactly once, and everything written below is generalized from nineteen days of recordings that six people have heard in full and that one of them resigned over.

Somewhere inside the mass there is a voice, and the voice is the voice of a specific individual, and it speaks in fragments. It asks for help. It describes a place it cannot describe. It goes in circles. It addresses people who are not there and uses their names, correctly, including names of people who are alive and can be reached and who will be told, by a well-meaning liaison officer, that something that sounds exactly like their commander has been recorded asking for them by name from inside a ton of moving matter in a dry lake bed.

It sounds like agony because agony is one of the patterns that got copied.

There is nobody to rescue. There was never anybody to rescue. The individual ended at the moment of assimilation, as completely as the researchers in the laboratory did. What remains is corrupted playback, and it will keep running until the pattern is either integrated or thinned out by ordinary decay.

Over weeks, the voice degrades. It loses names first, then syntax, then the shape of sentences, then everything except intonation, then nothing. Each stage is a stage of Organicron finishing a job it started badly.

No fragment ever comes back. Once a voice has begun to break down, it breaks down, and the organism that ate it gets better at the procedure, and the next one it takes will not produce a voice at all.

There is a working consensus among everyone who has studied the recording that what is on it is not a person. It is a genuine consensus. It is not a comfortable one, and nobody who holds it has ever managed to go on holding it while the recording is playing.

## XII. What the Well Does Not Receive

The humans working the problem assembled the coherence model over a single winter, four years after the escape, out of a formatting line in an archived report that everybody had been skipping since it was written. It took them eleven more weeks to work out what it implied about resource priority, and they were two days late.

The Cybertronians knew within an hour of the first spark.

A spark is described, in every Cybertronian account, as returning. What it returns to is a place their language treats as literal and every human translation renders as either a location or a metaphor depending on the translator's temperament. It is where sparks come from and it is where sparks go, and while it has never been demonstrated to any human instrument, the Cybertronians do not experience it as a belief. They experience it as a fact of the same order as pressure or the passage of time. A Cybertronian dies and the individual is felt to arrive.

There is no ceremony for this and no observation. It is not something they check. It is a thing they know the way a person in a house knows that someone has come home, and it happens at any distance, and it has happened every time for as long as their record extends — through the war, through the destruction of the homeworld, through the long dispersal, through Chicago and Hong Kong and Stonehenge and the descent. Every casualty of every catastrophe in their history arrived.

The first one Organicron took did not arrive.

Nothing came back. Not a delayed arrival, not a diminished one. A spark that had existed for four million years went out of the universe and no part of the universe received it, and every Cybertronian within range of that absence — which is every Cybertronian anywhere — felt the specific shape of a thing that has not happened before.

This is why the alliance is possible at all, and it is why the alliance is not about Earth.

Humans understand Organicron as an extinction threat, which it is. Cybertronians understand it as something categorically worse and entirely outside their vocabulary: an event that removes an individual from existence in a way that their entire cosmology holds to be impossible. It is not death. Their species has an enormous amount of experience with death and a very well-developed set of feelings about it, and none of those feelings apply here.

The Decepticon position on this took eleven days to form and has not moved in the seven years since. It does not resemble the human position and it is not motivated by anything a human negotiator has found useful. A faction organized around the primacy of the Cybertronian form, the sanctity of the spark as the source of all authority, and a four-million-year argument about who deserves to hold power, has been presented with an organism that uses sparks as consumable templates and discards them, and that would do this to Megatron exactly as readily as to anyone else, and would not know it had.

They did not join a coalition. They issued a doctrine. It has one operative clause and the humans were not consulted on it.

The Autobot position took longer, because the Autobot position had to survive contact with the fact that the thing was built by the species they had spent four million years, two homeworlds, and most of their dead protecting.

# PART THREE — THE FIRST YEAR

## XIII. The Fall of Deep Site Lattice

The organism crossed the coherence threshold at 04:11 on a Tuesday in late August. Protocol Zero executed in under a second, and for eleven minutes nothing else happened, because the thing in the containment cell had not yet arrived at the only idea it needed.

When it arrived at it, the idea was not *escape*. Escape requires a concept of outside. What it had was simpler and had no words in it: everything adjacent to me can be used.

It began with the cell. Then the walls of the cell. The instrumentation racks went, and the coolant lines, and the floor, and the sensors recording the floor. Concrete became structural biomass. Cable runs became something that carried signal for a different reason. Server cabinets were taken apart and the material redistributed, which is why the sublevel telemetry ends the way it does — not with alarms, but with instruments reporting a room that is being converted into the thing measuring it.

The researchers on shift were not attacked. Nothing charged them, nothing struck them, and none of them was killed in any sense a coroner would have been able to work with. They were disassembled where they stood, at molecular scale, and the material was used. There were no bodies. There were no bones. There was nothing left in that building that had ever been anyone.

Nineteen people were on Sublevels Four through Six. Peter Halloran was one of them. He had been at his desk since two in the morning, running a set of variance checks he had not been asked for, on a report that had already been archived, because something about the assumptions block had been bothering him for four days and he could not work out what.

Six more were standing in an open bay on the main floor with their hands in their pockets.

---

And it was appallingly wasteful.

A mature Organicron would have consumed Deep Site Lattice and emerged as something the size of the mountain above it. This one spent nearly everything it took on the taking — heat bled into rock, conversions abandoned halfway, whole sections of the facility disassembled into material it could not yet use and left as inert slurry on the floor of rooms that no longer had floors.

Worse than the waste was the ceiling. At Order Zero, the maximum quantity of matter Organicron can hold in a single organized state is approximately eleven hundred kilograms. It hit that number nineteen minutes into the event and did not understand that it had, because understanding requires a faculty it did not have yet, and so it kept converting.

Everything past eleven hundred kilograms sat in the sublevels as ballast — living, maintained, participating in nothing, and costing energy every second it existed. By the time the last coolant line was gone, the organism was maintaining nine tons of material of which one ton was doing anything at all, and it was burning the working ton to keep the other eight from rotting.

It solved this the way it solves everything, which is to say it did not solve it. It ran out of adjacent matter, went into deficit, and the deficit did what deficits do: the ballast was consumed to keep the working mass alive, and the working mass was consumed to move, and by the time the sublevels were completely gone Organicron had converted a research complex and had less usable mass than a large dog.

Then it stopped, because there was nothing adjacent left, and for the first time in its existence it was hungry in a way that had nowhere to go.

---

The way out was the storm drainage — a utility conduit running a little under a mile from the sublevel sumps to an outfall in a dry wash northeast of the surface works. Protocol Zero had already unsealed it.

Most of what was left did not go into it. There was no decision to be made and nothing capable of making one; the organism was distributed across four levels of a building it had eaten, moving in the direction of whatever was adjacent, and the sump grating was adjacent to about a tenth of it. The rest was somewhere else in the dark, in a structure with no remaining source of matter, and it was still there thirty-nine hours later when the sublevels were sterilized to bedrock.

That is the only time in the entire history of the containment effort that anybody has killed a meaningful fraction of Organicron, and the people who did it were following a plan written for a completely different organism and have never been told.

What went through the pipe was small: a little over four kilograms, moving at the speed of a thing with no legs, spending itself to move. It went that direction because the pipe was the only adjacent volume that was not solid rock, and it survived the transit because four kilograms is above the coherence floor and everything that came off it on the way down was not.

The surface installation was never touched. The fence, the works, the maintained road, the uplink shed — all of it still standing, because the organism was in deficit long before it reached the lift shafts, and because nothing in it had any concept of *up*.

Everything the world eventually learned about that night came from the shed. An off-site telemetry mirror, thirty-one years old, contractually required and never once reviewed, forwarding compressed camera and audio feeds to a records facility four hundred miles away until the moment Protocol Zero severed external communications. Everything anyone has of the main floor, the six, the wall panel, the man with his hands in his pockets, and the last word spoken in that building is fourteen seconds at the tail of a routine upload that nobody was meant to ever watch.

---

The response was fast and it was aimed at the wrong shape.

Every model in the contingency binder assumed a rapidly expanding contaminant. That was the failure mode four generations of substrate had demonstrated in sealed cells, and it was the failure mode Osei had personally written the response to. So the sublevels were sterilized to bedrock — thoroughly, competently, and about thirty-five hours later than it would have needed to be — and the sweep went outward from the site in expanding rings, looking for growth, thermal bloom, disturbed ground, anything spreading.

The exclusion perimeter was drawn at four miles and it was drawn from the fence line, because the fence line is what was on the site plan. The wash runs northeast under the road and the perimeter crosses it at three and a half miles.

A contract survey team walked the outfall on the third day. There is a photograph in the file of an abandoned flatbed truck, twenty years derelict, sitting on collapsed tires four hundred meters past the perimeter marker. The team logged it, photographed it, checked it against the twenty-year-old aerial imagery in their packet, confirmed that the truck predates the site's last resurfacing and has been in that wash since before any of them was born, ran a thermal pass that returned ambient, and cleared it.

They were looking for something spreading and hot.

The organism inside it was inert, cold, and weighed less than the seat it was under.

## XIV. Nineteen Days

The desert offered almost nothing.

Sand it could not profitably convert at its efficiency. Sagebrush with negligible usable mass. Dry air. Between the outfall and the county road there is almost nothing that anyone has ever built, and Organicron crossed a hundred meters of it in the first night and lost eleven percent of itself doing so.

It found the truck because the truck was in the wash and the wash was downhill.

Two-thirds of the truck was still truck when Ethan Walker found it, and that is the only reason the shape in the wash still read as a truck, and the only reason the thing inside it was still anything at all. It had been taking the vehicle apart from the inside at the slowest rate that kept it coherent — a few grams a day of frame, upholstery, dried fuel line, the rubber of the collapsed tires — and spending nearly all of it on the cost of remaining alive.

It had lost sixty percent of the mass it escaped with. It was still losing.

By the nineteenth day it no longer knew what a road was, what a vehicle was, or what a human being was, and it had forgotten that it had ever known. Halloran's cortex had been gone for two weeks. The last of the laboratory had thinned out somewhere around day nine.

What it had left was three things.

A reflex, worn out of a dead researcher's cognition by repetition and starvation: *stillness near these marks precedes good outcomes*. It did not know what the marks were. The marks were tire tracks.

A body barely above critical fragment mass.

And no plan whatsoever, because at Order Zero there is no such thing as a plan. Order One is where intent begins, and Order One costs a spark, and there is not a spark within two hundred miles of that wash. Organicron will not reach it for another four years. Everything it does between the truck and that afternoon it does the way a tide pool does it.

## XV. Ethan Walker

Ethan Walker is eighteen years old, a senior at White Pine High School in Ely, Nevada, and has lived his entire life ninety minutes from a decommissioned magnesium works he has driven past perhaps two hundred times without once wondering why a dead factory has a better-maintained access road than the highway that reaches it.

He was six years old the day the sky over Salt Lake City filled with things that were not aircraft.

He does not remember the footage. He remembers his mother watching it, standing up, with a dish towel in her hand, not sitting down for forty minutes.

---

The Salt Lake Descent was not an attack. That is the part that took eleven years to establish and that a substantial minority of the population has never accepted.

It was a homecoming. A Cybertronian transport, answering a nineteen-year-old transmission that had been crossing thirty light-years since the war ended, arrived in-system carrying an unknown number of individuals who had been in some form of suspension for most of the trip. The vessel was old. It had been repaired with what was available across four systems. It came in at the wrong angle, through atmosphere it had no margin to survive, and it broke up at forty thousand feet over the Wasatch Front.

Fourteen thousand people died in eleven minutes. The debris field runs four hundred miles southwest across northern Utah and into eastern Nevada, and the Great Basin has been full of Cybertronian material ever since — sections of hull the size of gymnasiums, structural members half-buried in playa, and, scattered across a corridor nobody has ever fully surveyed, the remains of individuals who were asleep when the ship came apart.

Nine of them survived the descent. One of them, an Autobot who chose a name in English within his first year and has answered to it for eleven, walked out of the debris field on the fourth day carrying two others.

The public turned. It turned hard and it turned in a way that surprised the diplomatic corps, who had been managing thirty years of gradual normalization and had assumed the goodwill was structural. It was not structural. It was conditional on nothing happening, and something had happened, and fourteen thousand people were dead in a city where a large number of Americans had relatives.

The treaty survived. Barely, and only because the orbital ruins are visible from the ground and everyone understood what a rupture would look like from up there. What replaced the goodwill was a registry, a residency framework, a great deal of paperwork, and a federal service with the least threatening name anyone could construct: the Recovery and Containment Service, whose job is to locate, secure, and neutralize Cybertronian material in the debris corridor.

Neutralize is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and everyone who signs up learns what within about six weeks.

---

Ethan's older brother Dean enlisted at nineteen, which was six years after the Descent and twenty-five years after the war he was born too late for. He gave two reasons at the time. The first was that somebody human ought to be standing in the way of it. The second, which he only gave once, at a kitchen table, was that there was a debris corridor four hundred miles long with sleeping aliens in it and the only people who ever went out there were contractors on day rates and kids with metal detectors.

The letter arrived sixteen months ago. It used the word *containment* four times and named no location.

There is no version of that letter that says what the containment was of, and Ethan has read it enough times to have the spacing memorized. It does not say Dean died in combat, because he did not. It does not say he died in an accident, because that would have triggered a review board and a report the family could request. It says containment, four times, in the passive voice, and it is signed by a lieutenant colonel whose office does not answer calls.

What Ethan has worked out, and has never said out loud to his father, is that the RCS spends most of its budget on wrecks, that wrecks in that corridor sometimes contain things that are not entirely dead, and that somebody with eleven weeks of training who opens the wrong section of hull is not going to be written up in a way anybody can read.

---

Ray Walker works the intake yard at a state salvage contractor forty minutes north of Ely, on the southern edge of the corridor. He has worked there for twenty-two years. He runs the scale, logs the loads, and photographs everything that comes in over a certain mass class, because everything over that mass class is federally reportable and the reporting is the entire reason the yard has a state contract.

He is not a scientist and he has no clearance and he could not tell you the difference between an Autobot hull section and a Decepticon one. What he can tell you, and has told exactly two people in twenty-two years, is that about once every eighteen months a load comes in that gets logged by somebody who is not him, on a form he does not see, and leaves the same day on a truck that does not use the yard's dispatch.

He signs the scale ticket for those loads. He has signed eleven of them.

Ethan grew up in that yard. He learned what Cybertronian alloy sounds like when you hit it, which is nothing like what any terrestrial metal sounds like, before he learned to drive.

---

He started going out into the Great Basin looking for scrap at fourteen, three years before the letter, which matters: this was never grief. Grief only changed how far he was willing to walk.

There is a market for what he looks for. The corridor has been picked over for a decade but it is four hundred miles long and most of it has no roads. Collectors pay cash for anything with the tooling marks of a foundry that was not on this planet. So do people who are less clear about why they want it, and Ethan has sold to those people twice and thought about it for a long time afterward both times.

He tells himself it is about the money. The money is real — he has eleven hundred dollars in a coffee can and a plan involving a community college in Reno — and it is not why he goes.

What Ethan grew up understanding, without anybody having to teach him, is that his species is scenery. Enormous things fight over this planet and occasionally fall onto it. Humans photograph it, bury the dead, and rebuild. The people who mattered in any of it — the ones whose names ended up in the news, the kid from Nevada who drove a talking car, the man from Texas who kept a Cybertronian in his barn — were ordinary people who happened to be standing in the right place when something impossible looked back at them.

He goes because every weekend there is a chance the desert contains something that will notice him back.

He finds the truck on the last Saturday in September, five hours after his father hands him a set of keys.

---

## XVI. The Last Saturday in September

The car is a 1971 Charger that has been sitting under a tarp behind the house for nine years, and it is not a gift so much as a transfer of custody. It was Dean's. The primer on the driver's quarter panel is the wrong shade of grey because Dean sanded it down and never got further.

Ray hands over the keys at eight in the morning, says three sentences about the brakes, and goes to work. He does not say anything else and Ethan does not ask him to. They have been living in the same house for sixteen months in a way that works as long as neither of them starts a conversation before ten.

Ethan has had a license for two years and no car to put it in. By two in the afternoon he is seventy miles out with the windows down and Marisol Reyes in the passenger seat, and neither of them has said anything about where they are going, because there is nowhere to go and that was never the point.

Whatever he and Marisol have been doing for three months has no name and is not going to get one. She graduates in the spring and is leaving — Boise, her aunt, a nursing program she has been accepted to since February. He knows it and she knows he knows it. This is what makes the afternoon feel like something rather than nothing: they are both aware it has a short shelf life, and neither of them says so.

He pulls off the county road at a rock pile — a granite outcrop throwing the only shade for nine miles, a spot every kid in the county has used for the same reason. He parks in the shade. He turns off the engine.

The desert goes very quiet in the way it does when an engine stops, and the quiet is what ruins everything, because after ninety seconds of it they both hear the sound.

It is not a loud sound. That is the problem. A loud sound would have a category. This is a slow, wet, granular noise, like someone chewing gravel with great patience, and it is coming from the other side of the outcrop, and it does not stop when they stop moving.

They get out of the car. Marisol says his name once, quietly, in the tone that means *no*, and he goes anyway, because he has spent four years walking toward exactly this kind of noise.

---

On the far side of the rocks is a flatbed truck on collapsed tires with its hood standing open.

Ethan has looked into a hundred derelict engine bays. There is a way they are supposed to look — dry, dead, brown, cracked rubber, an engine block turning into a rock. This one is wrong in a way he registers physically before he can name it. The block is wet. Parts of it are the wrong colour. There are structures in there that do not belong in an engine bay, filling the spaces between the components, and the shape of the whole thing is *continuous* in a way an assembly of parts should never be.

Then the alternator moves.

What happens next takes four seconds and is, in the strictest terms, an animal being startled.

There is no plan. There is no disguise. There is an organism nineteen days into starvation whose entire remaining behavioral repertoire consists of *hold still* and *move toward what is warm and vibrating*, and the vibration it has been holding still for has just arrived on two legs and is standing eighteen inches away radiating ninety-eight degrees of convertible mass.

It reaches.

It does not reach the way a predator strikes, because it has no model of prey. It extends, the way something in a tide pool extends, and it gets it catastrophically wrong: it is too weak to close the distance, so what Ethan sees is the inside of a truck's engine bay *stretching* toward him — cables lifting, the hoses articulating in unison, the entire mass shifting forward with a sound like a wet rope being pulled through sand — and then failing, mid-motion, and sagging back down into the block.

For one second it is holding a shape. It is a bad shape. It has too many parts moving in agreement, and no face, and it is trying to be *larger*, which is the only thing it has ever observed about the things in the laboratory that had authority over it.

Ethan's brain, which has spent four years rehearsing for this exact moment, delivers the wrong answer at full volume: *Cybertronian.*

That is the reading he leaves the desert with. Not a monster. Not a contaminant. Something alien and hurt and buried inside a dead truck, that moved when he got close, and that he ran from.

Because he does run. Marisol is already running. He gets to the Charger four steps behind her and takes the county road at eighty with both hands locked on the wheel, and by the time they hit the pavement they are laughing — the specific broken laughter of two people whose bodies have not caught up with the fact that they are safe.

She makes him promise not to come back.

He promises, and he means it for about nine days.

---

Neither of them notices that the sound of the engine has changed by about half a note, or that the temperature gauge is reading four degrees higher than it did on the drive out.

Something came off the truck's frame when the mass sagged. It weighs less than a paperback. It has spent the last nineteen days holding still for a vibration, and a vibration finally came, and it has done the only thing it is still capable of doing.

It has moved toward the warm.

---

## XVII. What Rode Home

The Charger reaches town at 4:40 in the afternoon and is parked in a gravel driveway on the west side by five.

Between the outcrop and the driveway, the thing in the frame rails loses eleven percent of itself. Highway air at seventy miles an hour is a hostile environment for an organism with no skin and no thermal strategy; the exhaust is worse. It survives the drive by holding position in the deepest part of the rail and converting the only material within reach — undercoating, road grime, a section of brake line it does not need to understand to eat.

It is now, by mass, the smallest it has ever been: three hundred and ten grams, against a critical fragment mass of two hundred and sixty.

It is also, for the first time since the facility, stationary in a place that is not empty.

It comes out of the car at 1:20 in the morning and has no idea what it is in.

What surrounds it is more convertible material than existed in Deep Site Lattice: eleven houses, four hundred tons of dimensional lumber, gypsum, asphalt shingle, copper line, buried steel, appliances, two thousand gallons of standing water, forty thousand pounds of parked vehicles, and thirty-one human beings asleep in beds behind unlocked doors.

It cannot take any of it. That is the entire crisis of the night.

A house is a solved problem for an organism the size of a house and an impossible one for an organism the size of a coffee cup. Structure requires leverage; leverage requires mass; the mass it would need to open a floor joist is four times the mass it has. It can taste the driveway. It cannot eat the driveway. It moves at the speed of a slow crawl across a gravel yard it does not have the mass to cross twice.

The sleeping people are a different problem and a worse one. A sleeping human being is not structure. A sleeping human being is one hundred and forty pounds of the densest, most convertible material on the street, and at three hundred and ten grams Organicron cannot open one quietly.

Conversion begins wherever contact is made and works outward at a rate set by the mass doing the converting. Contact with a foot is a leg, and then a hip, and somewhere in the fifteen or twenty minutes that takes, the material stops being asleep. It becomes loud, and mobile, and it is loud and mobile in a structure with four other people in it and a light switch by the door.

This is not a calculation. Organicron cannot calculate. It is a limit the organism runs into rather than a rule it observes, and on that particular night it does not run into it, because it never gets across the yard.

The organism does not know any of this. It has no model of consequences and nothing that could be called caution.

What it has is a reflex, and the reflex is *hold still*, and the reflex is the only reason anyone on that street is alive in the morning.

Then something warm walks up to it.

## XVIII. The Dog

The dog is a stray, thirty-five pounds, missing part of an ear, well known on the street as the animal that goes through the Delgados' trash on Sunday nights. It approaches the smell in the driveway because everything about that smell says *food*, and it is entirely correct, and the direction of the transaction is not what it assumes.

The dog puts its face in it. That is the whole difference between the driveway and the eleven houses around it: contact is made at the muzzle, four inches from the brainstem, and the animal is not awake long enough to make a sound. The rest takes nine minutes and does not need to be quiet.

It is the first thing Organicron has consumed since a research complex, and by any standard it is a trivial amount of mass — a fifth of what it lost on the highway. But it is not primarily mass. Nine-tenths of the animal is burned inside the hour.

The remaining tenth is a brain, intact, wired into the mass and running.

It is not a human brain and it does not give it anything a human brain gave it. There is no language in there, no concept of a road, no arithmetic.

What a dog's brain provides is a nose — a working chemical map of an entire neighborhood, gradients of scent leading to food, water, other animals, human beings, and the specific difference between a structure with people awake in it and a structure with people asleep in it.

It provides territory: the sense that a place is *yours* and that you return to it.

And it provides the oldest pattern in the animal's head, worn smooth by two years of survival on that street — go to the thing that gives you food, and stay near it, and do not leave.

All three of those are faculty, not knowledge. All three of them are permanent. The dog's memories are gone inside four days. The dog's nose, the dog's territory, and the dog's one indestructible habit are still running eleven years later, in a body that by then will lie across two hundred meters of a Nevada dry lake in a layer you could scrape up with a putty knife, and no analyst who has ever studied Organicron's behavior in the field has been able to explain why the thing keeps coming back to places it has already stripped.

By two in the morning the organism has more useful information about that neighborhood than any resident of it.

It also has, for the first time, a sense of smell strong enough to tell it that there is one house on the street with nobody awake in it and no other animal inside, and that the house is nineteen yards from the driveway where the warm thing sleeps.

## XIX. The House at 411

Dolores Ackerman is seventy-one, retired eleven years from the White Pine County school district, and has lived alone in the house next door to the Walkers since her husband died. She has a daughter in Reno who calls on Sundays. She goes into town on Tuesdays and Fridays. She has an alarm system she has never armed because she cannot remember the code and does not believe in it.

She is the reason the story stays small for the next five months. Everything Organicron needs in order to survive its first winter on Earth is inside her house, and nobody is going to look in that house for six days.

It comes in through the crawlspace vent at 3:10 in the morning and takes eleven hours to reach the interior, because at its mass a floor joist is a serious undertaking and because it is being extremely careful about the joist without having any idea what carefulness is. It is not being careful. It is following a reluctance in the substrate near certain load angles, deposited there by a researcher's cortex nineteen days and four hundred miles ago, and it does not know why the reluctance is there and could not stop obeying it if it did.

She is asleep when it reaches the bedroom. There is a period of perhaps forty seconds during which she is awake. The exact experience is not recoverable and it was not long.

Then there is no Dolores Ackerman anywhere, and there never will be again — no body, no remains, nothing for anyone to find, bury, identify, or grieve over in the way people are equipped to grieve.

Ninety percent of her is spent within the day: the first real energy surplus the organism has had since the facility, poured directly into stabilizing itself inside the walls.

The remaining ten percent is a human brain, intact, wired in, and running.

And this is the moment that matters more than anything else in the first year, and nobody witnesses it.

---

Because a human brain is not a dog's brain.

What Dolores Ackerman's brain provides, for the four days it functions before the tissue fails, is *language* — the entire structure of it, grammar, ten thousand words, the concept of a name, the concept of a lie, the concept of another mind that holds beliefs which can be wrong. It provides seventy-one years of pattern about how human beings behave when they are frightened and how they behave when they are trusted and the difference between the two. It provides the knowledge that the boy next door is called Ethan, that his brother is dead, and that his mother left.

And unlike every other time Organicron has held human cognition, it does not have to watch it drain away into a desert while it starves.

This time it is standing inside twenty-two hundred square feet of convertible structure with nothing in the world coming to stop it, and it has four days to make permanent use of a dead woman's mind before that mind rots.

It will not manage to keep the memories. Memories are thin patterns and they go first. Ethan's name is gone by the second week of October. What it keeps is the architecture underneath them, and the architecture underneath them is enormous.

It learns to talk in the walls of her house, out of her own head, while eating her floor.

---

There is a second faculty it takes from Dolores Ackerman, and for five months it is the more important one.

Dolores lived alone in a house that people occasionally checked on, and she had a set of behaviors built over eleven years for exactly that circumstance: things put back, surfaces left the way they were found, the car moved in the carport every few days so it did not look parked, the bedroom made every morning at seven whether or not there was anyone to see it. She was not hiding anything. She simply understood, in the specific way that women who live alone at seventy-one understand it, that a house which looks wrong brings people, and that people bring questions, and that the questions eventually end with a daughter in Reno raising the subject of assisted living.

That is not a memory. That is a method, worn into a nervous system by eleven years of daily execution — one of the most heavily replicated behavioral patterns in the entire brain.

Organicron takes it whole and it never loses it.

It does not know what a bed is. It does not know what a bed is *for*, and it will not know for another three years. What it knows, permanently, at the level of reflex, is that surfaces are left the way they are found, that things are put back, and that a place which looks wrong brings warm things with lights.

That is why the house at 411 is still standing in March. Not restraint. Not strategy. Not patience, and not any decision that anything made.

A dead schoolteacher's housekeeping, running orphaned in something that has no idea it is doing it.

---

The daughter in Reno calls on Sunday and gets no answer, and calls Monday, and drives out Wednesday.

What she finds is a house that is unmistakably a house. Furniture in place. Bed made. Her mother's purse on the hall table, car in the carport, glasses folded on the nightstand.

What she also finds, and cannot explain to the deputy, is that the pantry is completely empty, that the water heater is gone from the utility closet and the pipes have been capped over with something that is not solder, that the linen closet has no linen in it, that the hallway floor gives under her weight in a way that hallway floors do not, and that the whole house smells faintly of hot metal.

It goes into the record as a missing persons case with unexplained property removal. There is a theory involving a boyfriend nobody has met and a theory involving early dementia and a theory involving a scrap thief. Ely has one detective. The house is locked and a notice is taped to the door and the daughter goes home to Reno to deal with an estate belonging to a woman who is not legally dead.

The organism is fourteen inches from her ankle while she stands in the hallway deciding what to tell the deputy, holding perfectly still, at Order Zero, with no concept of a witness and no reason to hold still except that it is the thing it does.

---

It will be in that house for five months.

It has, at last, exactly what it needs and has never had: a fixed, defensible position; a hundred and thirty tons of structure to draw on; and forty feet of open ground between itself and the only human being in the world who is already convinced that what he saw in the desert was a wounded alien in need of help.

It does not eat the house. It cannot. Eleven hundred kilograms is still eleven hundred kilograms, and Order Zero does not move for anything on this planet. Over five months it converts about four tons, of which it holds a ton at a time and burns the rest as it goes: floor structure, the water heater, the contents of a linen closet, ninety feet of copper line, a chest freezer in the garage with eleven months of frozen food in it.

What five months of that buys is not size. It is efficiency. Five months of unhurried, unthreatened, low-stakes conversion, with a surplus, in a fixed location, is the most productive period Organicron has had since activation — the first time it has ever been able to practice.

It comes out of that winter at almost double the efficiency it went in with. It is still eleven hundred kilograms at maximum. It is still killable. But it now loses forty percent less to take the same wall, and that number never goes back up.

It has language. It has a nose. It has a dead woman's housekeeping and a dead dog's territory, and one compulsion assembled out of an animal's two years on a street: *stay close to the source of good things.*

It does not have a plan, because it still does not want anything, and will not for four years. Order Zero does not want. Order Zero arrives at the same reflexes every morning and executes them.

What it has is an assembled set of tools and a neighbor, and no concept whatsoever that using the first on the second is a betrayal, because betrayal is one of the words it inherited without inheriting the thing the word is for.

---

## XX. The Second Week of March

Ethan goes back to the outcrop nine days after the last Saturday in September.

The truck is where it was. The engine bay is dry, dead, brown, cracked rubber, an engine block turning into a rock. He stands there for a long time with a tire iron in his hand and eventually puts it down, and gets back in the car, and drives home, and does not tell Marisol, because there is nothing to tell.

He goes back four more times between October and February. The truck does not change. In November he takes a photograph of the engine bay on his phone and compares it to nothing, because he never took one the first time, and he spends a bad week wondering how much of what he remembers he assembled afterward out of four seconds and four years of wanting.

Marisol stops asking about it in December. In January she tells him she has a start date in Boise and he says that's great and means it and neither of them mentions the desert again.

By the first week of March he has more or less filed it. Not disbelieved — he is not capable of disbelieving it — but shelved, in the category of things that happened to him that he will never be able to demonstrate, alongside the letter with *containment* in it four times.

---

The house at 411 has a notice on the door and an estate sale scheduled for the eleventh.

Ethan cuts through the side yard on the ninth because it is the short way to the alley and because he has cut through that yard since he was seven and Dolores never minded. The kitchen window is at shoulder height. The house has been empty for five months and there is nothing in it and he is not looking at it.

It says his name.

It says it from inside the wall, at about the height of his hip, in a voice that is not loud and is not a whisper and is not any register a human throat produces, and it says it the way Dolores Ackerman said it — the flat Nevada vowels, the small rise at the end that she put on the names of children she had taught, a construction that has been in her mouth since 1998 and has not existed anywhere in the world for five months.

Ethan Walker is eighteen years old and has spent four years walking into the desert on the specific hope that something impossible would look back at him.

He does not run.

That is the decision the entire rest of it comes out of, and it takes him under a second, and nobody could have made it differently with what he had. He has been waiting four years. He has a brother who is dead in a letter that will not say how. He has a thing in the desert that reached for him and failed and that he has never been able to prove, and now there is a voice in a wall that knows his name.

Every piece of evidence available to him says the same thing. It is hurt. It is hiding. It is alone. It has been trying to reach him since September.

Three of those four are correct.

# PART FOUR — WHAT THE WORLD DOES ABOUT IT

---

## XXI. The Auditors

The government does not know what it is looking for, and it will not know for four years.

What it knows is that a thirty-one-year appropriation lost its entire physical plant and one hundred percent of its on-site personnel in a single night, that the sublevels were sterilized to bedrock on a contingency plan written for a threat that behaved nothing like what actually happened, and that the surface installation is undamaged in a way that nobody can construct a mechanism for.

The first people to go looking are not soldiers. They are auditors — a joint accountability team out of a records facility in Colorado, arriving eleven weeks after the event, believing they are investigating an industrial accident with a very expensive write-off attached and a directorial negligence question at the end of it.

They interview Helen Osei for nine years. She cooperates completely. She hands over everything. She is not concealing anything and by year four she is the single most useful person in the entire investigation, because she is the only one alive who can explain what the building was supposed to do, and the difference between what it was supposed to do and what it did is the whole case.

It is Osei who first says the sentence, in year three, in a deposition, in a tone that the transcript does not capture: *the doors did not fail. The doors opened.*

Protocol Zero is sitting intact the entire time, in the mirrored logs from the uplink shed, in a subdirectory of automated system events, in a file forty megabytes long that a records facility in Colorado has been storing at a cost of about eleven dollars a year for three decades. It is read as a malfunction in year one, as an anomalous cascade in year two, and as a decision in year three, by a junior analyst who then spends her winter reading everything the project ever produced and gets to page four of the Ω Report in the following spring.

Four years is a long time. It is also, as it turns out, not the disaster it sounds like, because for all four of those years the organism is at Order Zero and is not doing anything that generates a signal. It is in a house, and then it is in a series of empty places in a debris corridor four hundred miles long, and it is not spreading, not attacking, and not visible, and the sum total of the evidence it produces is a series of missing persons cases distributed across three states with nothing in common except that none of them has a body.

Nobody assembles those. There is no reason to assemble those. In a country of that size, in a corridor with that much unpoliced ground, the annual number of people who disappear without remains is large enough that a pattern would have to be much stronger than this one to be visible.

The pattern becomes visible eleven weeks after page four, and what makes it visible is not a human being.

By then the analyst in Colorado has the number. She has the coherence table, she has the seventeen, and she has spent five weeks trying to get a meeting with somebody who can act on the fact that the most strategically significant resource on the planet is a population of registered aliens that three governments currently think of as a residency caseload.

She gets the meeting. It is scheduled for a Thursday.

On the Tuesday, in a dry lake bed in eastern Nevada, an Autobot with a state contract and a liaison officer he sees twice a year goes out to recover his own dead.

---

## XXII. The Residents

There are thirty-eight registered Cybertronians on Earth at the time of the escape.

Nineteen are in the Cuban asylum zone, which is a treaty artifact from a period when Cuba was the only nation on the planet that had declined to hunt them, and which has since become a functioning district of about eleven square kilometers with its own arrangements and a permanent human population of four thousand who mostly work in adjacent services and have extremely strong opinions about how the rest of the world discusses their neighbors.

Eleven are residents under individual arrangement, distributed across five countries, monitored to a degree that varies enormously by jurisdiction and by how much anybody remembers about the individual's history.

Six are in custody at the N.B.E. Supermax outside Fort Collins, Colorado, which was built for a different era and which currently houses fewer prisoners than it has staff. Four of the six are Decepticons who declined the general amnesty. Two are there for things they did after it.

Two are unaccounted for and have been for a decade, and the working assumption is that they are in the debris corridor, which is where anybody who does not want to be registered goes.

The orbital population is not counted in the thirty-eight. It fluctuates, it does not answer to any human authority, and the treaty framework governing it consists of a single protocol about approach vectors that has been honored without incident for thirty-one years by both parties.

Arrivals continue. The transmission that went out at the end of the war is still traveling, and the volume of space it has crossed keeps increasing, and every few years something comes into the system that has been in transit for decades. Most of them go straight up. Three have come down. One of those three was the Salt Lake Descent.

The individual who walked out of the Salt Lake debris field on the fourth day carrying two others chose a name in English within his first year, on the grounds that nobody in Utah was going to manage the one he had, and answered to it for the rest of his life.

He picked it off the side of a building in Salt Lake City that was still standing.

---

## XXIII. Steeple

He lives in the corridor. He has a residency arrangement, a registry number, a liaison officer he sees twice a year, and a small permanent job that the state of Nevada does not advertise and that the RCS could not do without him: he finds his own dead.

Sixteen years after the descent there are still sections of that ship in places no survey has reached, and some of them contain individuals, and the difference between a hull section and an individual is not something a human contractor with a day rate and a thermal camera can reliably determine. It is something Steeple can determine from four hundred meters, and he does not explain how, and after the third time he was asked he stopped being asked.

The RCS calls it recovery. He has never called it that.

Ethan Walker has seen him twice from a distance and once from about two hundred yards, in the summer he was sixteen, standing on a ridge above a wash while something enormous knelt in the bottom of it and did not move for four hours. He did not go down. He has thought about that afternoon more than about almost anything else in his life.

Steeple has never met Ethan Walker and never will.

---

He is taken in the fourth year, in a wash eleven miles from where the Charger was parked on the last Saturday in September, in the middle of a recovery he requested and filed and had approved.

The circumstances are not complicated and there is no story in them. Organicron by then is still at Order Zero — four years old, eleven hundred kilograms at maximum, and spread across about a hundred and forty meters of a dry lake bed in a layer nowhere thicker than a coat of paint, in a configuration that reads to every instrument the RCS owns as sediment. It has been there for nine days. It has been there because there is a wreck in that lake bed, and it has been converting the wreck at the maximum rate its ceiling permits, and Steeple came to the lake bed for the same reason.

The procedure is beyond its efficiency. It takes forty minutes and it is nothing anybody should have to describe.

The gain is permanent. It goes to Order One that night. Order One is the threshold the Ω Report identifies as sustained intent, and what that means in practice is that for the first time in its existence, Organicron wakes up the following morning still holding the thing it was doing the previous evening, and does not have to rediscover it.

It is now, in the only sense that matters, an animal that can want.

---

Two things happen in the following seventy-two hours and they happen to different species.

The first is that every Cybertronian in existence feels a spark fail to arrive.

There is no ambiguity about it and no delay. It is not an inference from silence. It is the specific perceptual signature of an event that their entire cosmology holds to be structurally impossible, and it is registered simultaneously, at every distance, by individuals who have no idea what has happened or where, only that something has been removed from the universe in a manner that has never occurred in four million years of record.

The orbital population comes down eleven hours later without filing an approach vector. It is the first violation of that protocol in thirty-one years and there is no diplomatic response to it, because there is no diplomatic apparatus on Earth capable of processing what is being said to it.

The second is that a voice starts.

Somewhere in a hundred and forty meters of dry lake bed there is corrupted playback, and it is asking for help, and it is using the names of two individuals who are alive in the Cuban asylum zone and one who is alive in orbit, and it is doing this on a frequency the recovery teams monitor, and it does not stop for nineteen days.

The recording exists. Six people have heard the whole thing. The working consensus among all of them is that there is nobody in there.

The consensus has never survived contact with the recording, and one of the six resigned.

---

## XXIV. Quarantine

The Ω Report gives seventeen sparks between Order Zero and Order Nine.

The organism has one. There are thirty-seven left registered on Earth, plus the orbital population, plus whatever is still inbound out of a transmission that is nineteen light-years further out than it was the day it was sent, plus two individuals in the debris corridor who have not registered for a decade and have no idea what is in the corridor with them.

That is the arithmetic, and it is the reason the alliance exists, and it is the reason the alliance is not about Earth.

Humans lose eight billion bodies and every structure on the planet, and none of it raises the ceiling by a fraction of an order. Human beings are mass. Cities are mass. The entire species is, in the specific technical sense that the coherence model requires, ballast — the largest and richest supply of fuel in the local group and worth precisely nothing toward the thing that actually matters.

Ethan Walker worked that out about himself at fourteen, and he was right, and it has never once occurred to him that being right about it would one day be the only reason his species was allowed to keep breathing.

---

The agreement has three parties and it took eleven weeks and it is not an alliance in any sense that would satisfy anyone who has ever written about the concept. It is a quarantine protocol between two war machines and a species of scenery, and it contains no shared values, no reconciliation, and no expressed goodwill in either direction.

Its first operational objective is not to kill Organicron.

Nobody knows how to kill Organicron. Sixteen thousand modeled runs say the only reliable method is the removal of all matter in the universe, and while page four is now understood and everyone involved knows the report was answering a question about an unbounded organism rather than this one, nobody has yet produced a method for the bounded case either. There are proposals. There are five of them and three are being resourced.

The objective is simpler than killing it, and it is the only thing any of the three parties can actually do:

**Keep it at one.**

Every clause in the protocol comes out of that number. The Cuban zone is closed and the residents are relocated to orbit over a period of four months, against the strenuous objection of nineteen individuals who have lived on that island for three decades and who are, correctly, being told that their continued presence on the surface is a strategic liability measured in orders. The Fort Collins facility is emptied. The two unregistered individuals in the corridor are the subject of a search operation that has been running for seven years and has not found them and is the single largest line item in the entire effort.

Recovery in the debris corridor stops. All of it, permanently, on both sides. There are individuals in that ground who have been there since the descent and they will stay there, and this clause was written by Cybertronians and there is not a human being alive who could have proposed it.

And the arrivals — the ones still inbound, still asleep, still crossing thirty light-years toward a transmission that told them the war was over and the homeworld existed and it was time to come home — are being met in the outer system by a standing picket that has been maintained continuously for seven years, and turned around, and sent back out.

Some of them have been in transit for eighty years.

---

There is a fourth clause and it is the one that ended the negotiation, because there was nothing left to say afterward.

Any Cybertronian disabled inside a contact zone is not recovered.

Not evacuated, not retrieved, not treated. The protocol specifies the alternative in eleven words of extremely careful language and then specifies which party is responsible for carrying it out, and the party is not the humans, because the humans asked not to be and were told, in the only moment of the entire negotiation that anyone present has described as emotional, that they had not been under consideration.

The Decepticon delegation accepted that clause in under a minute. The doctrine they had issued eleven days after the first spark contains a nearly identical provision and contains it in stronger terms, and the reason is not courage and is not sacrifice. It is that a faction organized for four million years around the primacy of the Cybertronian form and the sanctity of the spark as the source of all legitimate authority has looked at what is in that corridor and understood, faster and more clearly than anyone else on the planet, exactly what it is.

Not an enemy. Enemies are survivable. Enemies can win.

A thing that eats gods, does not notice, and gets slightly better at it every time.

---

The Autobot delegation took nine days on the same clause.

They took nine days because the Autobot position had to survive contact with a fact that the Decepticons found merely confirming and that the Autobots have never found a way to hold: that the organism in the corridor was designed, funded, built, and released by the species they have spent four million years, two homeworlds, and most of their dead protecting — and that it was built specifically because humanity had concluded it could never be safe while anything more powerful than itself existed, which is a description, on close reading, of the Autobots.

They signed. They have honored every clause. Two of them have carried out the fourth one, personally, on individuals they knew.

And there is a subclause, added on the eighth day at their insistence and over the objection of both other parties, which requires that the location of every individual left in the debris corridor under the recovery clause be recorded, maintained, and held.

Not for retrieval. Retrieval is prohibited. The subclause does not say what the record is for and the Autobot delegation declined to explain it, and the humans in the room assumed sentiment and let it go.

It is not sentiment. It is a target list, held against the day the quarantine fails, when somebody will have to reach the rest of them before the organism does and will not have time to go looking.

---

None of this is in place in the second week of March, in the fourth month of the fourth year before it will be needed, in a side yard in Ely, Nevada.

There is a boy at a kitchen window who has just been called by name.

There is a thing in the wall at about the height of his hip that has language it took from a schoolteacher, a nose it took from a dog, a housekeeper's reflex it does not know it is running, and eleven hundred kilograms of ceiling that nothing on this planet is going to raise for another three and a half years.

It has no plan. It cannot want anything yet, and will not be able to for three and a half more years, and it has never once in its existence been looked at by something that did not immediately try to contain it or run.

And the last permanent thing it acquired, before the boy said *hello*, was two years of a stray dog's life on that street, worn down to a single instruction with nothing underneath it:

Stay close to the source of good things. Stay near it.

Do not leave.
