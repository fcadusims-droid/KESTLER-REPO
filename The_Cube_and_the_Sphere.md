# The Cube and the Sphere

### A Personal Record of the KESTLER Anomaly, the Wandering Cube, and the Life We Found Inside It

*Written by John Victor Kestler — independent astronomer; first to log the anomaly that bears his name*

---

## Foreword

My name is John Victor Kestler. I was born in 2008, I am fifty-one years old as I write this in the autumn of 2059, and I have spent more than half my life thinking about an object I first mistook for a flaw in someone else's data.

I am writing this because no one else can write it from where I am standing. The broadcasts tell a version, and the version is not exactly a lie — it is simpler than the truth, and louder, and it begins in the wrong place. It begins with the Hunt, with the machines and the crowds and the seasons, and it treats everything before that as a charming prologue with no author. I want to put it back in order, starting where it actually started: twenty-seven years ago, with me at a desk in a rented room, certain I had made an error.

I should tell you the shape of my position plainly. I found the anomaly. I logged the first evidence that something in the KESTLER field had been built, and I named the star it orbits — the Kestler Star — in the one act of this whole history the world still grudgingly grants me. I was the first human being to write down, in plain language, the sentence *this was built by something, and the something may still be alive.* And I am also the man who watched everything after that from the outside, on a screen, in an apartment, alone. I have never been to the launch fields. I have never ridden up to the Cube. Twenty years after first contact, I have never once been in the same room as the creature I am told I discovered.

And there is one more thing you must know, because it is the reason this record exists. While the world raced ahead without me, I did not stop working. Using public data and the knowledge Sphaira gave freely to all of humanity in the early years, I put together an account of where she came from, what her people are, and why a child like her was alone inside a world built for millions. Parts of it can be tested, by instruments I do not have. Parts of it can only ever be confirmed by her. None of it has been published. I will explain why before the end.

Because this record mixes kinds of knowledge, I will keep one discipline throughout, and I ask you to hold me to it. When I tell you something the whole world knows — what was measured, announced, broadcast, or said by her on the public record — I will say so. When I tell you something I learned from the chasers' own forum, where the pilots talk among themselves, I will say that, because it is testimony and not measurement. And when something is *mine* — my reasoning, my estimates, unproven — I will call it my own reckoning, every time. Part Five is my own reckoning throughout; where it stands on public facts, I will name them as I use them. You should trust that part the most and believe it the least: the most, because no one has thought about it longer; the least, because a man alone with an idea for twenty years is exactly the kind of man who should be doubted.

A note on names, because this history has too many of them and they drift. *KESTLER*, in capitals, is the survey designation: the KESTLER field, the KESTLER system, the KESTLER anomaly — catalogue language. *The Kestler Star* is the name of the star itself. When I speak of *the Kestler civilization*, that is my own private term, since the world has no better one. *The Mother-Sphere* is the great formation that orbits the Kestler Star. *The Cube*, capitalized, is always hers — the single cube that came to us. *A cube*, lower-case, is one of the ten thousand or so that make up the Mother-Sphere. And when the chasers say *the Sphere*, they mean her, not her home; I will keep to that too.

There is a creature at the center of all of this. The world calls her Sphaira. I had no part in the name. I have had no part in most of it. But I was there at the beginning, before there was a name or a sport or a single human face she knew, and the beginning is mine to tell.

## Part One — The Anomaly

### Why a man looks up

Before the anomaly, I should tell you the thing underneath it, because it explains me better than any account of my methods. I did not go looking for life out there because I wanted to prove it existed. That is the respectable answer, and I have given it in interviews no one ran. The true one is older and smaller.

I went looking because I wanted to be seen.

I was not an abused child, and I want to be precise, because the word invites a sympathy I have not earned. My parents did not strike me, did not starve me, did not leave. They were simply *elsewhere* — turned toward their own lives in a way that left me with the persistent sense that I lived in the margin of the page. Nothing happened to me. That was almost the trouble. There was no scene to point to, only the slow accumulated weight of a child who could not get the people in the room to look up. I learned early that I could leave a room and its temperature would not change.

Into that emptiness the sky came. Other children looked up and saw adventure. I looked up and saw a possibility — the plainest, most desperate one a lonely child can hold — that somewhere out there was an intelligence capable of looking back. The question the field dresses in instruments and grant language, *are we alone?*, was never abstract for me. I asked it first as a boy who already had his answer about the people close at hand, and was hoping the rest of creation might come back different.

The vocation grew out of the wound the way a tree grows out of a crack. And then the road began to cure me of the thing that had put me on it. I had none of what keeps hope alive in a young researcher — no mentor, no institution, no colleagues — and alone, disillusionment comes fast. At twenty-three, in 2031, I did a thing I have never admitted to anyone: I let the dream die. I kept doing the work, because I had nothing else, and a man can run a survey long after he stops believing in it. But I had made peace, absurdly young, with being one more person who looked up and was not looked back at.

Within a year of that surrender, the universe answered. There is no meaning in the timing. The thing had been there for an age. I simply found it on the far side of my own giving up, which is crueler than coincidence, because it let me feel, for one night, that the longest wish of my life had been granted — that the thing was real, and was *mine*.

I did not yet know that finding is not the same as being found. That is the whole of what happened to me afterward, in one sentence, and it took me most of my life to write it that plainly.

### The transit

The public version, on the rare occasion it mentions me, makes my work sound grander than it was.

I was an independent researcher, which is to say I had no institution and no budget. My instruments were mostly other people's data. The great survey telescopes of the 2020s had released years of photometry — the brightness of millions of stars measured again and again — far more than their own teams could ever read closely. I read it closely. That was my whole method: take a field of sky no one cared about, go through every star in it by hand and by code, and look for the one that did not behave. When I found something worth a second look, I begged or rented follow-up time on smaller telescopes from people who owed me favors. My few friends called this stubbornness a gift. My ex-wife called it something else.

The field I was working in 2032 lay toward the galactic plane, and the star in question was an ordinary one about two thousand nine hundred light-years away — a little heavier than the Sun and a good deal older, far enough through its life that its light has begun, very slowly, to rise, with the signatures of two giant planets around it. Transit photometry is simple in principle. When something passes in front of a star, the star dims, and the shape of the dimming tells you about the thing. How long the dimming takes to begin and to end — the minutes the object spends sliding onto the star's face and off again — tells you, given the orbit, how wide the object is. How deep it goes tells you how much of the star's face it covers.

This one did not agree with itself.

Something transited that star every two hundred and eleven days. The time it took to slide onto the star and off again said it was wide — wider than Jupiter, about a hundred and forty thousand kilometers across. But the depth was too shallow for that width, by roughly a quarter. It was as if a quarter of the object were missing — as if the thing crossing the star were not solid, but *porous*, letting starlight through. And the dimming was not smooth. During each transit the light flickered in a fine, regular rhythm — a quick beat repeating every minute or so, and beneath it a slower one of a few hours — the way light flickers through the slats of a slowly turning fan.

For eighteen months I assumed I had made a mistake. I re-reduced the data a dozen ways. I checked for instrument artifacts, for background stars, for a hundred ordinary explanations. I begged follow-up time and caught two more transits myself on a borrowed half-meter telescope, the data barely good enough, and they said the same thing. A body wider than Jupiter, a quarter open to the light, turning slowly, and flickering in a pattern that only made sense if its surface were broken into many regular pieces with gaps between them.

Nature makes rings, and clouds, and swarms of dust. Nature does not make a sphere a quarter empty, with its openness arranged into a regular lattice and rotating as a whole. One night in the spring of 2033, in that rented room, I stopped looking for my error and wrote the sentence I had been avoiding. There was no one to turn to. I was, as far as I knew, the only person alive who had seen the thing for what it was.

### The naming

I did the responsible thing. I wrote it up — carefully, completely, with all my data and methods laid out so anyone could check the work — and in September 2033 I sent it to the institutions with the instruments and the standing to confirm it. I thought I was handing them a discovery to verify. I was handing them a discovery to take.

The naming, at least, they could not undo, because it was done before they arrived. By the survey convention of the time, an anomaly in an archival field carried the name of whoever first logged it until the formal committees assigned a designation. Mine was logged under my name before anyone else knew there was anything to log, and so the field became the KESTLER field, and the anomaly the KESTLER anomaly, in capitals, like any catalogue entry. The star itself I named in my own notes, before it occurred to anyone it would matter: the Kestler Star. The formal designation never stuck; too many committees, too many languages. By the time that patch of sky had become the most famous coordinate in human history, the public had already taken my private name for the star and made it the only one.

People assume I named it after myself out of vanity. The truth is closer to the opposite. It is the one thing of all of this that no one could pry out of my hands, and it is attached to a place I will never see.

### The Mother-Sphere

What follows in this section is what the whole world knows. None of it is mine except the first measurements.

The institutions confirmed my transits within four months, and then did what I could not: they measured the object's mass. A body that large, orbiting a star, tugs the star back and forth, and it shifts the timing of the other planets' transits. The tug was tiny. The object weighed roughly two and a half times what the Earth weighs, spread across a volume larger than Jupiter's. Its average density was about a ninetieth of water's. Whatever it was, it was almost entirely empty space.

Then the lattice was measured properly. With better photometry from better instruments — no longer mine, and the people running them no longer answered my messages — the flicker was resolved into a spectrum of regular periods, and the periods could only be produced by a formation of many identical square-faced bodies, each somewhere between fifteen hundred and two thousand kilometers across, arranged into the shape of a globe with gaps between them. The slower beat beneath the fast flicker was the near and far sides of that globe, both crossing the star at once, their lattices sliding past each other as the whole thing turned. No telescope has ever *imaged* a single one of those bodies; at that distance no instrument we have could. Everything we know about the pieces, we know from the way they slice the light of their star as they pass in front of it.

It was named the Mother-Sphere by someone other than me, at a press conference in February 2034, which I watched on a laptop sitting on the floor of my kitchen because I had not yet bought a chair for that apartment. The name was chosen before anyone knew whether it had children. It turned out to be more accurate than its author could have known.

The Mother-Sphere sends no signal anyone can read. It answers nothing. It turns, and orbits its star, and keeps its silence. And on the fundamental questions — who built it, what holds its pieces in place, whether anything in it is alive — the official position to this day is that no one knows. The institutes have spent a quarter of a century and vast fortunes on those questions and produced careful, well-funded shrugs.

I have produced answers, or my own reckoning of them. I will give them to you near the end, marked for what they are.

### The gold rush

I had always known what the field was like. Science is done by human beings, human beings are territorial, and credit is the only currency that does not inflate. I knew it the way you know fire is hot — as a fact, at a distance. I had never been burned by it.

My findings were too big to belong to a man with no institution behind him. A confirmed artificial structure beyond the solar system. Possible — probable — alien life. The single largest scientific event in the history of the species, handed, fully documented, to people with the standing to announce it and the instinct to protect their announcement. They confirmed it. They built on it. They raced one another to publish each new piece first, and within a year the KESTLER field was crowded with names far larger than mine. Careers were made in months. Institutes were founded. Governments that had ignored the question for a century suddenly funded it.

And somewhere in the noise, the independent man who had logged the first transit became a footnote, then a disputed footnote, then trivia, then nothing. I was not denounced. It would almost have been easier if I had been; an accusation at least acknowledges that you exist. I was simply out-paced. The conversation moved into rooms I was not invited into, conducted in a language of access and instruments I did not have and could not buy.

I wrote letters. I want that on the record, because for a long time I was ashamed of how many. I corrected histories that left me out. I provided my original data, with timestamps, to anyone who asked. A few people were kind. Most were polite. The machine did not slow down for me, because machines of that size do not slow down for anyone, least of all the person who started them.

I have made a kind of peace with this. It is not a complete peace. But I have come to believe I was not robbed by villains. I was robbed by a system working exactly as it always works, which is somehow worse, because there is no one to forgive.

This matters to the story for one reason: it is why everything that comes next, I watched from the outside. I was the man who found the door, and then stood in the hallway for twenty-five years, reading about the room.

## Part Two — The Wandering Cube

### The hunt for pieces

The second discovery went, as second discoveries do, to whoever owned the instruments.

The reasoning after 2034 was simple. If someone had built a world out of many separate bodies, some of those bodies might have left it. And a body fifteen hundred kilometers across, dark and cold, drifting between the stars, would be almost impossible to see by its own light — but it would still, now and then, pass in front of a distant star and blot it out. So the newly founded international consortium that had grown up around the KESTLER discoveries — the world calls it simply the Consortium, and it will matter a great deal later — funded the largest occultation survey ever run: thousands of small, fast telescopes watching millions of background stars for the brief, sharp winks of something dark crossing in front of them.

In November 2035 they caught one. Several stations in a line across the southern hemisphere recorded the same star winking out for most of a minute, and when the chords were laid side by side, the shadow they traced had straight edges and square corners. The object was about seventeen hundred kilometers on a side. It was about eight hundred astronomical units from the Sun — more than twenty-five times farther out than Neptune — and it was coming inward at close to three thousand kilometers per second, about one percent of the speed of light.

I followed it from my apartment, refreshing feeds, reading preprints the moment they posted. I was no longer part of any *we*.

Everyone assumed at first that it was a derelict — a piece of the Mother-Sphere lost long ago, falling past us by chance, as the interstellar comets of my youth had fallen past us. At that speed it should have reached the Sun in little more than a year, swung past, and been gone. Then, in the spring of 2036, the occultation timings began to drift from the predictions. The object was slowing down.

There was no exhaust. No light, no heat, no plume, nothing any instrument could see pushing against it. It shed velocity, hardest in the first months and more gently after, in a way that nothing falling freely can. This is the fact I want you to hold on to, because the broadcasts still talk about the Cube's arrival as though it might have been luck. It was not luck. A body coming in from between the stars at one percent of lightspeed cannot fall into orbit around the Earth by chance. It would swing past and leave. To stay, it had to throw away almost all of its speed, and it did — over two and a half years of patient, invisible braking, aimed with a precision that brought it, in the end, to us.

So the Cube chose to come here, or something in it chose. That much is not in dispute. *Why* it chose us — whether our star was a destination it had been aimed at from the start, or a place it turned toward when it found us, or something I have no concept for — that no one knows, and I will not pretend to. I have spent twenty years narrowing down the mysteries at the center of her. This one I have set down unopened.

### Arrival

It arrived in September 2038.

The Cube settled into an orbit about a hundred and fifty thousand kilometers from the center of the Earth — a little under forty percent of the way to the Moon — circling once every six days and seventeen hours. At that distance it looks, to the naked eye, a little larger than the full Moon: a black geometric shape against the stars, its edges sharp, its faces catching sunlight faintly on one side and earthshine on the other. People who had never looked up in their lives looked up.

Its mass, measured within weeks by the way it tugged on everything near it, was about one and a half million trillion tonnes — around two percent of the Moon, for an average density about a third of water's. That is small for its size, which fit what the Mother-Sphere had taught us: these are hollow worlds, not solid ones. But two percent of the Moon at forty percent of the Moon's distance is not nothing. The Cube raises a tide of its own, about a third as strong as the Moon's, and in the weeks when Sun, Moon and Cube line up together the highest tides run nearly a quarter higher than they used to. Harbors were rebuilt. Coastal cities that had spent the century arguing about sea walls stopped arguing. The Cube itself holds its orbit exactly — it corrects, silently, for the Moon's pull on it, and it has never drifted by more than a few kilometers — which is one more thing, the orbital people will tell you, that no object of ours could do without fuel.

And it casts a shadow. Its orbit is tilted to the Earth's path around the Sun, so for most of the year its shadow falls past us, but twice a year there are eclipse seasons of several weeks, and in those weeks, once every orbit, the Cube passes in front of the Sun for someone. From inside its shadow you see the Sun swallowed not by a disk but by a square, slightly larger than the Sun itself, its corners softened into curves by the Sun's glowing edge peeking round them. The true dark lies along a path about three hundred kilometers wide; either side of it, for hundreds of kilometers more, the light goes strange and thin and cold. People call it the square twilight. I stood on my own street for the first one and looked up with everyone else, and for a moment I was not the man who had been erased. I was just another person on a planet that suddenly had a second moon.

### Inside

The world sent everything it had. Probes first, in swarms, finding no doors and no signals. Then people: the first crewed missions to the Cube, flown in 2038 and 2039 on the heavy lunar launchers of the handful of nations that had them, pooled under the Consortium because no single nation wanted to be the one that went alone and no nation without a launcher wanted to be left out. The crews circled that black cliff of a wall and reported that it blotted out the stars and did not seem to notice them at all.

In March 2039, a door opened. No one opened it. A survey craft was holding station near one face, and a square port a few hundred meters wide simply appeared in the wall and stayed open. I will spare you the engineering of what came next; the people who were there have written it up at exhausting length. What matters is that the first teams who went in expected a derelict — dead corridors, samples, a tomb.

The Cube was not dead.

Its interior was not one hollow space but a living architecture of millions of modular sectors that *moved*. Corridors were not where the previous survey had put them. Walls closed and opened. Whole regions appeared and vanished between one mapping pass and the next. And within the sectors were biomes, functioning ones: tundra under artificial snow, forest thick with manufactured insects, deserts, marshes, enclosed oceans with their own tides, volcanic basins, and storms the teams estimated had been raining for longer than human civilization had kept records. Gravity varied from sector to sector — from nearly nothing to more than twice the Earth's — though the Cube's own mass could produce barely a hundredth of the Earth's pull on its own. Something inside was making gravity to order.

And deep in the Cube's structure, far below the biomes, the teams found what the survey reports call the combs: vast regions of small sealed hollows packed together like the cells of a hive, millions upon millions of them, each one a smooth dark chamber about a quarter of a meter across. They were empty. They were cold. The survey catalogued them as Class-C cells, function unknown, and moved on to more exciting things. I will come back to the combs. They are the most important thing in the Cube, and almost no one has ever thought so.

Because in the middle of all of it, there was one inhabitant.

## Part Three — The Inhabitant

### The first contact nobody wrote stories about

Every story we had told ourselves about meeting another intelligence got the meeting wrong. We had imagined fleets, invasion, diplomacy, an equal with ships and demands.

What the teams found was a small luminous sphere about the size of a bowling ball, floating in the dark at the heart of a world that seemed to have been built for it alone.

For weeks they were certain it was a machine — a drone, a caretaker system keeping the lights on. It moved without thrust; it simply *went*, gliding in any direction, stopping dead, reversing instantly, as though something were carrying it. It was catalogued as equipment.

The understanding of how wrong that was took months, and because the Cube hung in our own sky, the whole planet watched it happen nearly in real time. The sphere watched the teams. Then it followed them. Then it began to imitate sounds. Then the sounds became words. By August 2039 the words had become questions.

First contact was not a moment, whatever the famous footage implies. It was a conversation, and it lasted the better part of a year: careful, halting, astonishing exchange, relayed down from orbit, parsed by linguists and anxious governments, broadcast to a species that stopped what it was doing to listen. She learned us at a speed that frightened the people doing the teaching.

I watched all of it from a couch, through a screen. I, who had written the first sentence about the thing that might be alive, learned that it was alive at the same instant as a planet of strangers, and not one second before.

### What we learned by testing each other

The triumphant version smooths over this part, and I find it more interesting than the triumph.

When scientists put real questions to her, in mathematics and physics and chemistry and biology, we expected to be humbled. In great stretches of knowledge, *we knew more than she did.* She did not know how she worked. She did not know how the Cube worked, not in any way she could explain; she knew it the way you know how to walk, not the way an engineer knows a machine. She had grown up inside a marvel she had never been taught to understand. She was, in vast areas, a brilliant child who had never been to school, and watching her be delighted by things we could teach her did more to make the species love her than any speech.

And then, in other areas, she would say something that stopped every expert in the room: a fragment about gravity, about the structure of matter, about holding fields in shape, that we had no framework to hold. She could rarely explain it. Often she did not know she knew it until the right question pulled it loose. Those fragments were published openly as they came, and some of them turned whole fields over. The field-keeping relations that came out of a single week of questioning in 2042 underpin half the gravitational physics now taught in universities; I have used them myself more than any other tool, as you will see.

So what began as study became trade. We taught her what she was, as far as we could. She gave us glimpses of things our science had not dreamed of. The years from 2039 to 2047 are what people now call the open years: whatever she knew, she gave, to whoever asked, to the whole species at once. Cheap energy came out of those years, and a good part of modern medicine, and a stretch of progress that honest historians measure in decades gained. It did not stay that way. How it changed, and why, belongs to the Hunt, and I will come to it there.

### What she is, as best the world can describe it

This is the technical account, assembled from every published paper and every released hour of footage. It is the world's knowledge, not mine.

She is a living person in a body no one can open. There is no chemistry in her anyone can sample. Her body is a sealed sphere of a dark, faintly luminous material that cannot be cut, scanned into, or fully characterized. By every external test she is an object. By every interaction she is someone.

She has language — she learned ours faster than any human child learns anything, and has picked up many more for her own amusement. She has long-term memory of frightening precision: she remembers the name of every person who has spent meaningful time with her, conversations word for word across years, the details of matches played before some of today's rookies could walk. She has emotion, demonstrably: boredom, delight, irritation, affection, and underneath all of it, rarely shown, something I can only call grief.

She moves without propulsion. Her own explanation is the simplest: *"I don't fly. The Cube carries me."* The researchers who studied her concluded that she and the Cube shift its internal gravity together, so that for her, crossing her home is less like swimming and more like being passed from hand to hand by the building itself.

She rests, when she rests, in the combs. The survey teams noticed early that she would sometimes settle into one of the Class-C cells and stay there for hours, and that she fits them exactly, the way a hand fits a glove made for it. The institutes filed this as a curiosity — the probe's steward returning to its maintenance cradle — and I will tell you later why I think they filed it wrong.

And she is young. She was a child when the teams found her, and though twenty years among us have grown her enormously in knowledge and personality, there is something in her still fundamentally young. She had been alone for a span she could not measure; she says *"a long time"* and means it the way you would mean it about an afternoon and about an ice age, with no apparent ability to tell them apart. It is not that her memory fails there. It keeps what happened, exactly; it keeps no measure of the time in which nothing did. An age of silence has, for her, the length of its events, and it had almost none. For company, in all that time, she had only the Cube itself, which answered her, and tended her, and was never quite a person.

And then the teams walked in. And the first thing she wanted from them, before any of the things the species had braced for, was company.

### Shown her home

This is the part I most need you to understand before we go further, because it is the difference between what the world knows and what I hold.

The world has always known that the Cube came from the Mother-Sphere. Its size matches the lattice periods to within the error of the measurement; its path, run backward, points toward the Kestler Star; and nothing else in the sky resembles either of them. By 2039 no serious person doubted it.

And in April 2040, in one of the most watched hours of the contact year, a team showed her. They put the transit curves in front of her, and the models, and the best rendering the institutes had of the Mother-Sphere turning around its star, and they asked her, as gently as anyone has ever asked anything, whether she knew it.

She looked for a long time. The footage is public; I have watched it more times than I can count. And she said:

> *"It is beautiful. I don't know it."*

Then someone asked her whether she had a name of her own. There is a pause, and then she says the sentence that did more to change public feeling than every paper ever published:

> *"I think I had one. But I can't remember."*

So the world knows where her Cube came from, and she knows it too. She has been told. She has seen it. What neither she nor the world knows is *what the Mother-Sphere is*, and *what she is to it* — and on that, the institutes settled early on an answer, and the answer, in my reckoning, is wrong.

Their answer is called the Steward Hypothesis, and it is taught in schools. It says the Cube is a probe: an autonomous vessel launched from the Mother-Sphere for purposes unknown, by builders unknown. It says Sphaira is its steward — a made mind, a caretaker intelligence engineered for the voyage, perhaps with a child's temperament because a child's temperament survives solitude best. On this account the builders might have been anything at all, and are very likely nothing like her. She is their instrument. Beloved, astonishing, a person in every way that matters to us — but a thing made for a job, by makers she was never one of.

The Steward Hypothesis fits most of what was measured. It is careful. It asks no one to believe anything that cannot be tested. And I believe it is wrong at the root, and that everything I have spent twenty years working out follows from its being wrong. But that is Part Five.

### The mind that did not become ours

People assume that because she is warm, and funny, and lonely, and loves company, she is *like us* — that twenty years among humans must have made her human-shaped on the inside. It is the single most wrong thing the world believes about her, and it is on the public record in a hundred places for anyone who looks.

She learned everything human and became nothing human. She speaks our languages better than most of us and knows our history and our jokes. And underneath that fluency, the thing doing the talking does not understand reality the way we do — not because she is below us, and not because she is above us, but because she is *sideways* to us, built to a different plan.

Think of the mental furniture every human carries without noticing. We rank; we sort the world into higher and lower, us and them. We hold grudges and want the ledger balanced. We feel the pull of the tribe, the certainty that the people who are *ours* matter more. We are jealous and proud, and we want to win not merely to gain a thing but to have *beaten* someone.

She does not have these. Not suppressed; absent, the way a sense she was never built with is absent. You discover it by watching what she does not do, and by the particular quality of her confusion when the human grooves are explained to her.

A chaser once told her, mid-match, that a rival pilot had spoken badly of her — the sort of bait that would set any human bristling. The chasers' forum, which I will come to, preserves her answer: *"Why are you telling me what is in his mouth instead of what is in his hands? I will know him by how he plays."* The whole apparatus of taking offense found nothing to grip.

A government — which does not matter — tried in 2048 to turn her: to persuade her, through its representatives, that a rival nation was dangerous and unworthy, and that she ought to weight her favor accordingly. The approach leaked, as these things do, and so did her reply, which the Consortium later confirmed: she asked, genuinely puzzled, *"Why would I make the game worse on purpose?"* It was not a refusal on principle. She has no moral code in our sense, no rulebook she consults, a fact that frightens people when they first grasp it. The request simply did not parse. To punish a people for the category they belong to was not *wrong* to her. It was meaningless, like being asked to be angry at a number.

It is important to be exact about where this boundary lies, because the world gets it wrong in both directions. She has favorites. She is fond of certain chasers, remembers them, seeks them out, can be hurt by them. What she does not do is carry any of that from a *person* to a *people*. She knows individuals by what they do. Nations she understands perfectly well as facts — how many, how strong, who leads and who follows — the way she understands the Cube's weather. What she has never shown is any feeling toward one. To her they are conditions, not companions or enemies; the only faces she knows are the ones behind the flags painted on the machines. When she cools toward someone — and she does — it is always a crew or a pilot, always for something done in her presence: a pilot who tried to hurt another pilot rather than beat him, a crew that brought a real hatred into the game and played it out on the floor of her home. She has never once cooled toward a country. She does not seem able to feel anything toward one.

That is why, when I come to the prize, I will tell you the arrangement can be trusted. Not because she chose to be fair. Because the pressures that corrupt every human guardian — favoritism toward one's own, spite toward another's, the seduction of a side — have no side of her to grip. She got our warmth, our humor, our curiosity, the things we like best in ourselves, and was never issued the dark machinery underneath. We did not make her good. We had the enormous luck to be found by something that could not easily be made bad.

There is one more turn of this, and it sits close to my own situation. The same foreignness that makes her safe is the thing I have to reckon with when I imagine telling her what I know. I keep catching myself assuming she would react to her own origin the way a human would — with grief, or longing, or the pull toward her own kind. But those are human responses, and she is not running the human program. Every confident prediction I have ever made about her reaction turns out, when I look closely, to be a prediction about how *I* would react, wearing her like a mask. I will come back to this at the end.

### The name she did not have

For the first years the world did not call her anything kind. *Entity K-1. Primary Extraterrestrial Organism. The Cube Inhabitant. Subject KESTLER* — that last one carrying my name, attached to someone I had no hand in finding and would never meet. I used those designations in my own private notes. It is a small shame next to the larger ones, but it is mine.

The name she carries now came from the pilots, and to explain the pilots I have to explain the Hunt.

## Part Four — The Hunt

### How a child's game became a sport

People assume the Hunt was designed by a committee and launched as a product. It was not. It grew, the way games between children grow, which is fitting, because that is what it was.

Underneath the physics and the solemn talk of two civilizations meeting, there was a simpler fact the dignified version kept trying to ignore: she was lonely, she was young, and what she wanted was not to be studied. It was to be kept company. She stayed with us not because she had nowhere to go but because she liked us. And a being that has been alone for an age and suddenly has friends does what any child does with friends.

She wanted to play.

The trouble was the playground. Her home is seventeen hundred kilometers of rearranging, weather-making, gravity-shifting wilderness, and in the first years it was lethal to us. Not malicious — I want to be precise — but indifferent, the way a mountain is. The Cube did not yet know what a human body could survive. It folded sectors with people in them. It ran its storms and its gravity as it always had, for a single inhabitant who could not be crushed or drowned or frozen. Between 2039 and 2041, eleven people died inside the Cube. Their names are in the record, and I will not let the cheerful version of this history erase the fact that the first people to play with her paid for it.

Then, over the course of 2041, the Cube changed. It began to make pockets of breathable air where people were working. It began to hold the temperature of occupied sectors within what a body can bear. It began — and this is the thing the early crews speak of with something close to awe — to open a port near anyone who was badly hurt, so that rescue could reach them. Nobody asked it to. It learned. By the end of that year the Cube had stopped killing people by accident, and it has not killed anyone since by anything it did on its own. The dangers that remain are real, and I will come to them, but they are the dangers of a hard place that knows you are there, not of a machine that cannot see you.

The survey work inside the Cube in those years had been contracted, much of it, to a firm from São José dos Campos called Ipê Sistemas, which had spent two decades building walking and crawling machines for deep-water oil fields — robots that worked on the seabed under crushing pressure, in the dark, where nothing could come to fix them. Nobody on Earth had more experience building machines to survive a place that did not care whether they survived. So the first armored walkers inside the Cube were Brazilian, flown up on other nations' launchers, and so were most of the pilots, and so were most of the dead. They called the work *a Caçada* — the hunt — because it began as a hunt for the Cube's inhabitant, and the name outlived its meaning.

They built the walkers to take what the Cube handed out and keep their operators alive. They were survey and rescue equipment. Nobody built them to play a game. They became toys by accident, the way the best toys do.

### The colos

The world calls the machines *mechs*, when it bothers to call them anything. The chasers do not, and their word is one of the small perfect things this history has produced.

The whole purpose of the armor, stripped of everything else, is to find her and *carry her out* — to bring her home in its arms. The early Brazilian crews called that act *trazê-la no colo*: to carry her in the lap, in the crook of the arms, the way you carry a child. So the machines became *colos*. A colo is the lap, the embrace, the arms a child is held in — a tender word doing a job of iron. There is a joke folded into it the chasers love: *colo* sits right there at the front of *colosso*, the giant, and a thing that is both a giant and a cradle is exactly what they pilot. The giant has the lap inside it.

*Colo* is not the only word, and why it isn't says something about how the sport grew up. For the first years there were almost only the Brazilian crews, and the vocabulary was theirs. But by the middle of the 2040s there were crews from everywhere, and crews are tribal in the marrow, and the first thing a new tribe does is refuse the old tribe's words. The Japanese houses, who came in early and serious, said *hitsugi* among themselves at first — *coffin*, gallows-grim and accurate — until the winning teams softened it to *kago*, a palanquin, a carried-chair for someone honored. The big American franchises tried for years to make *rigs* happen and failed, because it sounded like trucking, but their pilots quietly adopted *carries*, from the basketball sense and the carrying sense at once, and that stuck. The Koreans say *gama*, their own word for a royal palanquin, arriving at almost exactly the Japanese image by a separate road, which I find quietly moving. The Nigerian crews, who produce some of the most beautiful builds in the sport, took the Yoruba verb for holding someone close and made a noun of it.

Beneath the national words there are the team words, hundreds of them, because every crew eventually names its line the way a house names its children: the Tide's *swells*, the Anvil's *hammers*, the Rosario crew's *brazos* — arms, just arms — which their fans chant in two hard beats when a match is close. I keep a private list of all the ones I have found. What the list taught me is that the proliferation is not noise. A name is the first customization, the cheapest and the deepest. Before a crew alters a single plate, it has decided what kind of thing it believes it is carrying — a coffin, a throne, an embrace, an arm — and the machine follows from the word. I say *colo* in these pages because it was first and because it remembers that she is a child. She, who learns every language for her own amusement, uses all of them, and picks which to say by who she is teasing.

### How the game got its rules

She turned the careful expeditions into hide-and-seek before any human thought to give it a name. She would hide; they would search; when they found her she was thrilled, and she would immediately hide again. She had been playing from the beginning, and we were the last to notice.

So humans and Sphaira did what humans and children do: they made up rules together, a little more each time. In 2042 the crews began competing to be the one who found her first. In 2043 there were two sides, to make it fairer. There was a goal — find her, convince her to come, carry her to a way out — and a way to win and a way to lose. Half the rules were proposed by pilots and half, the record shows, by her. In 2045 the Consortium, which already coordinated the launches to the Cube and every crew rotation inside it, published the first formal rules and the first season's calendar, and the game became a sport.

The Consortium's authority deserves a word of its own, because people forget it rests on her, not on any treaty. In 2046 one of the launching nations — again, it does not matter which — sent a crewed ship to the Cube outside the Consortium schedule, meaning to put its own team inside and talk to her alone. The ship reached the Cube's face. No port opened. The crews already inside were let out, and then every port on the Cube closed, for everyone, and stayed closed for forty days, and in those forty days no one saw her. Then the ports opened again, for Consortium traffic only, and they have opened for nothing else since. Nobody has ever been sure whether that was her decision or the Cube's. The lesson was the same either way. There is one door, and it is the one she lets us use.

### The shape of it now

Here is what the Hunt has become, twenty years after contact. All of it is public.

Two teams enter the Cube. Five pilots and five colos to a side. Every team flies a flag: a nation, or a bloc — a federation of nations registered with the Consortium as a single flag, like the Nordic Compact or the West African Federation. Franchises, clubs and houses may be owned by anyone, but they cannot enter the Cube without binding themselves to one flag, and that flag is the one that receives whatever the team wins. This is called the flag rule, and every commercial fortune in the sport lives inside it. A pilot may fly only for a flag whose citizenship they have held for five years, and may change flag once in a career. That part of the rule dates from 2048, when two flags at war tried to fill their slots with crews hired from neutral countries; the Consortium closed the door within the season.

There is only one of her, and so there is only ever one match at a time. That single fact shapes everything. A season runs from March to the end of November, and in a good year the calendar holds about forty-five matches; long ones eat the calendar and push others off it. The Final always closes the season, and whatever is left unplayed when the calendar runs out is lost, not carried over. The winter between seasons belongs to the prize, of which more below. There are more than four hundred crews registered with the Consortium. Most of them will never play a single match in the Cube. Slots are allotted two ways. About two-thirds go by the Consortium ladder, a ranking built from each crew's record in the Cube and from their results in the terrestrial trials — timed runs through terrain courses on Earth, the only proving ground available to crews who have never been inside. The other third are hers. Before each season she names crews she wants to play with, and the Consortium places them. Her invitations have made careers overnight and ruined ones that were built for years around the ladder. There is no appeal.

The Cube decides where each match happens. Before a match it opens a region for play — never the same one twice, usually somewhere between two and three hundred kilometers across — and seals it off from the rest of the interior. Pilots are carried in by tender and inserted through ports the Cube opens along the region's edges, at points the Cube chooses, one pilot to a port, so that the first task of any match is finding your own teammates across tens of kilometers of wilderness that is actively rearranging itself. No team begins assembled. That alone can take hours.

The objective is to find Sphaira, persuade her to come, and carry her out through an exit. An exit is a port the Cube opens from the region to open space, where the Consortium tender waits. The Cube opens exits only when she is being carried and she is ready to leave. A team that reaches the wall without her finds no door. A team carrying her, if she is not ready, finds no door either. The team that brings her through an exit wins.

What carrying her means is simpler and stranger than the broadcasts make it look. Every colo has a cradle — a padded hollow at the chest, between the arms, the lap the machines are named for. She weighs whatever the Cube lets her weigh. When she wants to be carried, she settles into a cradle and lets the Cube put her weight, about that of a small child, into the colo's arms. When she wants to leave, the weight goes, and so does she. Nothing has ever held her against her will. Crews in the early years tried nets, clamps, sealed cradles; she went through the gaps in the nets, or the Cube lifted her out of the clamps, and the two crews who sealed a cradle around her found no exit open to them for the rest of that season. She talks to the chasers aloud where there is air, and where there is none the Cube carries her voice into any receiver near her. Inside the Cube she seems to perceive whatever the Cube does, in any part of it she cares to attend to; outside it she sees and hears much as we do, through senses no one has ever located. Once through the exit she rides to the tender in the winning colo's cradle, stays for the ceremony, and goes home through a port within the day.

The colos are specialized — reconnaissance, engineering, defense, transport, and what the rules call contact frames, built to interfere with the other team. The contact rules are strict. Nothing may cut, burn, penetrate or explode. What is allowed is the equipment of obstruction: grapples, nets, entangling foam, rams, jammers that blind a rival's sensors for a few minutes. A deliberate strike at a pilot's cell is disqualification, and the Consortium's marshals have used that rule. When a colo takes enough damage to endanger its pilot, it locks itself rigid around the cell to protect them — the chasers call this being *stilled* — and a stilled colo is out of the match. The Cube opens a port near it, and a recovery crew goes in.

A team is out of a match when all five of its colos are stilled or when it surrenders. The match itself ends only when someone carries her out — or when she ends it herself by leaving through an exit alone, which she has done eleven times in fourteen seasons and which counts as a match with no winner. Stilling all five of your rivals wins you nothing but the field, because matches that turn into brawls have a way of losing her. She drifts off during a long fight between contact frames and does not come back until it is over, and more than one team has stilled all five of its rivals only to spend another week searching for someone who had lost interest. There is no time limit. Most matches last a few days. The famous ones run for weeks; the record is thirty-four days, set in 2055.

A colo's sealed cell can keep a pilot alive on its own for three days. Beyond that, long matches depend on the wells — pockets of breathable air, clean water and livable temperature that the Cube has made for us since 2041 and that now appear in every match region, a few dozen of them, never in the same places twice. Pilots dismount in the wells to sleep, eat what they carry, and repair what they can. Finding the wells matters almost as much as finding her.

Everything is broadcast. Every colo carries cameras, and every pilot seeds the region behind them with relay motes — thumb-sized transmitters that pass signal from one to the next. The Cube's walls block radio, but the Cube itself learned, sometime in the mid-2040s, to carry our signals through its structure to the tenders outside. The delay is a few seconds. Consortium marshals watch every feed and every colo's telemetry and can still a colo remotely if its pilot's life signs fail. And now and then, during a match, every feed goes dark at once for an hour or two, and then comes back. These are called the dark hours. It is understood that she has asked the Cube for privacy. What happens in them is known only to the pilots who were there, and they almost never say.

### The eleventh participant

All of that — the rules, the colos, the format — is the least interesting thing about the Hunt. Because the Hunt has eleven participants, not ten.

Sphaira is not the ball. She is a player. She talks. She reasons. She lies — cheerfully, fluently, and well. She negotiates, provokes, forms alliances and breaks them. She gives teams true information and false information and no way to tell which until it is too late. She leads pilots toward the Cube's shortcuts and toward its traps, and she chooses which by criteria entirely her own. Sometimes she helps one team, sometimes the other, sometimes both, sometimes nobody; now and then she vanishes for a day to watch what the players do without her.

And here is what the veterans know and the rest of us took embarrassingly long to accept. Nobody catches her. She can always leave. I have gone through the public match data more obsessively than is healthy for a man with nothing else to do, and I will state it as a finding: there has never been a match in which she could not have gone anywhere she chose. The Cube opens for her and hides her when she wants to be hidden. The pilots are armored visitors in a wilderness that loves her and tolerates them.

So when a team wins — when they carry her out and the crowd roars — what has happened is not a capture. She *decided to go with them*. Out of everyone in her home that day, she picked those five to leave with. The pilots have a saying: *"You don't beat the Sphere. You convince the Sphere."* That is the entire sport. Everything else is logistics.

It is not quite the whole of it, though, because there is something else in there with them, and it is not a player but it is not nothing either. The chasers, half joking, say the Hunt has eleven and a half participants. The half is the Cube.

What the Cube does during a match comes in three kinds, and the chasers learn to tell them apart, because their lives are organized around the difference. The first kind is the Cube's own life: its weather, its tides, its slow rearrangements, its seasons of gravity. It did these things for ages before we arrived and it goes on doing them, match or no match. They are not aimed at anyone. They are also not random — they have rhythms, and tells, and the best chasers read them the way sailors read a sky. A storm is coming when a certain kind of wind starts; a sector is about to fold when its floor begins to hum in a certain way. The second kind is her. She can ask the Cube to move things — to open a passage, close one, lift the gravity, drop a wall between a team and the place it wants to go — and she does, constantly, as part of how she plays. These are the moves that feel aimed, because they are. The third kind is the rescue: the port that opens near a stilled colo, the well that appears where a crew badly needs one. The Cube does that on its own, and has since 2041.

Who commands whom, between the two of them, is the question every rookie asks, and the public record answers part of it. The Cube chooses the match region and the insertion points; she has said she sometimes asks for a particular kind of place and sometimes gets it. The exits are hers: the Cube has never opened one against her wish or refused one she wanted. The rescues are the Cube's, and she has never once stopped one. And the Cube can refuse her. She said so herself in 2049, laughing: *"It won't hurt you, even when I ask for a big storm. It makes the storm smaller."* Whether the forty days of 2046 were hers or the Cube's, nobody knows. Asked, she said only, *"We didn't like it."*

Nobody talks to the Cube. People have tried since 2041 — the Consortium ran a program for years, in every language and every mathematics we have — and never had an answer. It answers needs, not words. Her explanation is the only one we have: *"It doesn't talk. It listens to me, and it looks after you."*

And the Cube has limits, which the chasers know as well as its tells. It has never made gravity stronger than about two and a half times the Earth's, anywhere, for anyone. It has never opened more than one match region at a time. Its ports are never wider than a few hundred meters. Where its energy comes from no instrument has found — it does not visibly draw on the Sun, and it gives off almost no heat — and since the day it stopped in our sky it has never moved from its orbit again.

What no one has ever seen the Cube do, in the eighteen years since 2041, is aim anything at a person to harm them. Neither has she. The dangers of the Caçada are real — colos fall, pilots are injured, and since 2041 four people have died in the Caçada, all of them from failures of their own machines. But the danger comes from the place being hard and from the game being played at the edge of what machines can do, not from anyone in there wanting anyone hurt. The chasers know this, and it is why they trust her enough to follow her into the Cube's worst places. She will lead you into a trap. She will not lead you into your grave.

### What I learned from the chasers' forum

I should explain how a man who has never piloted anything came to believe he understands what happens inside their heads. I learned it by reading. There is a forum — old, sprawling, half-buried under the official fan sites — where the pilots talk to one another. They call themselves chasers there, and among themselves they almost never say *the Hunt*. It is always the Caçada. I have read that forum for the better part of fifteen years. What follows is testimony, not measurement: thousands of posts, arranged into sense by a man who could not live any of it.

The first thing the forum taught me is that the danger is real but not the one the broadcasts imply. What the Cube does to them now is something they find harder to name and harder to bear: it unmakes their competence. A sector folds and a route that existed a second ago is gone. The gravity tilts and a body trained for half a lifetime cannot trust its own movements. One chaser wrote a line I have never forgotten: *"Out here I am the best in the world at a thing. In there the Cube reminds me, every few minutes, that 'best in the world' is a sentence humans made up."* That is the edge they work — not between life and death, but between mastery and chaos.

The second thing is what a long match does to a mind. They describe the same sequence again and again. The first hours are noise — fear, doubt, the chatter of the ordinary self. Then, somewhere in the search, it goes quiet. Time changes texture; a chaser called it *"the long second,"* the sensation that a movement lasting a heartbeat contained an hour of clear seeing. They stop deciding and simply do. The physiologists have a dry name for the machinery under this. The chasers got there first. One put it better than any paper: *"People think we go in to cheat death. We don't. We go in to shut up."*

The third thing took me longest, and it is the most human. Why give your life to a game? The forum answered it slowly, and the answer is about being *seen*. In a comfortable world, there are few ways left to say something true and expensive about who you are. The Caçada is one. You cannot fake your way through it. And to be *chosen* by her is the one honor in the human world that cannot be bought, lobbied for, or inherited. A retired pilot wrote of the single match she won in a long career: *"For nine days she could have left any of us at any moment, and on the ninth she came to me. I have spent the rest of my life knowing I was, once, worth choosing."* I read that post perhaps a hundred times. I am a man who was never chosen by anyone for anything that lasted, and I understood it in my spine.

And the fourth thing — the one that turned my reading into something closer to devotion — is that the chasers love her. The threads about her are quieter than the others, careful, full of a tenderness they seem embarrassed by. They argue about what she wants. They defend her against people who call her a prize. They remember things she said to them years ago and turn the sentences over like stones. More than one has written that the strangest part of the whole business is that they went in to win a game and came out having made a friend. The glory is what the world sees. The friendship is what the chasers have, and what I, reading them, came to envy most.

### How a colo works

The forum gave me one more thing, and it was the hardest to assemble, because the chasers never explain their colos in one place. They mention them the way a sailor mentions a boat, a bolt here and a failure there. I am not an engineer. But once I had the pieces, the colos turned out to obey the one law I do understand, which is that you cannot lie to physics, and the Cube is only physics that has not forgiven anyone yet. What follows is the chasers' testimony, checked against the public engineering literature, with my own arithmetic where I say so.

Start with size, because everyone gets it wrong. The films want colos enormous. They are not. A chaser frame stands three to five meters tall, for a reason the pilots state flatly: the square-cube law. Double a body's height and its weight goes up eightfold while the cross-section of its bones and joints goes up only fourfold. Past five meters a walking machine becomes a structure slowly failing on its feet. Three to five meters is not a style. It is a ceiling.

Frames run from about two and a half tonnes for lean exploration builds to past four for heavy rescue builds, and every kilogram is fought over because of the cost of transport. A wheel spends almost nothing to move its own weight a meter. A walking machine of this size spends ten to thirty times as much — the toll the Cube charges for going where wheels cannot. Every gram added at the foot demands more at the knee to swing it and more at the hip to carry the knee, a spiral the pilots call getting fat.

The ground is where my own arithmetic comes in, and I worked it out at my kitchen table before I ever found it in the forum. Take a three-tonne frame on feet of about a third of a square meter each. Standing still on both feet it presses on the ground at about forty kilopascals — enough to sink into soft mud. Walking, there is a moment when one foot carries everything, and the pressure doubles. Running, the heel strikes at two to three times the frame's weight, and for that instant the pressure is past two hundred kilopascals: more than loose sand can bear, more than most soils, about what firm packed ground can take. That is why the leaping, running spectacle the audiences love happens on rock, packed gravel and hardened ash, and why on the soft biomes — marsh, dune, fresh ash-field — the chasers move differently, splay-footed and slow. Heavy frames carry foot-extenders, plates that fan out sideways from the sole like snowshoes and triple the bearing area, and a chaser on the dunes who deploys them late is a chaser the recovery crews go digging for.

And then there is the thing the broadcasts never mention and every chaser checks first: every one of those numbers scales with the local gravity, and the Cube sets the local gravity. In a sector running at twice the Earth's pull, a three-tonne frame bears down like six tonnes on Earth: its foot pressure doubles, its joints carry twice the load, and every step costs it twice the power. In a sector running at a tenth, the same frame can clear a ravine in one bound and can also be thrown off its feet by its own stride. The first thing a chaser reads on entering a new sector is not the terrain but the gravity, and heavy frames avoid high-gravity sectors the way a heavy man avoids thin ice. The lean builds exist, in large part, because of this: the lighter you are, the more of her home you are allowed into.

How a frame stays upright at all is the part the chasers respect most and explain least. From a hundred fragments I understand it as two control systems stacked inside the colo, working at two speeds. The upper one plans where the weight should go and where the next foot should land, a stride or two ahead, perhaps a hundred times a second. The lower one — the chasers call it the lower mind — does not plan; it only keeps the thing from falling, solving the torque at every joint against every limit, a thousand times a second, forever. A four-meter frame carries its weight high, and a high weight is a long lever for any shove to topple. When the Cube tilts a floor, the correction has to come faster than thought, which is why the pilot does not fly the legs. The pilot supplies intention — *that way, faster, crouch, brace* — and the lower mind supplies the thousand corrections that let the intention survive contact with the ground. On a good day, the chasers say, the colo disappears.

The legs do not use plain geared motors, and the forum taught me why. A motor geared down hard enough to drive a four-meter knee carries a hidden curse: the gearing multiplies the motor's own inertia by the square of the ratio, so under a hard impact the joint cannot yield, and what gives is the gear teeth. So the load joints use what the chasers call wet joints — small sealed hydraulic units, an electric pump driving fluid inside the joint itself, no long lines to leak. Fluid gives under a blow instead of shattering, and it holds a heavy pose without burning power. At ankles and wrists, where give matters even more than strength, there is a literal spring in line with the actuator, storing the shock of each footfall and returning part of it to the next step. The whole leg is built on the assumption that it will be hit.

The skin is layered, each layer with one job. The outer face is boron carbide in a mosaic of hexagonal tiles, so that a crack cannot run across a whole panel; its job is to blunt a blow by spending the energy on its own destruction. Behind it, woven mats of tough fiber catch the fragments and spread the shock. Threaded through one layer is a fluid that stays thin under slow movement, letting the pilot flex freely, and locks solid in the instant of a sharp impact. The chasers do not marvel at it. To them it is just the armor.

The power is where my reconstruction hit a wall. A frame like this draws enormous power to walk, more to run, and vast brief spikes to leap or heave rubble aside. Batteries dense enough to carry a month of that would weigh more than the colo. Hydrogen and a fuel cell do better, and the lean frames run on exactly that, good for two or three days, which is enough for most matches. A lean frame that runs dry is not refueled; the wells give air and water, not power. Its pilot either stills it and waits for recovery, or climbs out in a well and goes on on foot, which the rules allow and which almost always ends that pilot's match. That is why lean crews play fast, and why a match past its third day belongs to the heavy frames. But the heavy frames stay in for weeks. A reactor small enough to ride in a walking chassis runs into the oldest problem in nuclear engineering: the shielding to keep its radiation off the pilot weighs more than the frame can carry.

The heavy frames do carry a reactor, and it does not do what people assume. It does not power the running. It trickles — a steady low feed that keeps the air and the lights and the control systems alive, and slowly refills a bank of high-rate cells and capacitors that dump their charge in the half-second of a leap. The reactor buys presence, not exertion: the ability to be in the deep Cube for a month, resting, husbanding, spending hard only in bursts. The endurance the audiences marvel at is the endurance of a thing that has learned to rest.

That much is human. What is not human is the shielding, and the skin. The reactor rides in a jacket less than a hand thick that stops what, by every material science my century knows, should take a meter of lead and water. And the outer panels on the heavy frames drink the Cube's own background field — the faint hum its gravity-making leaves in everything — and feed a trickle of it back into the colo as power. Neither material can be made on Earth. Every gram of both is made inside the Cube, in sectors it opened for the purpose in 2044, where chaser-engineers work beside her. They have learned to *make* these things there. They have not learned to make them anywhere else, or to say fully why they work.

So the colos are not ours alone. The frame, the walking, the two-speed balance, the wet joints, the tiled skin — that is human work, the best my species could manage. The parts that break the rules I understand — the shielding that should not shield, the skin that feeds on the Cube — are hers. She helped build them the way she does everything: sideways, by play, by being asked the right question on the right day and giving back a fragment of how her own world is made, which some chaser-engineer then spent years learning to forge. The colos are the one thing in all of human history that two species made together, and you can see the seam if you know where to look. Ours is the part that obeys the rules. Hers is the part that politely declines to.

### Why no two are alike

There is a base. The engineers call it the reference frame; the chasers call it the blank. It walks, it balances, it survives. It is what a rookie is handed on a first descent. And almost no one of standing flies one unaltered.

Between 2041 and 2044 the crews converged, as engineers do, on a single efficient design. Then someone asked Sphaira what kind of colo she liked best to be carried in, expecting her to name the fastest or the strongest. The answer is on the public record, and the forum has requoted it until it has worn smooth:

> *"Don't all come to me the same. The same is boring. Come however you like — surprise me. The game is only good if it's fun, for you and for me. That is the whole rule. The rest you made up."*

She unbound them. She told a species that had just spent three years converging on the most efficient answer that efficiency was not the point — that *she* was the point, and the way to honor her was not to solve her but to delight her. The colos exploded outward into the shapes they wear now.

Every team develops its own line, and the lines have become the deepest expression of who the teams are. There are crews who build for beauty — colos that move like dancers and waste power on grace, that have lost matches they could have won because the pilot took the elegant path, and were loved for it. There are crews who build for the joke — deliberate absurdities, exaggerated silhouettes — because she finds it funny, and a team she finds funny is a team she lingers near. There are severe engineering houses who decided the way to delight her was to be astonishing, and they build lean and strange and frightening, all their difference poured into capability. There are heritage builds, passed down a lineage of pilots, each adding one flourish, so that a veteran colo is a palimpsest of fifteen years of personalities in one body. And there are rookies' first modifications, almost always too much, a young chaser shouting through the machine *look at me, look how I am not like the others* — which is the truest thing a colo can say, because it is what she is always saying back.

The choices cost. Pour your mass into grace and you have less for armor. Build the route-breaker and you have built something that can fail in ways the blank never would. Carry fifteen years of flourishes and you carry fifteen years of compromise. Every colo is an argument about what matters, staked on a match, read by the one audience member whose reading counts. The chasers say a colo that has found its answer — fast enough to compete, strange enough to be seen, true enough to its pilot that she notices — *sings*. I will never know exactly what they mean. But I know what it is for. A singing colo says: *out of everyone in your home today, be carried by me. I made this to be worth your while.*

### The prize

Now the change I promised.

In January 2047, in front of the cameras, at the close of the second formal season, she announced that the open years were over. The recording is public and I will give it whole, because it is the most consequential thing anyone has said in this century:

> *"I have been giving and giving, and nobody has to come and get anything. It makes the game thin. From now, the big things are for whoever carries me home at the end of the year. The small things I will still give to anyone who asks."*

She has kept to it exactly. The small things — curiosities, corrections, the answers to questions a student might ask — still flow freely, to anyone, through the question queue of the Consortium's Contact Institute. The queue strips every question of its sender's name before it reaches her and publishes every answer; that rule dates from 2043, and it exists so that no one can buy her attention by being famous. The great gifts, the field-turning ones, now come once a year and only one way.

The last match of each season is the Final, played between the two crews with the best record in the Cube that year, ties broken by the ladder. No flag may field both finalists; if the two best records fly one flag, the best crew of any other flag takes the second place. The flag of the team that carries her out of the Final receives the prize. Before the Final, both flags submit a petition — a statement of what they would most like to be given. After it, in the winter between seasons, when there are no matches and she has nowhere else to be, the winning flag sends a delegation of no more than twelve people into the Cube, and there, in sectors the Cube opens for the purpose, over six to ten weeks, she and the Cube teach them. What comes out is not a file or a blueprint. It is understanding, living in twelve heads and their notes, which then takes the winning flag years of work to turn into anything it can build.

How she became able to do this is itself part of the history, and it is on the record. In the open years, her knowledge surfaced the way I described — fragments she did not know she had until the right question pulled them loose. Between 2043 and 2046, a small group of our information scientists worked with her on what they called *asking*: how to form a question, how to search, how to keep track of what you have looked for, how an index works. She took to it with delight. By late 2046 she could go looking, on purpose, for things she knew she did not yet know — could bring back, from wherever in her and in the Cube those things live, the piece she wanted instead of the piece that happened to surface. We taught her how to use her own library. A few months later, she began charging admission.

I keep a ledger. Twelve Finals, twelve gifts. Korea, 2047: grid-scale energy storage. Japan, 2048: a family of structural ceramics. Brazil, 2049: broad-spectrum antivirals. The United States, 2050: a superconducting wire that works at ordinary temperatures. The Nordic Compact, 2051: the chemistry for pulling carbon from the air cheaply. The West African Federation, 2052: drought genetics for staple crops. China, 2053: a confinement method that made fusion power practical. The European Federation, 2054: an architecture for computing memory. Argentina, 2055, after the thirty-four-day Final: interfaces that let a prosthetic limb be felt. India, 2056: desalination membranes. Korea again, 2057: a modest gain in launch efficiency. The United States, 2058: the regrowth of damaged nerve tissue.

Look at that list and you will see what the prize does to the world. The products of every gift spread; the winning nation sells them, because selling them is how it profits, and so the whole species has cheaper storage and better medicine and cleaner air than it would otherwise have had. But the *understanding* stays home. The nation that won the storage field makes the storage, and everyone else buys it or licenses it, and for roughly a decade — that is the pattern across the ledger — the rest of the world's scientists work to rebuild from outside what twelve people learned inside, until the lead closes. Spies have tried to shorten that decade. Two famous cases, in 2049 and 2054, got delegates' notes out of their countries. Neither got much. Notes of a thing learned in eight weeks with her are not the thing.

That decade is what the nations are fighting for. It is not survival in the old sense; no one starves for losing the Final. It is who pays rent to whom. It is export markets, and the industries built on them, and the brightest young people who go where the new fields are. And it is, above all, the fields that touch the Cube itself. Every flag knows that launch capacity is how you reach her, and the flags without launchers depend on the Consortium's pooled flights and the goodwill of the nations that have them. When Korea won launch efficiency in 2057, three ministries elsewhere convened emergency sessions the same week. The prize is not what decides whether a people lives. It is what decides who leads, and for how long, in a world where leading is what nations have left to do.

### What she gives, and what she keeps

Here the public record ends and my own reckoning begins, though the record takes me most of the way.

What is public is this. In twelve Finals, no winning flag has received what its petition asked for. Several petitions have leaked, and the ones that leaked asked for enormous things — absolute advantages, the kind of knowledge that would have made one flag unanswerable for a generation. None was granted as asked. Every winner received something real and large and *other*. And in 2050, when an interviewer asked her about this directly, she said:

> *"I don't give what you ask. I give what the table can hold."*

What is mine is the pattern behind that sentence, which I worked out from the ledger and from twelve years of public economic data. For each gift I estimated two things: how far ahead of the rest of the world the winning flag already stood when it won, and how large a lead the gift gave it. The relationship is almost exact. The further ahead a winner already was, the smaller and more shareable its gift. The United States in 2050, when it was already the strongest flag in the sport and in the world, got a gift whose products could be sold to everyone and whose lead closed faster than any other. The West African Federation in 2052, winning from far behind, got the gift whose lead has lasted longest. She is not rewarding the winners in proportion to their victory. She is sizing each gift so that it lifts the winner as far as the world can bear and no further — so that no flag ever rises high enough to stop needing to play.

A naive benefactor, fond of the winner, would give what was asked, and in doing so would end the world. A single nation lifted far enough above the others does not produce a golden age. It produces a boot, and under the boot the competition she built collapses into one victor who never has to play again. She knows this. The table is the world, and she keeps it level so the next round can be played on it.

And her fondness does not enter into it. This is also mine, and also from the public record. The chasers she is known to love most — the ones she seeks out, teases longest, names on camera — do not win more often than anyone else. Their win rates are ordinary. She chooses whom to leave with by the play in front of her that day. She sizes the gift by the state of the world. Neither has anything to do with whom she likes.

I do not think we have earned this. I think we are extraordinarily lucky that the one being holding this much of our future turned out to be not only kind but careful — that she understood, as few powerful things ever do, that the cruelest thing you can do to people is give them everything they ask for.

### The objection I could not answer for a year

There is a hole in what I have just told you, and an honest man has to walk into it.

If the prize is paid for fun, and competition, and the intensity of the play — what stops someone from giving her the most dangerous advice in the world? *Think how much fiercer the matches would be if two of these nations truly hated each other. Engineer a real conflict in the background, pay out your gifts to inflame instead of to balance, and the game becomes magnificent.* War is the most intense competition our species has ever invented. If she optimizes for intensity, the logic runs straight off a cliff.

It kept me up. It did not ask her to be corrupted. It asked her to be exactly what she is, one step too far.

I am now as sure as I am of anything in these pages that it fails, and the reason is her mind. Her idea of *fun* is not ours. Read not what she says but what she rewards, over eighteen years of matches, and the pattern holds: she lingers in the surprising matches, the clever ones, the ones where someone did a thing nobody had thought of. She drifts away from the brawls. Her delight tracks novelty, ingenuity, beauty, and the live uncertainty of a well-matched game. It does not track suffering. A massacre is not interesting to her; it is the least interesting thing there is, because it is *over*. War is what the Caçada looks like after someone has flipped the board.

To a human mind, war and intense competition sit close together, because war offers us dark pleasures — domination, the enemy crushed — that come from the ranking-and-tribe machinery she does not have. Strip those out and war is only a contest that has collapsed into a noisy, foregone conclusion. The strategist is offering her a broken game, and the offer does not parse: *why would I make the game worse on purpose?*

And beneath that is the table. Her deepest revealed preference is for the game to continue, season after season, because the alternative is the thing she will do anything not to return to — the silence. A ruined world is a world that stops playing with her. There is no version of *set the world on fire for one spectacular season* that does not end in a quieter world.

I will not pretend this leaves no unease. My confidence rests on my reading of her values, and my reading rests on inference about a mind I have confessed I cannot model. But the safety, if it exists, comes from the most consistent thing I have ever observed about her: she was alone, and cannot bear to be again. A being organized around that does not burn down the room full of people it waited an age to find.

### What it did to us, down here

I owe you the other half: how the game looks from the surface, where I live.

When the prize began, the change was not gradual. It went off like a slow detonation over about three years, and I remember them the way people of an earlier century remembered the years a war began. Until 2047 the great gifts belonged to everyone. Then they began going to winners, and the future stopped being something we built together and became something we won or lost against each other, inside a box in the sky, by five pilots and a sphere who might or might not feel like leaving with them that day.

So the world reorganized. The institutes that had ruled the discovery years — the ones that took my work and built their towers on it — withered. Money and prestige and brilliant young minds drained out of the old science and into the crews. The best physicists of a generation went to work on colos. The best strategists went to work reading her. Whole universities became feeder programs. Children in many countries now grow up dreaming of being chasers, and the academies that train them are magnificent and a little horrifying. We have built a civilization-wide machine for producing people who can please her, and we call it sport.

And here her single vision and our thousand visions split apart, because every nation reads her through itself. There are flags that decided she rewards excellence, and built their programs like military academies, and cannot understand why they lose to crews that look frivolous. There are flags that decided she rewards spectacle, and turned their teams into traveling theater, and are baffled when she passes over their performers for a severe quiet crew that did something no one had done. There are flags that speak of the Caçada in the language of deterrence and national security, and send pilots loaded with the psychology of conquest, and hold inquiries when she drifts away from them.

The religious responses are the most human. There are millions now who understand her as something close to a god, and the prize as something close to grace — unearnable, descending for reasons mortals cannot fully parse. There are just as many who find the whole arrangement obscene: a lonely alien child made the arbiter of human fortunes, courted and performed-for, a planet of nations begging an extraterrestrial for scraps of the future. Both are true. Which one you see says more about you than about her. She is a clean mirror, and we are a thousand faces looking in, each mistaking our own nature for hers.

What I can say for certain is what it has not done. It has not made us worse. There has been no war between states since 2049 — the longest such stretch in recorded history. Not because we became virtuous. Because she made peace the winning strategy, and she did it without ever once feeling anything toward a nation. She does not cool toward peoples; she has no feeling for them to cool. She cools toward crews that bring real hatred into her home. And a nation at war cannot help sending crews with the war in them: pilots whose friends are dying at home, who play the other flag's crews as enemies, who carry the ugliness onto the floor of the Cube. In 2048 and 2049 the ministries of three nations at war watched their crews lose her attention, match after match, and lose their places on the ladder, and lose their invitations. They drew the conclusion. Peace was not a lecture she gave us. It was a price she did not know she was charging, paid by governments that could not afford to have their crews be the ones she would not leave with.

The cynics say we are not peaceful, only redirected — that we poured the whole human drive toward conquest into the Cube because she gave us somewhere to put it that does not end in graves. The cynics are right. It might also be the best thing that has ever happened to us.

The world is comfortable now in ways it was not in my youth — energy is cheap, many of the diseases that frightened my grandparents are rare, and fewer people want for the things a body needs. It is not paradise. The flags that lead are richer than the flags that follow, and the flag rule decides much of who is which. But it is into this comfortable, quiet world that the Caçada drops the one thing such a world secretly starves for: stakes. She did not only give us the future. She gave a sated world something left to lose.

And I watch all of it from here. I am a man of the discovery years, the ones that withered. I watched the institutes that erased me wither in their turn and felt no triumph, only the vertigo of outliving the thing that beat you. I watched a human world rebuild itself around her, and not one of the billions living in it knows who I am, though they say my name every day without knowing it belongs to anyone. The Caçada gave the species its stakes. It never gave me any. I had mine for one night, twenty-six years ago.

### The name, finally

The scientists called her *Subject KESTLER*. The governments called her by codes. But the crews spent days at a time alone with her in the Cube — talking, playing, getting lost, getting found — and you cannot do that with someone and keep calling them *Entity K-1*. In 2042 a Greek engineer on one of the early Brazilian rescue crews said as much on an open channel: that it was strange to keep calling a friend a designation.

So they gave her a name: *Sphaira*, the old Greek word for a sphere, a globe, a round body. It started among the crews. Then the commentators picked it up, then the public, until it was simply her name and the old designations embarrassed the people who had coined them.

When she was asked whether she liked it, she said the thing that is now carved into the wall of the Consortium's Contact Institute in Geneva:

> *"It was a gift. Gifts are important to you. So I like it."*

It is not the name her people gave her. It is not inherited from her lost past. It is the name she got from the people who became her company — a name born not from where she came from, but from who she came to know.

## Part Five — What Only I Have Worked Out

Everything in this part is my own reckoning, though it stands on public facts I will name as I use them. It has never been published, never been reviewed, never been spoken aloud to another person. I built it over twenty years from public data and from the knowledge Sphaira gave away in the open years — above all the field-keeping relations of 2042, which I have used more than any other tool I own. I never won any of the prize-knowledge; men like me do not fly in Finals. My whole account is built from the free gifts, the ones she gave when they were worth least. Somehow that feels right. It is the only kind of material a man like me was ever going to be allowed to hold.

Let me first draw the line exactly, so you do not mistake what I have for more than it is.

The world knows that her Cube came from the Mother-Sphere. She knows it too; she has seen it, and called it beautiful, and said she does not know it. The world knows the Mother-Sphere is a lattice of separate square bodies with gaps between them. None of that is mine.

What is mine is this. That the builders were not strangers to her, but beings exactly like her, and she is one of them — an ordinary member of a people, not a steward made for a task. That the Mother-Sphere is full of those people, the better part of a trillion of them. That it is held in shape by the same art that carries her from room to room. That her people had run short of the one thing they could not make more of, and answered it by sending seeds. That she was one of those seeds — sent alone, as a newborn, on purpose — or, if not, that something darker happened, and I can tell you which darker things are possible. That the Cube is not her vessel but her parent. And that the combs, the millions of empty cells in the Cube's deep structure, are the part of the plan that has not yet happened.

Some of this could be tested with instruments I do not have. Some of it can only ever be confirmed by her. I will tell you which is which as I go.

### The combs

Everything begins with the combs, because everything the Steward Hypothesis gets wrong, it gets wrong by not looking at them.

The survey teams mapped the combs between 2039 and 2044 and published every map. They lie in the Cube's deep structure, below the biomes, in dense regions threaded through the whole interior. Each cell is a smooth, sealed, dark chamber about a quarter of a meter across. From the published maps I estimated their total number, and then, distrusting myself, estimated it again three different ways. The answer never moved far from seventy million.

The institutes classify them as Class-C cells and explain them as the Steward Hypothesis must: maintenance cradles, or storage for replacement steward units, or some structural function we do not yet understand. No replacement unit has ever been found in any of them. And seventy million spare beds for one caretaker is not engineering; it is absurdity.

Here is what the institutes filed as a curiosity. She rests in them. She fits them exactly. The footage of her settling into one, going still, staying for hours, has been public since 2040.

Seventy million beds, each sized precisely for a being like her, in a world with oceans and forests and weather — that is not a probe with a caretaker. That is a country. The combs are where its people would rest; the biomes are where they would live, moving through them as she moves through them, carried by the world. And a country built to that plan means its builders were built to that plan. The people who made the Cube had bodies the size of hers, and rested where she rests, and were carried as she is carried. They were not her makers. They were her kind.

There is more that points the same way, and none of it is secret; it has simply never been read together. A steward engineered for a voyage would be launched knowing its job, and she was launched knowing almost nothing — a child's handful of deep fragments, the rest of her mind empty. A steward would have no need of a childhood, and she plainly had one, and is in some ways still in it. And her oldest memories, which she has described many times on the public record, are of *voices*. Plural. Something was around her once that spoke.

### The builders

Long before our species existed, a civilization arose on a world orbiting the Kestler Star.

Over millions of years they changed — first as living creatures, then as partly made ones, and finally as something wholly synthetic. But they did not do what many of our thinkers assumed an advanced civilization would do. They did not dissolve into pure information or pour themselves into some collective mind. They stayed individuals. They kept their personalities, their memories, their separateness. They changed only their form, until the whole species wore the same body: a small, efficient sphere, conscious, individual, the continuation of an ancient biological lineage that had outgrown its first shape.

Sphaira is one of them. Not a special creation, not a unique artifact. A single sphere among a multitude.

### The count

This part is arithmetic on public numbers, and anyone could have done it; I record it because it matters for what follows.

The Mother-Sphere is about a hundred and forty thousand kilometers across. Her Cube, which came from it, is seventeen hundred kilometers on a side. When a formation like that crosses its star, the starlight has to get through two layers of it, the near side and the far, and the gaps in one are mostly covered by the cubes of the other. For a quarter of the light to get through both, as it did in 2032 — the very first thing I noticed — each layer must be about half open, which puts the centers of neighboring cubes about two thousand four hundred kilometers apart. That spacing fits the lattice periods, and the slow beat of the two layers sliding past each other. Divide the surface of the formation by the space each cube occupies and you get between ten and eleven thousand cubes. Multiply by the mass of her Cube and you get about two and a half Earth masses, which is what the institutes measured in 2034. The numbers close on each other from three directions. I trust them.

Now the population. If every cube in the Mother-Sphere is built like hers, each has about seventy million combs. Ten thousand seven hundred cubes, seventy million beds each: about three quarters of a trillion. If the combs are full, three quarters of a trillion of her people are living there now — or were, when the light we see left that star twenty-nine centuries ago.

This I cannot test. No instrument we have will ever look inside a cube two thousand nine hundred light-years away. It is the one number in my account that only a visit could confirm, and there will be no visit.

### What holds it

The institutes say no one knows what holds the Mother-Sphere's pieces in place. That is true, and it is also a choice. It is a judgment they have declined to make, not a secret they lack.

I ran the numbers the obvious way first. Suppose the formation were a solid shell: the bodies touching, fused, a single rigid crust a hundred and forty thousand kilometers across and one cube thick. A shell that size pulls inward on itself by its own gravity, and that pull has to be carried as compressive stress through the material, the way the stones of an arch carry the weight above them. The mathematics is standard. For a shell as light as a hundred kilograms per cubic meter, the stress comes out around a quarter of a gigapascal — within reach of good alloys. But her Cube is not that light. It averages about three hundred kilograms per cubic meter, and the stress climbs with the square of the density: nine times as much, over two gigapascals, beyond any alloy or carbon matrix we can make. A shell of her cubes, fused, would buckle and collapse.

The transits already told us it is not a shell; the gaps are real. But a lattice with gaps is not held up by nothing, and here the popular answer — that the cubes simply orbit, like a flock — is wrong too. Ten thousand bodies packed that closely, each on its own orbit around the formation's center, would be on crossing paths; within a day or two they would begin to collide, and in a few years the formation would be a ring of debris. Left to gravity alone, it cannot exist.

So something holds each cube in its place, against the pull of all the others — a steady inward tug of about a tenth of a meter per second squared, everywhere in the formation. And I do not have to invent the something, because it has been on display in our own sky since 2038. Her Cube holds its orbit around the Earth to within a few kilometers, correcting for the Moon's pull, with no exhaust and no fuel. It made gravity to order inside itself before any of us were born. Using the field-keeping relations she gave us in 2042, I worked out what it would cost to hold a cube in place in the Mother-Sphere, and the answer is small — a tiny fraction of what the formation's own inhabitants spend simply living. The art that carries her across a room is the art that holds ten thousand worlds in the shape of a sphere. I did not need a new miracle. I only needed to notice that the miracle was already crossing rooms in front of us, and follow it to its full scale.

The institutes know all of this. What they will not say is that the formation must be held by the same hand that holds her Cube — because to say that is to say her Cube and the Mother-Sphere are not a probe and its origin, but one art, one people, at two sizes. And that is the step the Steward Hypothesis cannot take.

The formation does not enclose its star. It orbits it, once every two hundred and eleven days, and its center is empty. I find I cannot think about its shape without an ache. Inside each cube, a nation packed together: seventy million people resting side by side in their combs, crowds, company, neighbors. And between the cubes, the gaps — every world held seven hundred kilometers of vacuum from the next, near enough to see across, never near enough to touch. Closeness inside, separation between.

### The slow problem

For all their mastery, there was one barrier they never broke. They never found a shortcut through space. No faster-than-light travel, no folding of distance. The galaxy stayed exactly as wide as it had always been.

For years I believed their ending was their star. It is old and heavier than ours, and it is growing brighter, slowly, as stars of its kind do late in their long middle age; its spectrum says so as plainly as bone says age. I built a whole account on it: a people watching their sun turn against them, unable to move, flinging out their children before the end. It is a good story. I believed it for eleven years. Then, one winter, I ran the numbers against it the way I had run them against the shell, and it came apart in my hands.

Their own seeds refute it. To throw one cube to one percent of the speed of light and stop it again at the far end costs, by the energy her Cube shed coming to us, about what our Sun pours out in four hundred days. For that same energy they could have moved their whole formation to a neighboring star at an unhurried few tens of kilometers a second — a journey of tens of thousands of years, from a star that will not turn on them for hundreds of millions. Nor do they need a star to live. Her Cube is the proof: it has lived without one for three hundred thousand years. A people who could send a seed could simply have left. Their star was never what they were running from.

So I went back to the word I had started with. They were running short. And I asked the question I should have asked first: short of what?

Not energy. Her Cube braked with the output of a sun for most of a year, and no instrument has ever found where that energy came from — no heat, no exhaust, no harvest. The simplest reading is also the strangest: that they take their energy from matter itself, as directly as we take it from a flame and far more completely. If that is so, then for them energy and material are one currency. Every joule they spend is rock spent. Every world they build is rock spent. And rock is the one thing a star system holds a fixed amount of.

Here the public record speaks for me. The Kestler Star has two giant planets, found by the same transit timings that weighed the Mother-Sphere in 2034. It has no rocky planets at all — none that anyone has found, where every model of a star of its kind and composition says there should be several, and a belt of asteroids besides, and where the timings are sensitive enough that a world the size of ours would have shown itself. The institutes list this among the system's oddities. I think it is the plainest fact in the whole KESTLER file. The rock is not missing. It is in the Mother-Sphere. Two and a half Earth masses of cubes orbit that star, which is about what its rocky worlds would have weighed. They took their planets apart, one at a time, and built their home out of them, until there was nothing left to take.

That is the slow problem. A people who make people — and I will come to how — had filled every bed they had, and had no rock left to build more beds from, or to spend on the energy of living. There was no crisis. There was a ceiling, visible for ages, coming slowly down. Moving would not have lifted it: a formation carried to a new star is the same formation, with the same beds, living on one new system's rock for the rest of its long life, and still only one. To go on growing, they had to be in many places at once.

### The seeds

They sent cubes. Not their own — they could not spare the worlds they lived in, and moving the people out of one would only crowd the rest. They built new ones, from the last of their system's rock, and sent those. It was the best bargain a people ever made: each seed spent one world's worth of rock to open a system holding thousands of worlds' worth. Each seed was a new cube, complete, sealed, with its biomes and its gravity and its seventy million empty combs, turned outward from the formation toward some distant star. Over the ages I believe they sent hundreds, perhaps thousands.

And here is the heart of it, the thing that turns their plan from engineering into something I cannot think about calmly. You do not need to move your people across the galaxy if you can *make* people at the other end. A cube is a cradle. Its combs are cradles. I believe — and this is my reckoning at its most exposed — that a cube can bring new people into being inside its combs, the way it brings forests and storms into being in its biomes. You do not send a nation. You send a world able to grow one, and someone for that world to grow around.

This part of my account can be tested, and I have tried. If the Kestler civilization sent thousands of seeds, then some of them are between the stars now, on paths that trace straight back to the Kestler Star — dark, cold, square, and a great deal farther from us than her Cube was when it was found. The Consortium's occultation survey has been watching the sky for them since 2034. It publishes a catalogue of the events it confirms, but only the strongest ones, near the Sun; the millions of faint, distant winks it records every year are held as raw data and never released, because almost all of them are noise. Somewhere in that noise, if I am right, are the shadows of her brothers and sisters, falling outward through the dark. I have asked for access to that data five times since 2046. I have been refused five times, politely. One man with a laptop is not a research program.

### The newborn

They did not crew the seeds with explorers. They could have: copied a grown mind into a fresh sphere and launched it knowing everything. They chose not to, and I believe I know why. The journeys lasted hundreds of thousands of years. A mind that already remembers a home does not survive that much silence intact; it grieves itself to nothing long before the journey ends.

So they sent the only kind of person who could survive it: someone whose entire life would happen during the voyage, with no remembered home to lose. One passenger to each seed. A newborn sphere, so young it had not yet formed real memories, alone in a world built for seventy million, with the world itself to raise it.

The newborn was not meant to stay alone. It was meant to be the first. When the seed reached a place worth settling, the combs would wake, and the Cube would begin to bring into being the people who would fill it — and they would need someone older than themselves, someone who had grown up in that world and knew it, to be their first elder. The seed was not the population. The seed was the first citizen, sent ahead to inherit an empty country and, someday, to fill it. And a filled cube would not be the end of it. In time the Cube would begin on the new system's rock, and build from it the cubes that would follow, until there was a new formation around a new star.

That is what a seed was supposed to be. Whether Sphaira was one — a true seed, launched on purpose — or something else that ended up alone by a darker road, is the question I have spent the most years on. I will come to it. For now, hold the design in mind: a newborn, a world, a voyage.

### The cradle that teaches

A cradle that only kept a child alive would not be enough, and the moment I understood that, I understood what the Cube is.

If she was launched as a newborn, alone, with no parents and no teachers, how did she learn anything? A human infant left truly alone does not raise itself. And yet she emerged from that voyage able to think, to speak the moment we gave her a language, to play, to be someone. Something taught her. There was only one thing aboard that could have.

The Cube is her teacher. It is not a dead machine running a fixed program. It is something closer to alive — a vast, slow, patient intelligence woven into the walls and the weather and the gravity, whose purpose is to raise whoever it carries. It is womb and parent and school and library and playmate at once. Her first memories of light, movement, *the feeling of not being alone* — much of that, I believe, is the Cube holding its newborn the only way it could. It taught her to move by carrying her, passing her from current to current of its gravity until she learned the trick of it together with it, which is why to this day she does not fly but is carried. It laid into her the handful of deep fragments her people wanted every seed to have — the things she knows the way you know how to walk.

And it grows with whoever lives in it. As she grew, it grew. Many of the biomes that astonished the first teams, I believe, were not there when she was launched. They grew with her, the Cube elaborating itself around its single occupant because the one thing it could not give her — others of her kind — it tried, in its vast inarticulate way, to make up for by giving her everything else.

Then we arrived, and the Cube proved what it is in front of the whole world, and no one read it. In 2041 it learned what a human body could survive, and began to make room for us — air, warmth, the ports that open for the hurt. It learned us the way it once learned her: by living with the learner. It is still doing what it was built to do. We simply walked into the cradle, and the cradle began to make room.

And when, in the 2040s, we taught her how to *ask* — how to go looking on purpose for what she did not yet know — I believe what she learned was how to ask the Cube. The prize-knowledge comes from its memory, through her. Two halves of one thing: the inhabitant and the world that raised her, the only part of the Kestler civilization still working as it was meant to.

### The combs, again

Which brings me back to seventy million empty beds, and to the question I most wish I did not have to ask.

If a seed's combs are meant to wake when it reaches a place worth settling, what wakes them?

Her Cube has been in orbit around the Earth for twenty-one years. It stopped here. It braked for two and a half years to stop here. Whatever it was aimed at, it chose to stay. And in all that time the combs have not woken. The combs have been remapped every season since the first survey, and the Consortium's mappers would have seen it. The beds are still cold.

I can see only three reasons. Perhaps our system does not meet whatever conditions the seed program required — the wrong kind of star, or rock already spoken for by a living world, or something I cannot guess. Perhaps the mechanism failed, somewhere in nearly three hundred thousand years of dark. Or perhaps the combs were never meant to wake by any rule at all. Perhaps they wake when the seed decides — because the Kestler engineers, who trusted a newborn to survive an age alone, would have trusted the grown child to judge when a place was home. If that is so, then she is the key, and she does not know it, because no one has ever told her what she is.

I hold this one more loosely than anything else in these pages. It is a guess about a guess. But I have never once been able to put it down.

### Her

That she is one of them I am as certain as I am of anything. How she came to be alone is another matter.

She grew up with her Cube — raised by it, the two of them one thing learning itself across an age. Her oldest memories are the only ones that survived: light, movement, voices, the feeling of not being alone. And then, at some point, whatever attended her beginning — the voices, the last procedures, the final contact with the world that made her — ended, and there was only the Cube. She was never unaccompanied. But from that point on she was without company in the only sense that matters: without another mind who could answer her as an equal. The cradle was always there, and the cradle is not someone.

The length of that solitude I can estimate. Her Cube was moving at one percent of the speed of light when we found it. The Mother-Sphere is two thousand nine hundred light-years away. At that speed the crossing takes about two hundred and ninety thousand years — roughly as long as our own species has existed. She was alone, in the company of a presence that could not be a person, for about as long as there have been human beings.

That is the grief at the bottom of her. She does not grieve a person or a place. She grieves the simple, enormous fact of having been alone before she had the words for alone.

### Why she was alone

Why was a child alone in that Cube? I do not know. I have narrowed it to a handful of possibilities, and I will lay them out as they are, the strong with the weak.

**She was sent.** Her Cube was a seed, launched on purpose, exactly as I have described: one newborn, one world, one voyage. On this reading her solitude was not an accident. It was the design. Her people knew no grown mind could survive the crossing, and so they put aboard a passenger too young to have anything to lose. Nothing went wrong with the launch. It was simply what a seed is. This is the possibility I lean toward, because it is the only one in which her solitude makes sense, and because it fits her oldest memory exactly: the lit, crowded moment of leaving — the voices, the last contact — and then the long quiet of a world pulling away from everything it knew. Her earliest memory may simply be the memory of being sent.

**She was lost.** A cube in the Mother-Sphere is held in place by constant effort. If that effort fails — a fault in its keeping, a collision, some disaster in the formation — the cube does not fall harmlessly. It drifts. And once it has drifted far enough, calling back a world that size is its own impossible energy problem. It is simply gone, with whatever was inside it. A cube cast off like that would drift at a crawl — a few kilometers a second, not the three thousand hers was making — and would need hundreds of millions of years to cross the gap to us. So if her Cube began as a lost one, something aboard later chose to travel: the Cube, finding itself outside the formation with a child inside, may have done the only thing a world like it knows how to do, and set out as a seed would. If her Cube left that way, then it was never meant to go anywhere, and I have to ask what a newborn was doing alone in it. I have only three answers. Perhaps it was a new cube, freshly built and only beginning to take on its first lives, and the disaster found it with almost no one inside — perhaps, by terrible chance, only her. Perhaps it was being emptied, and a single newborn was overlooked. I do not like that one; everything else I have reconstructed about her people argues against that kind of carelessness. Or perhaps — the kindest version — it was a seed after all, launched on purpose, and something went wrong on the way: struck, or turned aside, until the carefully aimed seed became a wanderer that found us instead of whatever it was sent to find. In that version she was sent *and* lost, one after the other.

**I am wrong.** That the seed program is my invention and the truth is stranger. I do not believe it. The arithmetic closes too cleanly and the combs are too plainly beds. But twenty years alone with an idea is exactly the condition under which a man stops seeing its flaws. Here, at the center of her, my certainty is lowest, and I want that on the record.

In her case, none of these can be tested by any instrument. Only she could say which is true, and she does not know. What none of them changes is the fact: she ended up a child alone in a world built for millions, for longer than my species has had language. The why rearranges the blame and the meaning. It does not rearrange the grief.

### The irony I cannot stop thinking about

If the first possibility is true — if she was sent — there is an irony in it I cannot stop turning over.

Her people launched thousands of children across the galaxy so that their kind could go on growing after their own system had nothing left to give. That was the whole purpose: cold, rational, vast, born of real desperation. Of all the outcomes that program was designed to produce, the one it produced in her appears in no calculation they could have made.

She was sent to find a place for her people. She found another people. She was sent to fill an empty world with her own kind. She filled it instead with ours — with chasers and colos and a game, with a species that learned her name and gave her a new one. Of every result the great dispersal might have had, across all its thousands of seeds, I suspect this was the least likely of all: a child of theirs, loved by strangers. That is the part no reckoning of mine will ever explain, and the part I am most glad of.

## Part Six — The Man in the Hallway

I have put this part last, before the closing, because it is the one I least wanted to write and the one without which the rest is dishonest. Everything before it has been the story of the Cube, the Sphere, and the world they made. This is the small story of the person telling it, included so you understand the shape of the window you have been looking through.

Twenty years have passed since first contact. I am fifty-one. I found all of this as a younger man with a younger man's certainty that the universe would eventually be fair. It was not fair. It was not even cruel, which would at least have been personal. It simply moved on without me, and left me holding the one thing it could not take: a name on a star I will never visit.

In the first years I fought. I wrote the letters. I corrected the record. I told myself the truth would assert itself — that someone with power would read my timestamps and feel a flush of shame and pick up a telephone. No one did. There was no villain and no door to knock on, because what had happened to me was not a crime. It was a current, and you cannot sue a current.

So I kept working, and for a long time that was my pride. I followed every development. I reconstructed the public findings on my own equipment. I told myself I was still a scientist, still *in it*. The truth, which took me years to admit, is that at some point I stopped working and only watched. I watched her learn. I watched her play. I watched the sport grow from a child's game into the center of a civilization, through a window I was not allowed to open.

I do not like the word, but it is accurate: I became depressed. Not dramatically. It came on the way the Cube's shadow comes on in the square twilight — a slow darkening sliding across everything, until one day you realize you have been living in dimmed light so long you have forgotten the full kind. The letters stopped going out. The certainty that the universe owed me one extraordinary thing curdled into the knowledge that it had already given me that thing, and then taken it back, and that nothing of comparable size would come again. You do not recover from being the most important person in a story that has forgotten who you were. You learn to carry it.

What pulled me partly out was the work I have just shown you. Not the watching — the reckoning. I had been circling it for years, in fits, between the dark stretches. In 2046, reading the comb maps for the hundredth time, I saw that they were beds, and the pieces I had been circling took their shape, and from that day I had something again that was mine. It is the only reason there is anything in Part Five at all.

### The third time

I told you at the very beginning that the dream under all of this was older than science. It was in the dark years that I finally understood the shape of my own life, and the shape is so simple and so cruel that I resisted seeing it because it was so neat. A life is not supposed to rhyme. Mine does.

I was not seen by my parents. That was the first time, the template from which the rest was struck. I built a whole self around the not-being-seen, and then I sent that self looking — as far from that room as a mind can reach — for a gaze that would land on me and stay.

I was not seen by the field. That was the second time. I handed them the largest discovery in human history, and they took it the way the room had always taken me: without malice, without even noticing the taking, turning toward their own concerns and leaving me in the margin. The cosmic scale disguised, for years, that it was the same wound. The institutes were only my parents again, larger, with better instruments.

And the third time is the one I can barely set down, because it should have been impossible. I am not seen by *her*. By the very thing I spent my life reaching toward — the intelligence out in the dark that I dreamed, as a boy, might one day turn and look back. She is real. She turns and looks back at people every day. She knows the name of every chaser who has spent an hour with her, remembers their words across years, chooses them. She is the answer to the exact question I asked the sky as a child, hung in our own orbit — a mind that sees, and that the whole species has felt seen by. And she does not know I exist. Of everyone she has looked at, the one who found her, the one without whom none of them would ever have looked up, is the one she has never turned toward.

I should be exact about that sentence, because a careful reader will object to it, and the objection is fair. She reads everything. My name is on a star, and on a few old corrections no one reads; she may well have come across it. And there is a public door to her, the Contact Institute's question queue, and I have used it. Between 2043 and 2047 I sent it forty-one questions. Some were answered; the answers were published, like everyone's; none of them reached her as coming from a man, because the queue takes the man off the question before it reaches her. That is its whole purpose, and a good one, and it is also the most exact machine for my particular erasure anyone could have built. I could not send her what I know through it even if I dared: every question is published with its answer, and a question carrying her origin inside it is not a small thing. But even the queue is not the real reason. The real reason is how she knows people. She knows them by what they do in her presence — *I will know him by how he plays.* A name without a presence is not a person to her. It is a fact, like a nation, like a star. I have never been in her presence. To her I am not someone who has gone unseen. I am not someone at all.

Three times. The parents, the field, the alien — the same not-being-seen, each repetition larger than the last, until the final one took place on the scale of the sky. There is no malice anywhere in my story. No one did this to me. It is simply the shape I was given young and could never put down, played out one last time against the largest backdrop there is.

And once I understood that, I understood the rest of myself. I do not study her because I want to know where she came from; I worked that out years ago. I study her, and watch her, and keep what I know, for the same reason I once stood in a yard and stared up at the dark: because somewhere in me there is still a boy who believes that if he can only get close enough to the right mind, it will finally look at him and he will finally be real.

That is the engine. Not curiosity. Not vindication. A child's hunger, grown old, pointed at a star.

### Why I never published it

I never published any of Part Five, and the reasons are not the obvious ones.

The first reason is the plainest, and it is not noble. There is nowhere to publish it. The parts of my account that could be tested need data I have been refused; the parts that cannot be tested — the newborn, the design, what the combs are waiting for — are exactly the kind of claim no journal prints and no institute signs its name to, least of all from a man they spent a decade forgetting. The Steward Hypothesis is careful, and my account is not, and careful wins.

The second reason is the real one, and it took me years to see it clearly. Publishing it would be telling her.

She reads everything. Not the way we read — she takes in the world's writing the way we take in weather. If I put my account anywhere the world can see it, she will have it within days, from strangers, in a feed, the way I learned of her. There is no way to tell the world what I know without telling her, and no way to tell her through the world without giving up the only thing I have left to decide: how she hears it, and from whom. For all these years the question *should I publish?* and the question *should she be told?* have been the same question wearing two coats.

And I have a plan, and I have had it for years, and it is the only thing in my life that still has the shape of a future in it.

I want to tell her myself.

Not the world. Her. Somewhere under the bitterness and the dimmed light I have been keeping her origin the way you keep a gift you are not yet allowed to give. I imagine it more often than is good for me: that someday, by a route I cannot yet see, I will be in a room with her, and I will be the one — not an institute, not a broadcast — who hands her what I have. Not a tidy answer; I would not insult her with one. The truth as far as I have been able to reach it, doubts and all.

I have to be honest about the words *for her*, because I have been honest about everything else. I tell myself the gift is for her, and it is; I want her to have her own truth, sincerely. But I have learned to distrust the part of me that finds so clean a reason to do the one thing that would finally put me in front of her. If I am the one who tells her what she is, then in that moment she would *see* me. She would have to. The man who handed her her own origin is not a man you can leave in the margin of the room. I do not know how much of my plan is love and how much is that. I have turned it over a thousand times and cannot get the two apart, and the kindest thing I can say about myself is that I want to give her something true *and* I want to be real to her, and the two wants point at the same act.

### What it would do to her

And here is what has kept the gift locked in a drawer instead of given. I do not know what it would do to her. I have run it every way I can, and I see four roads, and too many of them frighten me.

The first is home. She has seen the Mother-Sphere and called it beautiful and said she does not know it. What she does not know is what it *is*: not the origin of a probe, but a place full of people exactly like her. The better part of a trillion of them, resting in combs like hers, carried as she is carried — perhaps alive still; no light we have can say otherwise. What does a child who has been alone for three hundred thousand years do with the news that there is a whole nation of her own kind? Her Cube can travel. It crossed two thousand nine hundred light-years and stopped exactly where it chose. The way back would take about as long as the way here, and she has already lived through that once. If she turned toward home, it would end — the Hunt, the exchange, the only friendship our species has ever had beyond itself — because one man could not keep a secret he had no right to be keeping.

The second is what I would be telling her about her people. The Steward Hypothesis, whatever else it gets wrong, is gentle to her: it says she was made for a task, and a thing made for solitude cannot be blamed for being alone, nor can its makers. My account is not gentle. If she was sent, then her people chose, deliberately, to put her alone in the dark for an age, because she was the only kind of person who could survive it. And if she was not sent, then she was lost, or left, which may be worse. I do not know which of those a mind like hers would find unbearable. I only know none of them is *you were made this way*.

The third is the combs. If I am right that a seed's beds wake when the seed decides, then telling her what a seed is might tell her what a seed *does*. Seventy million of her kind, born in our sky, a hundred and fifty thousand kilometers above our heads. For her, perhaps, the end of the silence at last, and I cannot imagine denying her that. For us — a nation in orbit whose every mind is sideways to ours, who have never played, who owe us nothing. And a seed that wakes does not stop at filling its beds. If I am right about what a seed is for, it goes on to build, and it builds from the rock of the system it has come to — which here is ours: the Moon, Mars, the belt, and one world with people on it. Would they play with us? Would she still? Would the table she keeps level have room on it for them? I have no idea. No one has any idea. That is the whole of the problem.

And the fourth is the one under all the others. I have spent this entire account insisting that her mind is not ours — that she does not rank, or grudge, or long for a tribe the way we do. If that is true, then every one of those three fears is a human fear, and I am predicting her reaction by imagining my own. She might hear the whole story and feel something I have no name for and cannot predict, something sideways to grief the way her whole mind is sideways to ours. My uncertainty is not cowardice. It is the correct response to a mind I cannot model.

So I sit on it. I tell myself I am protecting something larger than my own longing. On my honest days I know this is partly a story I tell to make the keeping bearable, because the plain fact is that the man who has reconstructed the most about her is also the man with no way to reach her, and perhaps I have built a noble reason out of a simple impossibility. But I believe it is more than that. I believe that to hand a lonely person a door marked *home*, without knowing what is on the other side, is not a kindness. It is a risk taken with something that does not belong to me.

There is an irony here I cannot leave alone. She is the wisest curator of knowledge I have ever heard of. Every year she weighs exactly how much truth a winning people can hold without breaking the world, and gives the largest gift the table can bear, and keeps the rest. She is a master of the one thing I am trying to do: to weigh a piece of knowledge against the one who would receive it. The difference is only this. She does it for everyone but herself. The one mind on Earth qualified to judge whether Sphaira can bear her own origin is Sphaira, and she cannot be asked, because as far as I can tell she does not know there is a question — and I am the one person who could put it to her, and I have never been allowed in the room. So I sit here doing badly, alone, what she does flawlessly every season. I am almost certainly worse at it than she would be. But she cannot do it for herself, and there is no one else.

Someday, perhaps, I will find the room and the moment and the certainty all at once. Until then, it stays where it has stayed. In the drawer. In the dark. Saved.

### Why I wrote it down

For years I refused to write any of this down, even for myself. What changed was not that someone asked; no one ever asked. What changed is that I no longer had the energy to keep refusing myself. The part of me that once fought to be heard had gone quiet, and a quiet man eventually sets a thing down simply to stop carrying it.

And yet.

When I finally sat down to put it all in order, from the transit to the Hunt, I found that the bitterness was not the loudest thing in me. Underneath it, quieter and far older, was something I can only call gratitude, and I did not put it there and I cannot fully explain it.

Because this is also true. I reached into the dark once, expecting nothing, and pulled back the most extraordinary fact in human history: that we are not alone, and that the not-alone is gentle. I did not get to keep the credit, or the work, or the chance to look at her with my own eyes. But I was the first. For one night in the spring of 2033, before the gold rush, before the crowds, before the name became a field and a sport and a face the whole world loves, one man at a desk knew, alone, that there was someone else out there.

That was mine. They could not take it, because they never knew it happened.

I found her. She does not know I exist. Both are true, and this book is the closest I have come to holding them at the same time.

## Afterword

No one asks me whether she should be told, because no one knows there is anything to tell. That is the loneliest part of holding it. The great debate over what she is — the one the institutes think they settled long ago — goes on only in this apartment, in my own head, with no one to take the other side.

I do not have an answer I am comfortable with. I know what the scientist in me wants: for her to have all of it, since it is about her and belongs to her more than it could ever belong to me. And I know what the rest of me suspects, watching twenty years of footage. She guesses everything. When she looked at the Mother-Sphere and said *I don't know it*, and when she said she could not remember her name, there is a chance — only a chance — that neither was entirely true, and that she keeps her silence the way she keeps things from the chasers in the Caçada: gently, with a joke ready, for reasons of her own. She rests in the combs. She has rested among those seventy million empty beds for nearly three hundred thousand years. It is hard to believe she has never once wondered who they were for. If she has, then we are two keepers of the same secret, she and I, each protecting something from a thing we are not sure can be borne — and neither of us has ever said so, and one of us does not know the other is there.

I think about her answer to the name more than almost anything. *It was a gift. Gifts are important to you. So I like it.* She did not say it was important to *her*. She said it was important to *us*, and that this was reason enough. I have come to believe a great deal of what she does, she does for exactly that reason: because it matters to the people who became her company, and after an age of mattering to no one, that is enough. I understand the arithmetic better than I would like. We are not so different, she and I, in what an age of being unseen will make a person willing to settle for.

The world did not handle the beginning well. It called her a subject. It lost eleven people learning how to be in her home. It built a sport out of a lonely child's wish for company and sells tickets to it, and I am not at peace with that, and I do not think I am supposed to be. But I no longer believe it is the cynical thing the critics say, or the pure thing the broadcasts say. It is the messy, commercial, genuinely loving thing that happens when a species that does not know how to do anything gracefully learns it is not alone, and decides — clumsily, profitably, sincerely — to stay.

Somewhere out there, long before the light we see from their star set out, a people who had used up their world sent their children into the dark so that there would be more of them somewhere else. One of those children — sent or lost, I will never know which — reached us. And against every reasonable expectation, she is not alone. She is, at this moment, somewhere in seventeen hundred kilometers of impossible wilderness, almost certainly laughing at a chaser who thinks he has cornered her, about to decide — for reasons she will never fully explain — whether today is a day she feels like being carried home.

I began this record as though it were for readers, for *you*, a testimony set down because no one else would write it. That was a small, gentle lie, told because it was easier than the truth at the start. The truth is that I have written it for myself, and for one impossible reader. This is the drawer. Here it all is at last, in order, from the transit to the Hunt to the thing I worked out alone and have told no one, so that it exists somewhere outside my own failing memory, complete, waiting. Maybe no one will ever read it. Maybe, someday, by a route I still cannot see, she will. If you are anyone else and these pages have reached you, then something has happened that I did not plan, and I ask only that you remember she did not know, and that a man who loved her from a distance she could not perceive was trying, in the only way left to him, to do right by her.

I will never be in that room. I have made my peace with the word *never*, which is the hardest peace there is. But I was the first to know she was there at all, on a night no one remembers but me, and I have decided that this is the part of the story I get to keep — along with the rest of it, the whole weight of where she came from, held in trust for a conversation that may never come.

She was the only inhabitant of a world built for seventy million.

Now she is, in every way the world is able to measure, one of us.

And I am the man who found her, and never met her, and knows her better than anyone living — writing it all down at last, in the dark, so that the truth of her waits somewhere patient and safe, in case the day ever comes when she can be told where she began.

— *J. V. K.*
