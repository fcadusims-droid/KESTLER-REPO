# Backstory

Everything here happened before the game begins. Character profiles for the people named are in [Cast](cast.md).

## The father

**John Spaid** was a professional GT3 driver.

GT3 is a real category of competition car: race machinery built to a regulation, rear-wheel drive, equalised against rival makes by Balance of Performance, and campaigned by customer teams across national and international championships. John spent his career in that world — contracts, sponsor liveries, endurance grids. A job rather than a fantasy.

**GT3 exists nowhere inside the playable world**, neither in Nitroit nor in Revada. It is the tier above both: the level Jake's father worked at and the level the ending sends Jake to. The player never drives at it. It appears in the game as history and as a destination, and the player sees it exactly twice: in what happened to John Spaid, and in the final scene.

In his last season John raced in the **LMGT3 class of the FIA World Endurance Championship** — the real-world class in which GT3 cars contest the WEC, and whose drivers race for the **FIA Endurance Trophy for LMGT3 Drivers**, the drivers' prize of the world championship's GT3 class ([References](REFERENCES.md)). He shared his car with two co-drivers, the two former teammates who would later spend five years telling Jake to race ([Cast](cast.md#the-spaids)). In the WEC a crew shares its points, so the three of them led the standings together into the final round, and John was driving the final stint of the final race, in the lead.

**He died on the final lap of that race, holding first place, with the trophy won on the road** — finishing would have taken it. He never took the flag. The race classified his car by the distance it had covered, behind every crew that completed the last lap, and the points that left it were not enough: the Endurance Trophy went to the crew that had been second in the standings. It was never awarded to him, or to the two men who shared his car.

The game takes place in the early 2030s; John died in the late 2020s.

## The son

**Jake Spaid was going to be a professional driver, and everybody knew it.**

- He started in karts at seven and was winning by nine.
- By thirteen he was a junior everyone in the paddock had already heard of — the kind of driver people expect to see on television in ten years. He had his father's job lined up before he had a driving licence, and he wanted it.
- **He was fourteen when John Spaid died.** He did not race again: not in karts, not in juniors, not anywhere. The seat he had been promised went to somebody else, and he did not ask about it.

### Five years

He lived with his mother, **Grace Spaid**, for five more years. She never pushed him back toward racing and never suggested he was wasting anything, which made her the only person in his life who did not.

**Everybody else pushed**: his father's old team principal, two of his father's former teammates, an uncle, family friends who had watched him win at nine. Every conversation at every family occasion, for five years, arrived at the same place: *you should be racing, Jake, your father would have wanted it.*

**Grace Spaid died of cancer when Jake was nineteen.** He is an only child. Everything came to him: the house, the savings, and the entire career earnings of a professional driver who had been paid well to race for twenty years.

### The inheritance

He sold the house. Every cent of it, and every cent of his father's money, went into one account, and he left it there.

**He does not consider it his.** John earned that money over twenty years at the level Jake was supposed to reach and did not. Jake was given the talent, the head start and the seat, and walked away from all three. In his own reckoning, taking the money would mean accepting the inheritance while rejecting everything that produced it. *He has not got the right to it* — that is the sentence in his head. No one has ever talked him out of it, because he has never said it out loud.

He took **$25,000** — enough to leave on, and nothing more. Not an allowance and not a stake: **the one exception he allowed himself**, the cost of getting away. He has been spending it down ever since — on coaches, hostels and, eventually, the first car he buys in Nitroit — and he will never take another cent ([Campaign](campaign.md#what-the-purchase-establishes)).

### Getting to Nitroit

He did not buy a car. He left on a bus, with a bag. Over the following months he crossed the country the slow way: long-distance coaches, terminals at four in the morning, and, more than anything, walking — small towns, service roads, the edges of highways. He was not going anywhere; he was putting distance between himself and everyone who had an opinion about what he owed his father.

**He stopped in Nitroit because nobody there had ever heard of John Spaid.** He got off a coach at the downtown terminal and checked into a hostel on the edge of the Old Quarter, because it was the cheapest bed within walking distance.

**When the game starts he has been there a few weeks, and $15,000 is left.**

## The money

**The inherited account is not in the game.** It has no menu, no balance readout, no interface and no button. The player cannot see it, transfer from it, borrow against it or interact with it at any point, in either half of the game. There is no unlock, late-game option or hidden path to it.

It exists as fact, not as a system: it is referenced in dialogue, known to exist, and is the reason Jake is in a hostel in a rust-belt city with $15,000 instead of anywhere else.

**Why it is closed:**

- **It is not a decision the game is entitled to reopen.** Jake made it alone, at nineteen, in the weeks after his mother's funeral. A withdraw button would turn a finished piece of character into a standing temptation and tell the player he is still arguing with himself about it. He is not. That is what makes it a scar rather than a dilemma.
- **The economy depends on there being no reservoir.** Impound fees, shop credit, wagers, rent and property, the ten festival salaries and the five legal festival cars are real constraints only because nothing stands behind them. There is $15,000 and whatever Jake wins.

| Where the player encounters it | How |
|---|---|
| **Dialogue** | Jake mentions it twice in the entire campaign: once to Nadia Ferrante in Act 4, and once to the mechanic assigned to Grip during the Epilogue. Both times briefly, neither time as a possibility, and neither time saying what it is or how much — the listener learns only that there is money and that it is not his. |
| **The ending** | Never resolved. The game does not say what happens to it. |
| **Everywhere else** | Not at all. |

## Why he wins

Jake is not a natural who discovered racing in Nitroit. **He is a trained junior driver who stopped at fourteen and started again at nineteen in a place with no scrutineers.**

He arrives already knowing how to find a limit, read a corner, stay smooth under pressure, and feel whether a car is working. What he does not know is the city, the people, the police, or how to build a car with his own hands. Act 1 is him being outdriven by worse drivers in better cars while he learns all four.

The festival is therefore not a step up in skill for him. It is a step back into a place he already knows how to work. What he must learn there is not driving but everything around it: the rulebook, the mechanic, the ten people, and the fact that his name is not the only one on the entry.

## The inversion

This is the load-bearing fact of the game, and it is deliberately ironic: **the only death in this fiction happened in the sanctioned, regulated, scrutineered, marshalled environment** — the one Jake's own reasoning tells him is the safer of the two.

Jake knows this intellectually and inverts it emotionally. After the funeral he never returns to a closed circuit. He goes to the streets instead, because there he believes he controls the variables: he knows the roads, the racers, and when the police patrol. Control is the lie he tells himself; familiarity is what he actually has.

**What the avoidance covers.** Not laps, corners or racing in circles — the city has lap-based street events and he runs them without difficulty. He avoids the **apparatus**: a permanent circuit, grandstands, an organising body, scrutineering, official timing, marshal posts, a classification sheet with his name on it. His father died inside that apparatus.

The distinction is precise: a multi-lap loop through The Heights at 3 a.m. is not a scene about his trauma; standing outside a festival gate is.
