# CALL OF DUTY: WAR SERPENT
### A World Bible

*A story set within the world of Call of Duty Zombies, reimagined with substantial creative liberty by its creator, Johnny Kestler. Familiar names and fragments of established history appear here alongside a mythology, cosmology, and cast built entirely for this telling — nothing that follows should be mistaken for an attempt to represent the existing Zombies canon as it stands elsewhere.*

---

## I. THE NAME

There is no single moment when the name *War Serpent* was chosen. It was assembled, the way everything in this world is assembled — out of fragments, misheard reports, and the last transmissions of soldiers who did not live long enough to be believed.

Radio operators across three different American deployments, separated by decades, used the same phrase to describe the thing they could not explain: a war that eats its own tail. A war with no beginning anyone can find and no end anyone can imagine. A serpent, coiled around the whole of human history, feeding forever on what it has already killed.

The people who live there now — the ones with enough mind left to name anything — do not call it War Serpent. They call the world itself **Oroboros**, and they have called it that for longer than any of them can measure. War Serpent is the operational designation given to it afterward, on the other side, by the people who found a way back out.

---

## II. THE PREMISE

Oroboros shares the geometry of the living Earth — the same coastlines, the same mountain ranges, a second globe folded directly beneath the one humanity walks on — because it was never a separate creation. It is the Earth itself, the same Earth every living person stands on, corrupted rather than replaced: not a mirror world built to resemble the original, but the original, still there, still the same planet, turned into a place where every war humanity has ever fought, and every war it has not yet fought as of the year 2045, is happening at once, forever, without pause and without resolution.

It is not another dimension in the sense of an alien plane with alien rules. It is *this* Earth, wearing the last moment of every violent death as a permanent, standing present. When a person dies inside the machinery of a war — on the beaches of Normandy, on the walls of a Roman frontier fort, in the collapse of an apartment block in a city no one on the other side of the world could name, in a firefight fought last month in the calendar of the living world — some of them, not all, and not by any rule the living world would recognize as fair, open their eyes again in that place. Not healed. Not new. Continuing.

The dead do not rest on Oroboros. They redeploy.

In the summer of 2045, in the third year of the conflict the United States government still refuses to formally name a World War, a classified Department of Defense initiative operating under the cover designation **Project Phantom Corridor** attempted to tear open a controlled rift into what its own paperwork called an adjacent volume.

The paperwork was not honest, but it was not exactly a lie either. What Phantom Corridor sold to the committees that funded it was logistics: a rapid-deployment corridor that would let American forces enter and exit any theater on Earth faster than any enemy could reposition to meet them, a door that let you leave from Texas and arrive in the Pacific before the other side's radar finished its sweep. That application was real, in the sense that the men who wrote it believed it would eventually be possible. It was also invented after the fact, by a program that had already found something it did not understand and needed a reason to be allowed to keep looking at it.

The anomaly came first. Seven years before Phantom Corridor had a name, a deep-listening array built for something else entirely registered a structure underneath the world — not a signal, not an object, a *seam*, present at every point on the planet simultaneously and equally, indifferent to distance in a way nothing in physics is supposed to be indifferent to distance. The corridor pitch was written to explain why a government at war should pay to open it. Nobody involved seriously believed the seam had been built by anyone. It was too old and too evenly distributed for that. What they believed, and what turned out to be exactly correct in the worst possible way, was that it had never been reached.

The rift did not stabilize. It did not behave as a corridor. It behaved as a mouth, and for the eleven seconds it remained open, it swallowed most of the people standing close enough to the containment field to be caught in it — researchers, security detail, technicians mid-shift, more than a dozen in total — and closed itself around all of them at once. Not everyone within reach of it was taken, and the pattern of who was not is the single most important thing anyone in that building could have noticed, and nobody did.

They are, as far as anyone left in the world above knows, dead. A containment failure buried under a classification most of the surviving research staff never had clearance to name. More than a dozen notifications sent to more than a dozen families, care of a government that could not tell the truth about Phantom Corridor even if it wanted to.

They are not dead. They are simply the latest arrivals in a world built entirely from the dead — and, for as long as any of them last, the only things standing on it that are still alive.

## III. THE MAN THIS HAPPENS TO

### 3.1 Staff Sergeant Marcus Deshawn Reeves

He is twenty-eight years old, U.S. Army infantry, assigned to Phantom Corridor's security detachment for reasons that had nothing to do with the science and everything to do with the fact that the program needed men who would not ask what was in the room.

He is Black, raised in a mid-sized American city by a single mother who worked two jobs for most of his childhood and was rarely home before dark. He does not talk about his father, and there is nothing to say — the man left before Marcus could form a memory of him, and by the time Marcus was old enough to notice the absence, he had already decided it was not a wound worth reopening. What filled the space his mother's work hours left behind was the street, and by thirteen he had learned it well enough to survive in it: the arithmetic of who to avoid, what to carry, how to make himself look like more trouble than he was worth to anyone thinking about starting something.

He never once used any of it as a reason to put distance between himself and his mother. Whatever he learned to do outside her door, he still came home to her every night he could, still gave her most of what he made even when what he made came from things he would rather she not ask about, still put himself between her and anyone or anything that looked like a threat to her — a habit that started before he was old enough to understand what he was actually protecting her from and never once stopped. She fought him on the life he was building for himself the entire time, every year of it, and never once succeeded, and never once stopped trying either.

He turned eighteen three months before his second arrest, and that one held. Two years served, most of it spent doing the kind of quiet, disciplined work that gets noticed by the right people for the wrong reasons. One of the corrections officers on his block, a former Army NCO who saw something in him worth not writing off, spent those two years pushing him toward a door most of the men on that block never got shown — a service program that could turn a sentence into an enlistment, if Marcus could prove the discipline was real and not just good behavior with an expiration date.

He proved it. The Army took the version of him the street had already built — watchful, loyal past the point of self-interest, incapable of leaving people behind — and gave it a uniform and a purpose that finally matched what he had already been doing his whole life without anyone calling it that.

He is the most physically capable of the survivors, and the one for whom staying alive is the smallest problem he has on Oroboros. Almost everything else about him is a problem.

What he cannot do is stop protecting people who cannot be protected anymore. On a world with no rear echelon, no home to report back to, and no mother waiting up for him this time, the instinct that made him a good son and a better soldier — the refusal to ever, under any circumstance, let the people near him go undefended — is the first thing Oroboros recognizes in him, and the first thing it starts working on. Everything on this planet is a soldier who could not stop. He is a man who cannot stop putting himself in front of things. The distance between those two sentences is smaller than he would like, and it gets smaller every month he is there.

He is also, in the specific and technical sense the place cares about, the wrong kind of soldier — and that is the fact that eventually matters more than any other thing about him. Oroboros is built out of men who died with the killing being the truest thing left in them. Marcus has killed, and will kill a great deal more before this is over, and none of it was ever the point. He has spent his entire life fighting *toward* something on the other side of the fighting. There is no other side here. Watching what this world does to men who never had one is the closest thing to a warning he ever gets, and he spends most of his time on Oroboros failing to understand that the warning is addressed to him.

Marcus is the one whose eyes the story is told through. He is not the smartest of the survivors, not the most senior, not the one who understands what is happening to them. He is the one it is happening to.

### 3.2 Dr. Elena Farro

Thirty-two, Phantom Corridor's lead field theorist on rift stability, and the only person in the program who was correct before the rift opened.

She did not volunteer. Her father, a two-star general with his own career staked on the project's results, brought her onto it over her explicit objection — she had seen enough of the preliminary containment data to believe, correctly, that whatever the seam actually connected to was not going to behave like a corridor. She said so. She was overruled, and then quietly informed, through channels she was never meant to trace back to her father directly, that a scientist of her caliber declining a program of this classification would not simply end her own career. It would draw exactly the kind of attention her family, and specifically her five-year-old son, could not afford her father's enemies to take an interest in.

She is a widow. Her husband died in a highway accident two years before Phantom Corridor, and her son, Jack, is the only reason she has ever needed for anything since. She said yes afraid, not persuaded, and she carried that fear through the rift with her, sharper on the other side than it ever was in a lab.

She is a practicing Christian, quietly and without performance. On Oroboros this turns out to matter more than any of her credentials, and not for any reason she would have predicted. She is the worst-equipped of the survivors for physical survival and the best-equipped for understanding what is actually happening to all of them, and her work there follows a single unbroken line downward: every answer she finds makes her more certain that Phantom Corridor did not create Oroboros, and more afraid of what that means about what has been waiting underneath human history the entire time.

To Marcus she is, for most of their time there, the person he is protecting. That is how he understands her, because that is how he understands most people. It takes him a long time to notice that she is also the only one of them still doing something Oroboros has no mechanism to produce, and longer still to understand that this is the thing actually keeping the four of them alive.

### 3.3 Thomas "Whit" Whitfield

Thirty-nine, civilian contractor attached to Phantom Corridor's hardware division, and the only one of the four who never wore a uniform.

His family was never the kind that held together on its own. Money was tight for most of his adult life, tighter after his wife, Carol, was diagnosed with a chronic illness that never quite goes into full remission and never quite gets bad enough to stop being something they manage rather than something they solve. They have three kids, two boys and a girl, the oldest fourteen, and none of the ordinary stability other families seem to get handed for free.

What they had instead was Whit. He picked up every shift he could get, learned to fix anything that broke before it cost money they did not have to replace it, kept Carol's treatment schedule straight in his head better than any of her doctors did, and made sure his kids never once had to know exactly how close to the edge the family actually lived, because that was his job, the same as any of the fabrication work that paid the bills. Nobody taught him to be the load-bearing wall of his own household. He simply became it, the way he becomes it for anything else that is coming apart, because someone always has to and it was never going to be anyone but him.

He understands machines, fortification, and the physics of holding a structure together under stress. On Oroboros this makes him something no one there has been in a very long time, and it is not a comfortable thing to be. Every faction on that planet lives off salvage. Nothing on it is manufactured. Whitfield is a man who can *make* things, standing in a world that has spent several hundred thousand years exclusively taking things apart, and once that fact becomes known — and it does become known, faster than any of them would like — it stops being a skill and starts being a reason for armies to want him.

He and Marcus understand each other immediately and almost entirely without conversation, which is not the same as agreeing. Both of them are men who hold things up. The difference, and it becomes an argument neither of them ever wins, is that Marcus holds people and Whitfield holds structures, and Oroboros will eventually force each of them to choose which one they actually meant.

### 3.4 Colonel Desmond Ashgrove

Forty-six, U.S. Army, Phantom Corridor's ranking officer and the man who signed off on the live test.

He grew up in a household where letting anything slip out of control was not a mistake you were allowed to make twice. His father, a career officer himself, ran the family the way he ran everything else — a chain of command, a house where chaos was the one thing never tolerated, because chaos was what happened to people who had not planned well enough to prevent it. Desmond was taught to lead before he was taught much else: how to read a room and take charge of it, how to make a decision under pressure and never let anyone see the pressure, how command is less about giving orders than about being the one person in any given room who has already decided what happens next before anyone else has finished being afraid.

He is married, with two children mostly grown, and he has run that household the same way his father ran his — competently, protectively, and with a level of control his wife has spent two decades gently and unsuccessfully trying to talk him down from. He is a good husband and a good father by any measure that matters to him, and he has never had to ask himself whether that is the same skill as the one that makes him a good officer, because on the world he came from, the two were never tested against each other.

He signed off on the live test, and nothing about Oroboros lets him off easily for it.

His first instinct, arriving in the middle of a war with an inexhaustible supply of combatants, is correct in its own cold logic: this is not a horror to escape, it is the largest strategic resource that has ever existed. What makes Ashgrove genuinely dangerous, rather than merely wrong, is that Oroboros agrees with him — and proves it, personally, in his fourteenth month there, in a way none of the others can talk him out of because all three of them watch it happen.

He is the mirror Marcus spends the longest refusing to look into. Both of them believe, at the root, that the way you keep people safe is by standing between them and the thing that wants them. The only difference is scale, and Oroboros spends every day either of them survives there quietly demonstrating that scale is the whole of it.

### 3.5 What They Carry, and How Long It Lasts

Only Farro arrives on Oroboros already a believer. Marcus was raised in a house that went to church twice a year and stopped entirely once he enlisted; Whitfield thinks about God the way he thinks about weather in a country he has never visited, real presumably and none of his business; Ashgrove treats the question as a category error, something for men with less to actually control.

None of that stays fixed. It is where they start, not where the place leaves them, and each of the three moves toward whatever they come to believe by a different road, at a different pace, for reasons that have nothing to do with anything Farro sets out to do on purpose.

What matters more, and what none of them understand for the first several months, is that everything they carried in is on a clock. Whatever it is in a person that makes another person matter more than the arithmetic, Oroboros does not remove it and does not have to. It simply starves it, at a rate nobody has ever survived long enough to outlast, for reasons set out further on in this account.

The four are not exempt. They are simply early. What they are actually racing, underneath the war and the salvage and the question of how to get home, is the rate at which the thing that makes them recognizably themselves is quietly draining out of them — and the first symptom, in every documented case, is that the person it is happening to feels perfectly fine.

## IV. THE PHYSICS OF THE PLACE

### 4.1 Geography, and the Absence of Anachronism

Oroboros shares the Earth's geography. Its Italy is Italy-shaped. Its Pacific is exactly as wide as the real Pacific. Marcus can read a real map and understand, in general terms, where he is standing, even as everything about who holds that ground and what waits on it obeys none of the rules the map used to promise.

Time on Oroboros runs the way time runs anywhere. There is one clock, one direction, one sequence of days, and every soul on the planet moves through it together. Centuries pass. Ground changes hands in an order that can be reconstructed. A soldier who has been there four hundred years has been there four hundred more years than one who arrived last spring, and both of them are standing in the same afternoon.

What that clock does not do is match the one above it. Time on Oroboros runs faster than time on the living Earth — not by any ratio anyone there has ever fixed precisely, but by something close to ten to one, which is why a planet fed by a species only a few hundred thousand years old has accumulated a depth that feels geological. A man killed in a war the living world remembers inside a single lifetime has been on Oroboros for three centuries. Nothing there perceives the discrepancy, and nothing about it can be measured from the inside. The four work it out slowly, from arrival dates and from what newer soldiers still have on them, and Farro is the one who understands first what it means for anyone still waiting on the other side.

What does not exist on Oroboros is the *sense* of anachronism. A Roman legionary does not experience himself as ancient. A soldier drawn from a war the living Earth has not yet fought does not experience himself as being from the future. Whatever internal mechanism allows a person to locate their own war on a timeline relative to other wars did not survive the crossing, in anyone, ever. Every soldier on that planet understands exactly one temporal fact: the war in front of him is the war that is happening. Anything that contradicts that — a man in armor that no century he recognizes could have forged, a machine crossing a ridge that has no business existing — does not register as evidence of a larger history. It registers as an enemy, or an asset, and nothing else.

This is what makes the landscape more than a visual curiosity. A Roman line and an armored column do not meet each other as past meeting future. They meet as two forces that each understand the other to be simply *here*, in the only war either of them has ever been in, and neither of them will ever ask the question that would explain the other.

The terrain records what the inhabitants cannot. Cities are not preserved as museum pieces of their worst day; they are actively and continuously fought over, which makes the physical ground of Oroboros a palimpsest — a Roman road cutting through a trench line cutting through the collapsed shell of a building leveled in a war fought decades into the future of the century that built it. Ground gets taken so many times, by so many forces from so many centuries, that its surface geology is less like a battlefield and more like a wound that has scarred over a thousand times without ever fully healing.

The oldest inhabitants can read those layers. It is the only history anyone on Oroboros has, and it belongs to the dirt rather than to any person standing on it.

### 4.2 The Return and the Scattering

Nobody who belongs to Oroboros stays dead. That is not the same as saying nobody is killed, and the distance between those two facts is the entire mechanism of the place.

A body on Oroboros can be shot, burned, crushed, or taken apart over an afternoon for no reason beyond whatever the killing gave the one doing it, and that body dies exactly the way any body dies — completely, with nothing left in it afterward that could be called a person. What does not die is whatever the body was carrying. Somewhere on Oroboros, the same person claws up out of the ground again, alive in whatever sense anyone there is alive, remembering everything that was done to them and nothing at all about how they got to where they are standing.

**Where** the ground gives them back is the single most important rule on the planet, and it is not random.

A soldier returns near his war. Not the exact place he fell — never that, and no one has ever found a case of it — but within reach of the front he belongs to, close enough that walking back to it is possible if he survives the walk. This is called the Return, and it is not a mercy. It is the reason the war can exist at all. Without it there would be no fronts, no units, no held ground, nothing but a planet of individuals scattered by every death into permanent, meaningless dispersal. The Return is what lets an army stay an army across centuries of casualties: its dead keep coming back to it, footsore and disoriented and short whatever they were carrying when they died, and it absorbs them again, and the line holds.

The walk itself is part of the suffering, and every soldier on Oroboros has made it more times than the concept of counting still applies to. Days of it, sometimes weeks, across ground held by forces that have no reason not to kill him again on the way — which they do, which sends him back to the ground, which starts the walk over. There are soldiers on Oroboros who have spent more of their existence walking back to a war than fighting in it.

Civilians do not get the Return. A civilian belongs to no front, and the ground has nothing to align them to, so a civilian killed on Oroboros comes back wherever the planet happens to put them: a woman who died outside Moscow reappearing in a jungle on the other side of the world, with no language in common with anything nearby and no idea which direction anything is. This is the single largest reason civilian existence on Oroboros is worse than soldier existence in every practical sense, and the reason no civilian has ever succeeded in building anything permanent out of a group of specific people. Every death is a permanent exile from everyone they knew.

What survives among civilians is therefore never a group. It is a place. A shelter that has been continuously occupied for six hundred years has been occupied by several thousand different people, almost none of whom ever met the ones before them, each arriving to find a fire already lit and the same handful of rules scratched into the same wall. Margin-Dweller history is not handed down. It is left behind — in the ground, on the walls, in the caches — and picked up by whoever the planet drops there next. This is why their traditions are old and their communities are not.

And then there is the Scattering, which is what Oroboros does when a war starts to look like it might end.

The Return is not a promise. It is a tendency, and the tendency degrades. When any single force holds ground too completely for too long — when the fighting on a stretch of the planet starts to resolve into something that could eventually be called control, and then peace — the distance of the Return begins to stretch. Its dead come back a little further out. Then further. Then not within walking distance at all, then on another continent, then indistinguishably from the way civilians come back, which is to say anywhere. The army does not lose a battle. It simply stops being replenished, and starts bleeding its own soldiers outward across the planet at the exact rate it takes casualties, until what was an empire holding a peninsula is a few thousand men holding a road and several hundred thousand strangers scattered across ground they will never walk back from.

Nothing announces this. No enemy arrives to deliver it. The soldiers experiencing it cannot perceive it, because perceiving it would require holding two facts side by side across years, and no soldier on Oroboros can hold anything across years. Only the oldest civilians have ever noticed the pattern, and what they have concluded from it is the closest thing Oroboros has to a law of nature:

*The ground gives you back to your war. It stops when your war stops being one.*

It resumes, too, the moment that ground is contested again. This is not a reprieve. An army that has been Scattered for twenty years gets its Return back and does not get its men back, and the distance between those two sentences is the reason no force on Oroboros has ever recovered from winning.

This is not enforcement. There is no one on the other end of it deciding, no correction dispatched, no intelligence selecting targets. It is a property of the world, in the way weather is a property of a world — total, indifferent, and impossible to appeal. Oroboros holds war the way a bowl holds water. Anything that starts to become peace runs out through the bottom.

### 4.3 The Rule of Endless Front

There is no rear on Oroboros. There is no safe zone maintained by any army, because no army on Oroboros is capable of winning, and this is the mechanical law from which the rest of the place follows.

An army can take ground anywhere on the planet, regardless of what century built it or what century holds the ground beside it. Nothing here is regional. Nothing clusters conveniently by era the way a tidy war map might suggest it should. An army built out of gunpowder-era regulars can hold ground bordering a force from a war fought three hundred years later. A force that died fighting in mountains can end up defending a coastline none of them ever saw in life. The only thing that determines where a force ends up is where the ground first gave back enough of its soldiers to constitute a front, and after that, where it can push.

**Every war fought on the living Earth feeds Oroboros a new army.** This is what makes the endlessness literal rather than thematic. As long as humanity keeps making war, Oroboros's supply is inexhaustible, replenished from every century simultaneously, arriving with the weapons and doctrine of the moment of death — up through the newest arrivals of 2045, wearing the same war still being fought on the living Earth the day the four cross over, and still carrying full magazines, which makes them for a little while the most dangerous thing on the planet and guarantees that everyone on it comes looking for them.

The **Ninth That Would Not Die** — a Roman legion whose soldiers took the name informally, after an old and half-true legend from the world above — held the Italian peninsula for a little over four hundred years. It did not hold it through superior arms. Around the second century of that hold, a third of it was carrying weapons taken off armies from wars two thousand years past its own; by the third century almost none of it was, because the ammunition had run out and the legion had gone back to iron, which is the only thing on Oroboros that never stops working. It held because its Return was tight, its discipline never dissolved, and every man it lost came walking back within the month.

It fell because it won. Not a battle — the peninsula. Around the four-hundredth year, the last force capable of contesting it collapsed, and for something close to two decades, there was a stretch of ground on Oroboros where nothing was actively being fought. The legion did not celebrate. Its soldiers had no capacity for the concept. It simply kept patrolling ground nobody was taking, and its dead — because men still died, of wounds, of accidents, of each other — stopped coming back. First a hundred miles out. Then across the sea. Then nowhere anyone could name. Twenty years of quiet reduced four hundred years of legion to a few thousand men holding a road, and when the next force arrived, the ground was already gone.

What was left did not vanish. It scattered — reduced, deteriorating, absorbed piecemeal into whatever other broken units it could still recognize as kin, the way everything on Oroboros scatters instead of ending. Fragments of the Ninth are still fighting on four continents, in units that have no idea what the men beside them used to be part of.

**Captured ground means captured arsenals.** When a force falls, what it was carrying does not vanish with it. The soldiers who take the ground take the weapons left on it, and Oroboros's dead need no training manuals to understand what they have taken — the same battle-instinct that lets a legionary read a firing line reads a rifle's function on sight. It is not unusual, on ground that has changed hands a dozen times to a dozen unrelated forces, to find Roman regulars in salvaged plate working a captured autocannon, or infantry from one war carrying swords they took off soldiers from a war that had nothing to do with theirs.

Nothing on Oroboros stays in its own century for long. The front does not just move the map. It cannibalizes it, and every occupying force looks less like the army it used to be with every stretch of ground it takes.

### 4.4 The Long Decay

Nothing is manufactured on Oroboros. This is the fact that governs everything about how war is actually fought there, and it is the first thing Whitfield understands about the place that nobody else does.

There is no industry. There are no factories, no foundries running, no chemical plants, no refineries, no supply lines reaching back to anywhere, because there is no *back*. There is no agriculture, because there is no ground on the planet that has gone unfought long enough to grow anything and no season that a front has not crossed. There is no manufacturing base because building one would require exactly the sustained, undisturbed control of territory that the Scattering exists to prevent. Every object on Oroboros arrived there the way every person did — carried through by someone's death — and from the moment it arrived, the only thing that ever happens to it is that there is less of it.

The consequences run in a straight line, and they are the reason the world looks the way it does.

**Ammunition is finite.** A modern rifle is the single most effective weapon ever to arrive on Oroboros and the least sustainable. Every cartridge fired is gone. Every cartridge is replaced only by the cartridges taken off the dead of another modern force, which are themselves finite, which means that across a long enough span the total quantity of manufactured ammunition on Oroboros can only fall. It has been falling for a very long time. There are stretches of the planet where a working firearm is a ceremonial object, carried by whoever the unit's silent hierarchy has decided outranks the rest, loaded with eleven rounds that will never be replaced.

**Fuel is worse.** An armored column arrives on Oroboros with whatever it had in its tanks. Aircraft fly for as long as somebody can find something that burns and nowhere near as long as they need to. This is why the sky and the sea are contested in short, violent, decades-apart spasms rather than continuously — a naval engagement on Oroboros is what happens when two forces that have both been hoarding fuel for a century decide to spend it in the same week. The orbital layer visible on a clear night, faint flashes and long slow burns above the atmosphere, is the last remnant of crews from a war fought later in the century than any of the four have lived to see, still up there, still fighting, running on the last of whatever they came with.

**Everything decays.** Metal fatigues. Barrels wear. Optics fog and crack. Batteries die and are never replaced. Composite armor delaminates. There is no maintenance on Oroboros beyond what a soldier's narrow, wordless competence can perform with what he can find, and what he can find is other broken equipment.

This produces the single most counterintuitive fact about the planet, and the four take almost a year to accept it: **the older an army is, the more stable it is.**

A legion is not obsolete on Oroboros. A legion is *sustainable*. Iron can be reforged over a fire by men who remember how. A blade never runs out of ammunition. A shield wall requires nothing that cannot be scavenged from the dead of any century. Meanwhile, a modern mechanized force arrives at the peak of its power and begins immediately, irreversibly, to descend — dominant for a decade, formidable for fifty years, dangerous for a century, and after two hundred years an infantry unit carrying rifles used as clubs and knives taken off men who died fighting Rome. Every technologically advanced force on Oroboros is a window closing. The soldiers inside it cannot perceive this, because perceiving it would require a memory of the fifty years before, and they do not have one.

The oldest civilians call it the Long Decay, and they use the phrase about people at least as often as they use it about equipment.

**Food is the part the four are least prepared for.** Nothing grows. There is water — it still rains, because Oroboros is the Earth and the Earth still has weather — but there is no agriculture, no livestock, no fishery anyone can sustain, and no calories entering the system from anywhere. Soldiers do not appear to require food, or rather, they do not appear to notice its absence in any way that changes their behavior; hunger is simply another thing outside the narrow function their consciousness has been reduced to.

Civilians notice. Civilians retain everything a living person's body does, hunger included, and there is nothing on Oroboros to answer it with. What they scavenge — preserved rations off the dead of modern wars, grain from a supply cache four centuries old, whatever grows in the few places nothing has been fought over recently enough to sterilize — is never enough, has never once been enough, and is not intended by anything to ever be enough. The oldest Margin-Dwellers — the civilian population that lives in the gaps between fronts, and the subject of a later part of this account — do not describe themselves as looking for food. They describe themselves as looking for a reason not to think about it, which is a different search and easier to succeed at.

There is one reliable source of protein on Oroboros. It is walking around. It cannot be permanently removed from the world. It comes back. This is not a secret, it is not rare, and it is not discussed, and the civilians who do it do not consider themselves to have crossed a line, because the line stopped meaning anything several centuries before any of them arrived. Farro works out what she is looking at during their fourth month. It is the first time on Oroboros she is physically unable to keep speaking, and it is the moment Marcus stops thinking of the civilians as the people he is there to protect.

### 4.5 Consciousness and Coherence

Oroboros uses the word the living world would use — zombie — but it means something more specific by it than most people who use that word are picturing, and the four learn the actual definition slowly, the hard way, across their first weeks.

Physically, it holds. Every soul that claws back up out of the ground here wears a body in some real, ongoing state of decay — not always advanced, not always visible at a glance, but never fully arrested. A soldier who has fought for centuries looks it: skin gone wrong in ways no living medicine could explain, wounds that healed crooked because nothing on Oroboros heals any other way, the slow compounding wear of a body that keeps getting used past every point a living body would have given out.

What Oroboros adds, and what that first picture never captures, has nothing to do with the body at all. The rot that matters here is not decomposition. It is a mind and a fate caught in the same loop, over and over, with no ability to recognize the loop as a loop or step outside it for even a moment. A soldier fighting the same war he has fought ten thousand times, with no memory of the nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine before it, is undead in the only sense the word means anything here — not because his heart does not beat, but because nothing about him is capable of ending, changing, or becoming anything other than exactly what he already is. A civilian running the same cold arithmetic around the same fire in the ruin of the same kind of building, century after century, indistinguishable from every other version of herself she has ever been, is caught in precisely the same trap, even though her body may show none of the decay and her mind may remain, by any conversation anyone could have with her, entirely intact.

Both are zombies in the sense Oroboros actually means it: not defined by what is happening to their flesh, but by the fact that they are doing — thinking, fighting, surviving, suffering — the same thing they have always done, endlessly, with no exit built into any of it.

**Who arrives as a soldier.** Not every person who dies in a war becomes one of Oroboros's soldiers, and the rule that decides it is not about whether a man was good.

What Oroboros keeps, in the shape of a soldier, is a man for whom violence had become the truest thing left in him at the moment he died. That is a wider category than enthusiasm and a narrower one than participation. It includes the man who loved it, who felt more like himself with a weapon in his hand than he ever did without one, who wanted to be exactly where the killing was — that man arrives, and he arrives having offered everything Oroboros requires long before he ever got there. But it also includes, and mostly consists of, the conscript who never wanted any of it and had it worn into him anyway: three years in a trench, six years in a jungle, a childhood in a city under siege, until the part of him that used to be a farmer or a clerk or somebody's son had gone quiet and the part that knew how to kill was the only part still fully awake when the round found him. He did not choose it the way the first man chose it. He chose it the way a riverbed is chosen — a thousand small surrenders, each one reasonable, none of them the moment it happened.

What does *not* arrive as a soldier is the man for whom something else was still louder. The one who died with his hand on someone else's wound. The one whose last coherent thought was about his daughter, or the boy beside him, or getting the rest of them to the treeline. Not because he was clean — nobody who dies in a war is clean — but because the killing was never what he was made of, and Oroboros has no grip on anything except what a person actually was underneath.

This is not a moral filter and it does not sort the guilty from the innocent, a distinction Farro spends a great deal of her time on Oroboros defending against everyone including herself. Most of the men on that planet were nineteen. Most of them were sent. What arrived was not their guilt; it was what was left of them after their war had finished doing what wars do to nineteen-year-olds, and the fact that Oroboros can hold onto it is a statement about the war, not the man.

**Soldiers** retain consciousness, but consciousness with exactly one shape — the same shape it had before they died. A soldier can read a battlefield with total instinctive fluency: assess a firing position, operate a weapon he never touched in life, coordinate with the unit around him, recognize rank and give ground to it, adapt to captured equipment inside a single engagement. He speaks, but only the one language war ever gave him — orders, callouts, rank, position, threat. He can bark a command, report a contact, demand a password, warn a stranger off at gunpoint in words that are unmistakably words.

What he cannot do is anything outside that vocabulary. He cannot explain himself. He cannot be reasoned with about anything that is not the battlefield in front of him, cannot answer a question about who he used to be, cannot be talked out of an order once it has been given — not because the words fail to reach him, but because there is nothing behind them left to be reached. What remains is total fluency inside war and total absence outside it. It is not mindlessness or muteness. It is a mind narrowed to one function, executed perfectly, with nothing beyond it.

**Civilians** retain something categorically wider. A civilian who dies in a bombardment, a famine, or any of the machinery of atrocity that surrounds every war humanity has ever staged wakes on Oroboros still able to speak, reason, remember, plan, lie, and fear in every way a living person can. They do not become soldiers, do not fight as a rule, and gain none of the battlefield fluency a soldier has. What they keep instead is everything a soldier lost — reasoning, memory, calculation, self-interest, the full undiminished consciousness of a person, unmoored from any single function.

A civilian is not admitted the way a soldier is admitted. Nothing about a woman crushed under her own apartment building constitutes an offering, and Oroboros does not pretend otherwise. Civilians are not collected. They are *caught*. Oroboros is the ground, and the ground does not ask. Anyone who dies inside the reach of a war — inside the blast radius, inside the siege line, inside the famine a war produced three hundred miles from any battle — dies inside territory that has already been given over, and the territory keeps what falls on it. Soldiers are what Oroboros is owed. Civilians are what it happens to be standing under.

**What no one keeps is love**, and the reason is the single most important thing anyone on Oroboros could understand about the place.

It is not removed. Nothing on Oroboros is removed; the world has no mechanism for taking anything out of anyone. What it has is an absence of everything that would keep love alive. Love is not a possession a person holds; it is a process a person runs, and the fuel it runs on is a future — the belief, however small and however buried, that there is going to be an afterward and that the person you love will be in it. Oroboros has no afterward. It has not had one for longer than the human record reaches. Every civilian who has ever arrived there arrived carrying something, and every one of them, given enough decades, has watched it thin down into arithmetic without ever once being able to identify the day it finished.

Grief survives, in the shape grief takes when nothing underneath it is still warm — the fact of a loss registers, the way any fact registers to a mind capable of memory, and it does not hurt. A Margin-Dweller can tell you the names of everyone she has lost across four centuries, in order, with dates, and feel nothing at all while she does it, and be entirely aware that she is feeling nothing, and be unable to locate any part of herself that considers this a problem.

Two things follow from this that the four take a long time to put together. The first is that what civilians build in the ruins is not community; it is cooperation with everything that makes cooperation feel like anything scraped off. A dozen people will gather around a burning barrel because numbers improve the odds of finding food, water, or warning before a front arrives, not because any of them woke up wanting the others to be safe. They will share warmth without calling it kindness, watch each other's backs without calling it care, and register a member lost to a front's arrival as subtraction: one fewer set of eyes on watch, one fewer pair of hands for the next run.

The second is that no child has ever been conceived on Oroboros. It is not that nothing has ever been attempted — new arrivals still carry something for a while, and some of them try, and the oldest Margin-Dwellers watch them try with the flat patience of people who have seen it before. Nothing has ever taken, in all the time anyone has been counting. Whatever a body requires in order to make a future, the ground it is standing on does not have any. Oroboros is not sterile by design. It is sterile as a symptom.

Which is part, but only part, of why there are no children there.

It takes Farro longer than it should to notice, because absence is harder to see than presence, and by the time she says it out loud all four of them realize they had felt it for months without being able to name it. There are no children on Oroboros. None. Not among the soldiers, which makes a certain grim sense. But none among the civilians either, and children die in every war this world has ever fed on, in numbers no living person could hold in their head at once.

The explanation the oldest Margin-Dwellers have converged on, over centuries of asking, is not comforting and not clean: the ground holds what it can get a grip on, and a child has nothing yet for it to grip. Adult survival in wartime is ten thousand small compromises — the door not opened, the bread taken, the road walked past instead of down — and none of them are damnable and all of them are *purchase*. A child has not made any of them. There is nothing in a seven-year-old for the ground to hold onto, so the ground does not hold them, and they go somewhere else.

Where, nobody on Oroboros knows. It is the only question Farro never stops asking, and the only one to which every source she finds — Margin-Dweller oral history, the deep strata, the Ascended who have held command since before the pyramids — gives the same answer, which is silence.

### 4.6 What Suffering Means Where No One Dies

Killing on Oroboros is meaningless as an ending and total as a cruelty at the same time. It never removes anyone from the world; it only ever relocates them, unconsulted, disoriented, carrying whatever the last thing done to them was. For the kind of person willing to spend an afternoon on someone for no reason beyond enjoying it, Oroboros has removed the only consequence that act would carry anywhere else. There is no body to hide, because a body was never the point, and there is no permanent silencing, because permanence does not exist here for victim or killer alike.

What replaces death, for everyone it touches, is two entirely different shapes of suffering, one for each kind of soul the place holds — and each shape is nothing more than what that soul already is, worn down into its purest and most permanent form.

For a soldier, suffering is the war itself with no far side to it. Every living soldier who ever fought carried the promise, however distant, that the war would end — through victory, defeat, a wound bad enough to send him home, or death, which ends everything a soldier can be asked to endure. A soldier on Oroboros has none of those exits, not even the last one. He can be killed as completely as any living man, and the war will still be the next thing he wakes up inside, and the walk back to it will still be waiting. Some diminished, still-aware part of him seems to know this, the way an animal knows weather is coming — not as a thought he could put into the one vocabulary left to him, but as a texture underneath everything he does. This is the suffering built into what a soldier's consciousness on Oroboros already is: endurance with the stopping point removed, war become total identity with no self left underneath it to eventually go home to. It does not need a wound to hurt. Continuing is the wound, indefinitely renewed.

For a civilian, suffering keeps the shape it always had in every war fought on the living Earth, except that here it never resolves, and even the one relief death offers everywhere else turns out to be temporary. Hunger, thirst, disease, cold, exhaustion, the particular fear of watching a shelter get overrun for the second or the two-hundredth time — all of it real in every sense a civilian would recognize as real, all of it fully felt by a mind that never lost the capacity to feel it, and all of it interrupted only long enough to be relocated somewhere she has never seen and dropped back into the middle of it.

There is an asymmetry here that nobody on Oroboros discusses and Farro cannot stop discussing.

The soldiers — the men Oroboros keeps because violence was what was left of them — are anesthetized. Their punishment, if it is a punishment, is administered to a mind that has been narrowed until it can barely register the shape of what is happening to it. The civilians, who did nothing, who were standing in the wrong country, who were the *reason* the word atrocity exists, are kept fully conscious, fully articulate, fully capable of understanding every year of it, forever.

The worse sentence is being served by the people who did not earn one. Farro says this out loud, in their second year, and cannot make it fit anything she believes, and does not stop believing any of it, and never resolves the contradiction. It is the closest thing to an argument she ever has with God, and she loses it, and keeps working.

### 4.7 The Hierarchy Beneath the Silence

A soldier on Oroboros speaks — orders, callouts, warnings, the vocabulary war gave him and nothing more. What he cannot do is be formally commissioned, promoted, or given rank in any way a living military would recognize. And yet soldiers on Oroboros recognize rank among themselves, instantly, without a word of it ever being declared.

It is not insignia. Rank markings rot, get lost, get taken as trophies by whoever wins the next ground, and mean nothing on their own to a mind narrowed down to war. What soldiers recognize sits underneath the words they still have — a structure of authority that exists independently of any individual soldier's memory of the living military he came from, as if *army* itself survived the crossing as a shape even after every soldier's ability to name its parts did not. Within any unit, at any moment, there is a corporal, a captain, a general, whether or not anyone alive would recognize the insignia on them, and every soldier in that unit knows it the way a body knows which way is down.

Nobody has ever been formally promoted into that structure. You do not receive the rank. The rank receives you — accumulated out of nothing more than sustained proximity to fighting and a pattern of decisions the surrounding soldiers begin, without any of them choosing to, treating as command. It happens slowly enough that the person it is happening to rarely notices until it is done.

The structure does not check what you are. It does not check whether you are a soldier. It does not check whether you are dead.

This is the mechanism by which Oroboros's oldest civilians have, in isolated and catastrophic cases, come to command armies of hundreds of thousands. It is also the mechanism by which, in their fourteenth month, a stranded American colonel gives an instruction to nobody in particular in a ruined street outside what used to be Kraków, and forty men who have been fighting there since the fifteenth century turn to carry it out.

### 4.8 The Living

The four are the last living human beings on Oroboros, and it takes them eight weeks to learn what that costs.

They learn it through a technician named Osei Mensah, who came through the rift with them, survived the first night, survived the first month, and was killed on a supply run in the eighth week by a soldier who took him for the enemy because on Oroboros everyone is the enemy.

They wait for him. The Return takes days. They give it three weeks.

Nobody who belongs to Oroboros stays dead. The four do not belong to Oroboros. The ground has no grip on them — no debt paid in a life spent choosing violence, no accumulated purchase of a hundred wartime compromises, nothing the corruption underneath the world has ever been able to hold. A living person who dies on Oroboros dies the way people die everywhere else in the universe: once, completely, and gone.

Gone *where* is a question the four ask each other exactly once, in the eleventh week, and never again, because Farro is the only one of them with an answer and none of the other three are prepared to hear it yet.

The practical consequence reorganizes everything about how the four exist there. They are standing on a planet where death is a formality — where the men shooting at them have died ten thousand times, where torture is not an atrocity but a pastime because it cannot take anything permanently, where nothing that happens to anyone is final — and they are the only four things on it that can be ended. Every soldier they face has infinite lives. They have one each. The most dangerous thing Marcus ever stands in front of on that planet is a man who has been fighting since before the Renaissance, wearing four centuries of other men's armor and carrying a spear because the rifle he took a hundred years ago has been empty for ninety of them — and that man is, in the only sense that matters, *invulnerable*, and Marcus is a soft-tissue animal who can be killed by a bad fall.

There is a second consequence, and it is the one that eventually gets them out.

Oroboros cannot hold the living. Not just their bodies — anything about them. The corruption underneath the world operates by grip, and grip requires something already given, and the four have given it nothing. This is why the silent hierarchy is a *risk* rather than a certainty for Ashgrove: the structure reads him, but it does not own him. This is why what Farro is able to do for a handful of souls works at all, and why it does not work when anyone else attempts it. And this is why there is a door, at the end, when there should not be one.

The four's aliveness is not a resource. It is not a weapon and it cannot be spent on anything except once. But it is the single fact about them that Oroboros has no answer for, and everything they eventually accomplish there runs through it.

The rest of it is arithmetic none of them says out loud. Four living bodies need food, on a planet that has none, and water, which at least still falls. They eat what the Margin-Dwellers eat, minus the one thing the Margin-Dwellers eat that they will not, and they are hungry for four years without a single day's interruption. They age. They heal slowly and badly and sometimes not at all. Farro works out, in their first winter, roughly how long four people can go on this way, and does not tell the other three the number, and the number is why none of them ever seriously argues for waiting.

Whitfield puts it, sometime in the second year, in the flattest way anyone puts anything on Oroboros: *we're the only thing here that can still be used up.*

### 4.9 The Shape of the Map

Because Oroboros shares the living Earth's geography, its map can be read the way any war map can be read, and that is where the resemblance ends, because nothing about who controls what ground bears any relationship to where the ground sits or what century built it. A force from a war fought in the Pacific holds ground in the middle of Europe. A unit that died fighting mountains defends a coastline. Rome borders something built decades past anything the four recognize, which borders a stretch of trench line from 1917, with no transition and no explanation.

The four learn early that a region's danger cannot be predicted by guessing what era its ruins suggest. The ruins under their boots record what happened there once. They say nothing reliable about what is holding that ground now.

What does hold the map together, loosely enough for four people to navigate it, is the Long Decay and the Scattering working against each other. Modern forces are dangerous and shrinking. Ancient forces are sustainable and slow. Anything that starts to consolidate begins to bleed outward. The result is a planet with no stable borders, no permanent powers, and one absolutely reliable rule: whoever is currently strongest somewhere is, by that fact alone, already beginning to lose it.

Oroboros's war is not confined to the ground. Warships contest coastlines with no relationship to the wars that built them, when they can find fuel. Aircraft from a dozen eras of flight share airspace that was never built to hold all of them, in short brutal seasons decades apart. And above the atmosphere, faint and constant on a clear night, there is still a war going on — burning debris, the occasional silent flash of something large enough to see from the ground, crews from a colonial war fought later in the century than any of the four have lived to see, present for the same reason everyone else is.

To the people fighting up there, it is not the future. It is simply the war, exactly as present and exactly as total as the one a scattered fragment of the Ninth is still fighting on a road in Anatolia none of them remember arriving on.

No force is ever permanently secure, anywhere, for any reason. This is Oroboros's version of what every real war eventually teaches every occupier, sharpened into law: the ground was never anyone's. It was only ever on loan, for exactly as long as the war needed it to still look like a war.

## V. THE ASCENDED

### 5.1 The Two Doors

Every civilian who has spent long enough on Oroboros eventually arrives, whether they say it aloud or not, at the same choice. This world is not going to end. The war around their shelter is not going to stop, this decade or this century, because it cannot stop — the living world keeps feeding it. A civilian can spend that time as what Oroboros calls a **Margin-Dweller**: hiding, running, rebuilding, being scattered across the planet by every death and starting over among strangers, forever, at the edge of a war that will never once acknowledge they exist.

Or they can walk into it.

The oldest civilians on Oroboros — the ones who have watched this cycle for so many repetitions that the distinction between patience and madness has stopped meaning anything — are the only ones who ever choose the second door. Not out of ambition, in most cases. Out of exhaustion. There are only ever two options for a mind that refuses to simply stop: keep running from a war that cannot be outrun, or become something the war has to answer to.

### 5.2 What They Become

A civilian who commits fully enough, for long enough, to a soldier faction begins to be read by that faction's silent hierarchy the same way any soldier is. But the mechanism is not what an outsider would assume, and the assumption is what kills nearly everyone who tries.

A civilian gains no battlefield fluency. Ever. A Margin-Dweller who walks into a front is an amateur among men who have been fighting for four hundred years, and the overwhelming majority of them are killed in the first engagement and scattered to the far side of the planet, ending the attempt permanently. That is the ordinary outcome. It is why Ascended are rare enough that most soldiers on Oroboros have never been near one and most Margin-Dwellers believe they are a story.

What the rare survivor accumulates rank through is not competence. It is *planning*.

A soldier on Oroboros cannot form an intention that extends past the engagement in front of him. He can flank, suppress, hold, and withdraw, all of it perfectly, and none of it is a plan — it is fluency, executed inside a horizon that ends where the current fight ends. A civilian standing on that field is the only thing on it capable of thinking about next week. And when a civilian says *the road, tomorrow, before they reach the bridge*, and it works, something in the structure underneath every soldier who heard it registers a shape it has no other name for. Not competence. Command.

It accretes. Slowly, over decades, from decisions the surrounding soldiers begin treating as authority without any of them choosing to. And what results when a civilian mind — full language, full memory, full will, full capacity to intend — is folded into a structure built to recognize command instinctively and absolutely is not a general. It is something the oldest Margin-Dwellers name in the tone usually reserved for weather that kills: **the Ascended**.

An Ascended does not lead an army. An Ascended becomes the point where an army's entire silent hierarchy resolves, the way a compass needle resolves toward north.

They can plan on a scale no soldier faction ever could, because they still remember, in full civilian detail, everything a person is capable of remembering — logistics, geography, the layout of a city that stopped existing four hundred years ago, the names of everyone they have lost. And they command absolutely, because every soldier under them reads their authority as totally as they would read any general's.

An Ascended also, and this is the part that makes them genuinely dangerous rather than merely powerful, gets the Return. Rank is proximity to a war, and an Ascended is the most proximate thing on the field. The ground gives them back near their front the way it gives back any soldier — which means an Ascended can be killed and simply walks home, and the army is still there, and the hierarchy resolves toward them again the moment they are inside it.

There is a threshold in this, and it is the cruelest part of the second door. A civilian walking into a front has no Return and no protection; the ground only begins bringing them back once the hierarchy has started reading them as rank, which is also the exact point at which they have stopped being able to walk away. Nobody has ever been granted the Return and then declined the rest of it.

And the Scattering does not exempt them. An Ascended who wins too completely begins to lose their own Return alongside their army's, which is why no Ascended command has ever grown past the low millions and stayed there — and why the oldest of them have learned, without ever being able to put the reason into words, to stop short of victory on purpose. They do not know they are feeding anything. They know only that a war that ends is a war that takes them with it.

The largest Ascended commands, by the time Marcus first hears one of them named, number in the low millions.

### 5.3 The Cost

Nothing on Oroboros is free, and the price of becoming Ascended is the last thing that made a civilian a civilian.

The fusion is not stable. The longer an Ascended holds command, the more the totalizing single-function shape of the consciousness they have merged with bleeds into the mind that used to be whole. They do not become soldiers. They never lose language, never lose the capacity to reason on a scale no soldier retains. What they lose, slowly and by every account irreversibly, is *proportion* — the sense that any single life, including their own, weighs anything at all against the front.

An Ascended a century into command still sounds, in conversation, entirely human. What they say, with total and unshaken calm, is the kind of thing that stops being sayable by anyone still capable of being called a person.

The four do not meet the oldest ones until deep into their time there, and when they do, the horror is not that the Ascended is inhuman. It is that she remembers, in perfect and devastating detail, exactly what being human used to feel like, and has simply stopped being able to let that memory outweigh anything else.

She is also, and this is the part Marcus cannot get out of his head afterward, entirely kind to them. She has no reason not to be.

## VI. WHAT ELSE LIVES THERE

### 6.1 The Historical Register

Oroboros is not populated primarily by named figures from any specific war. Its overwhelming majority population is the vast anonymous weight of ordinary human history's ordinary dead: legionaries, medieval men-at-arms, Napoleonic conscripts, colonial-era soldiers on both sides of history's ugliest expansions, First and Second World War infantry from every theater and every flag, and the full weight of every conflict fought on the living Earth up through 2045, arriving with whatever they carried at the moment of their deaths.

The living Earth of 2045 has a stranger recent history than the one most of its citizens learned in school, and Oroboros carries all of it. Its map holds the dead of a war fought over an assassination attempt on a one-armed Russian arms dealer, the coup and civil war his death eventually bought his movement, a small nation called Urzikstan that has spent decades fighting off occupation under half a dozen different flags, and the year hijacked drone fleets turned two continents' skies into a battlefield neither side had declared.

None of it sits apart from the older wars. What is left of that ultranationalist uprising still contests a stretch of frozen territory a few valleys over from Wehrmacht and Red Army positions still fighting the actual Eastern Front exactly as it was actually fought — except that both sides ran dry a thousand internal years ago, and what is left of Stalingrad is fought with bayonets, entrenching tools, and the last rifles anyone has, held back for the one shot a year they are worth. Urzikstan exists as its own theater, layered with decades of guerrilla resistance under the same banners its founders carried, a border away from colonial regulars fighting a war that needed no strangeness at all to be brutal. The drone fronts of the twenties hold their own ground, autonomous systems running whatever last engagement they were mid-execution of when their operators died, their batteries three-quarters through a decay curve nobody up there can perceive.

What differs between the old fronts and the recent ones is texture, not weight. The oldest carry the particular grinding anonymity of history — nameless units, forgotten dates, soldiers whose only story is the uniform they died in. The recent ones carry sharper edges: named operations, factions whose grudges outlived every person who first felt them, and a handful of figures specific enough that even other soldiers seem to recognize the weight of who they are standing near.

Oroboros is an ocean, not a roster. A small number of sharply drawn figures rise out of an overwhelming anonymous mass, and the mass is the point.

### 6.2 The Undiminished

There is a third category, and it is the rarest thing on the planet that is still recognizably a person.

Oroboros keeps what a man actually gave. For most soldiers, what was given was the war — the killing, the belonging, the thing violence let them become — and so what Oroboros preserves is a mind narrowed to exactly that and nothing else. But a small number of men who died in wars never gave the war anything. What they gave, freely, over the whole course of a life, was their devotion to *themselves*: to their own judgment, their own certainty, their own right to decide. The war was only ever the instrument. The object of worship was the mind holding it.

Oroboros preserves what was given. In these men, what was given was the mind.

The Margin-Dwellers call them the Undiminished, and they say it the way you say the name of a disease. An Undiminished has a soldier's total battlefield fluency and no narrowing whatsoever. He can speak, argue, remember, lie, plan, justify himself at length and with feeling. He is, for practical purposes, a fully intact human being with several centuries of uninterrupted war behind him and absolutely no reason left to stop.

They are more dangerous than the Ascended, and there are fewer of them, and unlike the Ascended they did not have to give anything up to become what they are, because they arrived that way.

**Vladimir Makarov** commands what is known, among those who fight near it, as the **Inner Circle Remnant** — a splinter force still fighting the ultranationalist war he died in the middle of, no closer to its objective now than the day Price killed him. Makarov offers, three centuries in, no attempt at self-justification beyond raw nationalism, no ideology beneath the ideology, nothing resembling a sympathetic wound. He believes his war still matters. He has had all the time since his death to notice, dimly, that it does not, and he refuses the thought every time it surfaces, and that refusal is the entire content of what he is now. He is the purest available demonstration of what the Undiminished actually are: not men preserved by their convictions, but men whose convictions were always only ever a way of preserving themselves.

**General Shepherd** commands with the same cold operational competence he had in life, and his presence dramatizes Oroboros's cruelest irony: he still believes, or has convinced what remains of him to believe, that there is a chain of command above him worth serving, in a place with no command above anyone. What made Shepherd was never simple villainy. Thirty thousand of his men died in a single afternoon in a war the public moved on from inside a news cycle, and what Oroboros preserves is a general who decided, once, that no soldier under his command would ever again die that unremembered, and who was willing to engineer a war large enough to make sure of it. On Oroboros that conviction has had centuries to calcify with no living men left to protect, and he commands exactly the way he always did — certain that the scale of the sacrifice justifies the hand that orders it.

He is, for Ashgrove, the harder of the two mirrors: not a fanatic to be dismissed, but a competent and sympathetic officer who convinced himself that unaccountable command, exercised in the name of the men under it, was the same thing as protecting them.

**Raul Menendez** commands soldiers, the way any of the Undiminished do, and what he does with them is unremarkable. What is not unremarkable is the civilian population that has attached itself to the edge of his force, because nothing else on Oroboros has one. He did not arrive a monster. He was a war orphan who protected his twin sister Josefina through years of exploitation neither of them chose, and everything after — the cartel, the insurgency, the cyberterrorism, the decades spent turning a legitimate grievance into a justification for mass atrocity — traces back to the day an American operative's raid took Josefina and left him with nothing to protect. What holds those civilians is not belief. Nothing on Oroboros still has belief to give him. It is doctrine enforced through fear and repetition, the same cold arithmetic that builds every other civilian gathering on the planet, dressed in the borrowed language of a cause most of them arrived decades after the living world stopped remembering.

Menendez is the clearest available proof of exactly the pattern Farro comes to fear in herself and in the other three. A real wound, left untreated long enough, does not stay a wound. It becomes a reason.

Taken together the three sketch the same shape from three distances. Makarov is what conviction looks like with nothing left to convince. Shepherd is what protection looks like once it stops answering to anyone who could tell it no. Menendez is what grief looks like once it has had centuries instead of years to decide the world owes it a debt. Each demonstrates the same thesis from a different angle: the wars of the living world do not end when the fighting stops. They end when the *reason* stops mattering to the people who fought them — and on Oroboros the reason never gets the chance to stop mattering, because nothing here ever ends.

It is not the men who carried out the worst orders that Oroboros preserves as its clearest warnings. It is the men who were each, in his own way, once trusted with more authority than anyone should be trusted with unaccountably. Which is the exact shape of the temptation Ashgrove spends his entire time on Oroboros learning to recognize in himself before it finishes hardening into one of theirs.

There is an inverse of the Undiminished, and Oroboros holds two documented cases of it standing side by side.

**Simon Riley and Gary Sanderson** are on Oroboros, and neither of them is a soldier there.

They were killed by their own commanding officer, unarmed, at the end of an operation they had completed, and burned afterward to destroy the evidence. Whatever a man has to have in him at the moment of death for Oroboros to keep him as a soldier, neither of them had it — what was in them was betrayal, and the understanding of it, and it arrived far too late to be anything else. So they came through the way civilians come through: full minds, full memory, full awareness, and not one shred of the fluency that had made them two of the most capable men who ever wore a uniform.

They cannot fight. They can remember, perfectly, how.

They have been on Oroboros for three centuries. They know exactly who is here. They know exactly where he is, because he commands an army large enough to be visible from a ridgeline, and they have known for three hundred years, and they are two unarmed men on a planet where the only thing they were ever good at has been taken away from them and given to everyone else.

Marcus meets them in his second year. What he does about it is the single most consequential decision he makes on Oroboros that is not the last one, and there is no version of it that is clean.

### 6.3 The Deep Past

Beneath the historically legible layers — Roman, medieval, gunpowder, modern — Oroboros's oldest strata are barely human anymore.

There are things down in the deepest and most fought-over ground, the parts of the world that have changed hands more times than any mind could count, that no longer remember which war they were part of or whether they were ever really people in the way the word means anything. Their equipment is stone. Their language, when they have one, is not any language. They are the population of wars fought before wars had names, in numbers nobody has ever estimated because nobody has ever gone down far enough and come back.

What they are close to, without any of them being able to say so, is older than all of them, and the four's slow understanding of what that means arrives entirely through what these things do rather than anything they say.

### 6.4 What Else Was Made Here

Soldiers and civilians are what Oroboros is built from, but they are not the only things hunting through it, and the four learn this in the first weeks, long before anyone has a name for what they are looking at.

Some of it is the land itself gone wrong the same way everything else has. Animals that died in the blast radius of a battlefield, or never lived anywhere but one, twisted by the same corruption that keeps the rest of the planet from ever fully dying, into something with too many of the wrong instincts and none of the ones that used to keep it manageable. A pack of something that was once ordinary wildlife does not read as tragedy the way a broken soldier does. It reads as exactly what it is: proof that Oroboros does not limit its corruption to the creatures smart enough to have made choices about war.

Worse, and considerably more deliberate, is what the living world's own wars handed Oroboros directly. Not every atrocity committed against a human body in the name of military research stayed contained to the war that produced it. Secret programs on more than one side of more than one twentieth- and twenty-first-century conflict ran experiments on real people — prisoners, conscripts, the unlucky and the unwilling — chasing enhanced soldiers, biological weapons, or something closer to both, and more of those subjects died from what was done to them than any official record was ever going to admit. Their deaths were violent and they were connected to war in every sense that matters to the ground, and so they arrived here too.

What walked out of those programs was never fully a soldier and never fully a person to begin with, and Oroboros had no clean category to preserve. What it preserved instead is worse than either: something with a soldier's aggression and none of a soldier's limits, something that had a civilian's fear reflex bred or burned out of it before it ever crossed over, wearing whatever the science that made it was actually trying to build.

They are rare, concentrated in the specific stretches of ground where the programs that made them once operated, and they are without exception the worst thing the four ever face — not because they are stronger than an army, though some are, but because there is no fragment of the person they used to be left anywhere near the surface to protect, reach, or mourn. Whatever Marcus's instinct finds worth saving in a deteriorated soldier, it finds nothing at all to work with here, and the discovery that his instinct has a floor is one of the hardest things that happens to him on that planet.

Oroboros does not explain why it kept these things instead of discarding them the way it discards so much else. The likeliest answer, and the one Farro eventually settles on without ever being able to prove it, is the same one that explains everything else: it does not turn anything away that violence made, and it has never once been interested in whether what walks out the other side still resembles what walked in.

## VII. THE QUESTION UNDER THE QUESTION

### 7.1 Why These Four

Phantom Corridor's rift did not take everyone in the room, and it did not take them at random.

The seam underneath the world does not decide anything, because it is not the kind of thing that decides. It responds. What it responds to is concentration — the shape of a war present in one place at one time, the way a magnetic field responds to iron without any part of it choosing to. And a Phantom Corridor test chamber held one of the densest such concentrations that has ever existed inside a single room: the man who fights a war, the woman who arms it, the man who builds its infrastructure, and the man who directs it toward a purpose.

Not four soldiers. Oroboros has all the soldiers there are. A *war*, in miniature, whole and functional and standing eleven feet from an opening.

Fourteen people were taken. Four of them were that shape. Ten of the fourteen were dead inside three months and those four were not, and the correspondence between those two facts is exact, and is not a coincidence, and is the last thing about Oroboros any of them work out.

Farro is the one who notices what is missing, and she notices it late, and she never says it to the other three.

A war needs more than four functions. It needs someone to pay for it. It needs someone to explain to a population why it is necessary. Both of those people were also in the building that day — a comptroller and a public affairs officer, forty feet from the containment field, well inside the radius that took a technician standing further away.

Neither of them was taken.

Farro works out why in their third year, sitting in the dark in a ruin outside what used to be Lyon, and the answer is not complicated once she stops looking for it to be. Oroboros does not need those two. It has never needed those two. It has had a supplier and a justification for several hundred thousand years, and both of them are the same thing, and it is not on Oroboros.

It is on the other side, and it is us.

None of this is explained to the four. It surfaces in pieces — through Farro's research, through what the deep strata let slip without meaning to, through everything that only assembles once their time there is nearly over. One structural truth holds and is never softened: they were taken as a set, for what they represented, and not one thing about who any of them individually were entered into it. What that implies about what is doing the responding is left exactly where it lands.

### 7.2 What Was There Before the First War

Nothing on Oroboros predates the thing the oldest civilians will, if they trust you enough, only ever call **the First Light** — never by any of the names it has taken since, and never above a whisper.

It was not born on Oroboros. Every fragment the four eventually piece together agrees on one point: it came from the same Earth the living world stands on, and it has been there since before humanity had a word for war.

It was present at the first violence — watching, not causing, the way a coastline attends a tide it does not command. For an age longer than recorded history it did nothing else. It did not create the wars of the earliest humans. It attended them, and something in the repetition of it, killing answered by killing and grief curdling into the next killing, began slowly to give it shape.

It first showed itself in something like a body during a massacre between two prehistoric bands, appearing to the losing side as a radiant figure that ended the slaughter in a single motion no survivor could afterward describe in any consistent way, other than that it happened, and then it was over, and every enemy who had been advancing on them was simply dead. The survivors approached in terror and in something closer to worship, unable to look away and unable to explain why. One of them found the nerve to ask what it was.

It answered in three words that meant nothing yet to anyone standing there, because no human language existed to carry their full weight and would not for tens of thousands of years:

*I am the Sin, the Lie, and the Death.*

Only the last of the three landed the way it was meant to, because they had just watched it happen three hundred times in front of them and the word for that was already the oldest word they had. The other two waited — carried forward imperfectly through however many retellings, until they finally reached someone equipped to hear them as something more than sound.

That someone, when the four assemble the fragment deep into their time there, is Farro. She recognizes the phrase for what it is, not because anyone explains it to her, but because she has spent her whole life inside the tradition that gave those three words their weight, and she is the only one of the four for whom hearing them lands as recognition rather than noise.

It is also the moment her faith stops being a comfort, because what she recognizes is a name she knows attached to a scale she does not. The tradition that taught her that phrase taught it to her as one figure among a cosmology, subordinate, defeated in advance, operating inside a story with an ending. What she is standing on is a planet. What she does with the gap between those two things — whether she says it aloud to the other three, and what it does to her when she decides — is the hardest year she has on Oroboros, and she does not come out of it believing less.

It offered that first band a fraction of what it was. In exchange it asked for the only thing it has ever asked for since: to be worshipped. They did not understand what the word would come to cost, could not have understood it, and agreed anyway, without understanding, because it was easier than refusing something that had just saved their lives. It taught them how. That is where all of it began.

Every act of worship since — every altar, every sacrifice, every prayer offered by a soldier to whatever he called on before a battle, every civilization that has ever built its gods, knowingly or not, in a shape that rewarded violence — has fed something that was already old before the first of them was born. It did not need every war on Earth to become what it is. It needed enough of them, repeated long enough, by enough people who never knew what they were feeding.

There came a point, long before any war the living world keeps records of, when it stopped being present *at* wars and began, across an accumulation of centuries no human record reaches back far enough to date, to take the world itself over. It did not create anything. There was already an Earth — the same Earth, the only Earth — and what the First Light did was corrupt it: fold it, layer by layer, war by war, into a second and permanent state laid beneath the first, until enough of that Earth's capacity for violence had been given over that it was no longer a presence haunting human wars from outside, but the total shape those wars take once they cross into what it has made of the ground beneath them.

It is not a ruler who lives in that world. It is not a general commanding endless armies from some hidden seat the four might one day find and burn down. Oroboros is not a place the First Light occupies. Oroboros is what the First Light did to a world that was never its to remake and never stopped being the same world underneath what it became. The hatred that turns soldier against soldier, the hunger that never resolves, the Return that brings a man back to his war and the Scattering that takes it away from him — none of it is administered from anywhere. It is not a system anything runs. It is the shape of the ground.

This is the fact the four eventually have to sit with, and it changes everything about what they came believing they might do. There is no chamber to reach, no throat to cut, no single act of violence that ends this the way violence has ended things in every other war any of them were trained for. You cannot defeat a world. You can only, if you are very determined and very lucky, find a way to stop being inside it.

What makes it harder to sit with, once Farro follows the logic all the way down, is that the worship never stopped. It only stopped looking like worship, and it has only ever counted, then or now, when it was *chosen*.

That is the load-bearing rule underneath every other rule on Oroboros. The First Light has never taken anything. It cannot. What it has is several hundred thousand years of accumulated offerings, and every single one of them was handed over by someone who was, in whatever degraded and cornered sense the moment allowed, choosing.

A man who let killing become the truest thing in him, across three years or thirty, was making that offering the entire time, in installments, none of which felt like worship and all of which counted. Oroboros does not have to extract anything from him after he arrives, because there is nothing further to extract; what it preserves in him is the shape of a debt already paid in full. It cannot touch what he does inside that narrow function afterward — a mind with nothing left to choose with is not a mind capable of a further offering — but it never needed to.

What it can still touch, actively and continuously, is a civilian's arithmetic. A civilian retains every faculty required to choose otherwise and chooses the war's terms anyway — not because anything forces it, but because it is easier, and safer, and has kept her alive this long. Every one of those choices is small. None of them is worship. All of them count.

And it can touch an Ascended most of all, because an Ascended is the one kind of soul on that planet who retains everything a person needs to know exactly what they are doing and does it regardless: holds a command structure together through fear and totalizing certainty, with the full unbroken awareness of what fear and certainty cost the people underneath, and keeps holding it, freely, because the alternative is giving up the one thing centuries ever gave them back.

That is worship in every sense the First Light has ever recognized the word — not ignorance mistaken for devotion, but a wanting mind choosing the war's terms with its eyes open, the same as the band that knelt the first time understood exactly what kneeling meant and knelt anyway.

Its power is not something it has. It is something handed to it, choice by choice, and it has never once been able to take a thing that was not given.

Which is the whole of the reason there is a way out at all.

Nobody on Oroboros states outright what the First Light is, and nothing forces the word. What the fragments add up to is a shape anyone paying attention will recognize — the same being, or something answering to the same hunger, that human memory has reached for under other names, in other places, long after this one had already finished its work on the world underneath the world.

### 7.3 Why the War Never Ends

The mechanical answer is the Return, the Scattering, and the Long Decay. That is what actually moves the front lines.

The harder answer sits underneath it, and it is the one that matters by the time the four understand it.

Oroboros is not a closed system being fed from outside. It is fed *by* the living world, continuously, for as long as the living world keeps making war. Every death Marcus witnesses on that planet is, in some sense, a death that happened because human beings on the world he came from — a world in 2045 that has itself been at war for three years and counting, for reasons that fit comfortably on one page and would not persuade anyone reading them cold — have never once stopped fighting each other long enough to let the reservoir run dry.

The most damning fact underneath all of it is not that something ancient and monstrous built this. It is that it did not have to build anything at all. It only had to corrupt what was already there, and then wait.

The First Light is not the architect of Oroboros standing apart from its own creation, because Oroboros was never a creation. But corrupting a world does not mean being its only cause. Every soldier who wakes there, every civilian caught in the margins, every fresh war that keeps the supply from running dry, is still made by living human choices nothing underneath the ground ever has to lift a hand to cause. It does not need to reach into the living world and start a war. It only has to exist, patiently, on the other side of every war humanity was always going to start on its own, and wait for what it is made of to arrive.

Humanity has been filling it, unassisted, since long before Phantom Corridor opened a door, and is filling it now, in 2045, for reasons that have nothing to do with anything watching from underneath.

The place does not have to trap the four. Reality does that on its own, every day, on the world they are trying to get back to. Oroboros simply makes sure that once it does, there is never again a way to stop.

### 7.4 What Rescue Actually Means

Farro is the one who eventually gives this a shape the other three can hold onto, and it is entirely her shape — drawn from her own faith, offered as what she believes rather than as anything anyone on Oroboros could confirm.

In her reading, every soul on that planet arrived already dead in a state her faith would call sin. Not because war made them wicked, but because death in the middle of hatred, terror, or violence is rarely a clean way to leave the world, and Oroboros is where every one of those unclean departures collects. She is careful, once she starts saying any of it aloud, never to say it to anyone not ready to hear it that way, and far more careful never to let it curdle into the belief that anyone she meets deserved to end up there. She does not believe that. She fights it hardest in herself, and she loses that fight at least twice, and she says so.

What she believes, quietly and without needing anyone to agree, is that whatever can be done for the people trapped in Oroboros is not a reward for their conduct in life or in death. It is mercy extended past the point anyone earned it, the same as it would have to be for anyone, anywhere, including her.

What the four eventually become capable of doing for a handful of the people they meet is real, and smaller than it looks, and Farro is the one who insists on saying so whenever any of the other three start believing otherwise.

The mechanism, as far as any of them ever understand it, is not power. It is the absence of grip. Everything on Oroboros is held by something it once gave. The four gave nothing, which means the ground has nothing to hold them with, which means there is a small and specific space around a living person where the rules of that world are — not suspended, never that, but *unenforced*. Where the Return does not have to happen. Where a soul standing close enough to something the ground cannot get hold of can, occasionally, if the moment is exactly right and if something in that soul is still capable of wanting it, let go.

A soul freed from Oroboros is freed *from that place.* It stops fighting a war that was never going to end on its own. It stops being made to relive the worst moment of its existence forever. That much, Farro will say, is simply true, whatever anyone believes about what waits afterward.

What she refuses to claim is that this is salvation in the fuller sense her own faith means by the word. Nothing the four do decides what happens to any of those souls next, and she says this to herself long before she says it to anyone else: she is not in the business of judging anyone, and she does not believe herself capable of saving anyone in the way only God, in her faith, is understood to save. What the four can do is open a door out of the suffering. What waits past that door is not hers to know.

The other three never fully take up her language, and nothing about them requires that they should. What stays with them is her conviction that none of it runs through the four themselves — that nothing about Marcus's growing willingness to help her, and nothing about her own courage, is what actually does the freeing. In the way she puts it, it is grace, moving with them because they are living, because they came from a world where grace is still possible in a way it no longer is for anyone already inside Oroboros. The other three come to trust that claim less because they are persuaded of the theology and more because they have watched what happens, over and over, when she acts on it and it works.

Each of their three roads there looks nothing like the others, moves at its own pace, and is never guaranteed to arrive.

## VIII. THE SHAPE OF THE STORY

### 8.1 The First Night, and What Comes After

They arrive together, in the middle of it, dropped into the same shattered stretch of warzone as the rest of the fourteen the rift pulled through. What greets all of them at once is chaos in its most total form: gunfire with no discernible front, structures burning on both sides of a street none of them recognize, ground unstable in ways that have nothing to do with any war the living world has ever fought.

Four of the fourteen do not survive the first hour. Marcus does not learn until much later that this is the only kind of dying anyone he knows will ever do there.

In the scramble for shelter the group comes apart, and Marcus spends the worst six hours of his life to that point moving through a burning street trying to find the rest of it. He finds Farro first, which decides more about the next four years than either of them understands at the time. They find Whitfield before dawn. Ashgrove has already found six others and organized them, because that is what Ashgrove does inside ninety minutes of anything.

By sunrise there are ten. By the end of the first month there are five, and one of them is a technician named Osei Mensah, who has eight weeks left.

What actually shapes their time on Oroboros is everything after that first night: learning what this world is, what it wants, what it did with the people they lost, and how four people who cannot come back are supposed to survive somewhere that was never built to let anyone leave. The driving questions are *what is this place* and *how do we get home* — and, once it is clear the rift no longer exists to be reopened, *what does going home even mean, and what is each of us willing to do to make it happen.*

Marcus's version of that question is narrower than the others and takes him longer to hear. He does not, at any point in four years, ask whether he can get out. He asks who he is getting out.

### 8.2 The Fracture

The four's survival does not resolve their differences. It exposes them, slowly, across the middle years, and the argument that finally breaks something is not about how to get home. It is about what they owe the place.

Whitfield wants to build. He is the only person on the planet who can, and he knows it before anyone tells him, and the first structure he raises — a fortified position outside a Margin-Dweller settlement, using salvage and about four hundred hours of work — saves eleven people and gets him noticed. Within a year there is an Ascended who knows his name. Within two there are three. Whit's argument is that the only thing worth doing here is the thing nobody else on the planet is capable of doing, and that building a door home means building, which means building other things first, which means being useful to people it is dangerous to be useful to.

Farro wants to understand the place before anyone touches anything, certain that acting on incomplete knowledge is exactly what stranded them, and increasingly unable to explain to the other three that her research has stopped being about getting home and become about something she cannot yet defend out loud.

Ashgrove wants what he has wanted since the second week, and in the fourteenth month the planet gives it to him.

He gives an instruction in a ruined street outside what used to be Kraków — not to anyone, just the reflex of a man who has been giving instructions for twenty-five years — and forty soldiers who have been fighting there since the fifteenth century turn and carry it out. Correctly. Immediately. Without a word.

The silent hierarchy does not check whether you are dead.

Everything after that is downhill in a direction that takes the rest of their time there to travel. Ashgrove is not a fool and does not believe he can carry an army home through a door that no longer exists; he understands that Oroboros was never a vault Phantom Corridor could lock. What he cannot stop is smaller and much worse. He is a living man on a planet of the dead who has discovered that the dead will obey him, and every single time he uses it, it is to keep the other three alive, and every single time it works, and he is better at it than he has ever been at anything.

By the third year he commands, in the loose and terrible way anyone commands anything there, somewhere between two and three hundred. He has never given an order that was not defensible. He has also stopped being able to imagine a version of the walk home that does not have several hundred dead men walking in front of it, and the reason he cannot imagine it is not strategy. It is that he does not want to.

Marcus is the one who has to say it, because Farro will not and Whitfield has his own compromise to defend. And when he says it, he discovers, standing in the middle of it, that his objection does not survive contact with his own record — that he has spent three years doing a smaller version of the same thing, that the men Ashgrove commands are men Marcus has fought beside and stopped seeing, and that the only real difference between them is that Ashgrove gives orders and Marcus stands in front of people, and Oroboros has never once cared about the difference.

This costs something real. Not a villain turn — the slow, resisted, partially successful argument of two men who have each spent a whole life believing that protection justifies the hand that administers it, confronted by a world that has been running that experiment for several hundred thousand years and can show them the results.

### 8.3 What Ending Looks Like Here

There is no final confrontation in the sense the four were trained to expect one, and no victory in any sense the living world would recognize.

The First Light cannot be found in a single location and cannot be killed, because it is not a combatant standing inside Oroboros. It is what Oroboros *is*, and nothing four people can carry can end a world the way a soldier ends an enemy. Oroboros does not fall. It cannot be unmade by four people, or four hundred, or any number the living world could ever send, because what would have to be undone is not a structure sitting on top of the world. It is the world. What ends Oroboros, if anything ever does, is not written into anything the four are capable of doing.

That is not their story. Their story is smaller, and it is the one that was always actually theirs to finish.

There is a way out, and it is not engineering.

Whitfield spends two years on the assumption that it is — that the seam can be reopened from below with enough salvage and enough of Farro's mathematics, and he gets further than anyone should be able to, and it is not close and it was never going to be. The seam is not a device. It was never Phantom Corridor's to build, and a man with a workshop in a ruin cannot rebuild what was not built.

What can be done is the only thing that has ever been done on that planet, by anyone, since the first band knelt in a clearing and did not understand the terms.

The ground opens for what is freely given. It has never worked any other way. It has no mechanism for taking, no capacity to compel, and no interest in anything handed over under duress or in ignorance — the entire architecture of Oroboros runs on offerings, and an offering is only an offering if the one making it knows exactly what it costs and makes it anyway.

The four have exactly one thing the ground has never been given.

A living life. Not a soul it already owns, not a debt already paid, not one more soldier walking back to one more front. The one currency several hundred thousand years of accumulated worship has never once collected, offered by someone who understands precisely what it costs, freely, with a full and undiminished mind, in a world where nothing else is undiminished.

It opens. Once. Long enough for what is left to walk through, into a world that has moved five months since the containment field failed and has not finished processing the paperwork on any of them.

The account does not record what was said in the last hour before it, because the three who came back have never repeated it, and the one who stayed is not available to ask. What is recorded is the shape: that it was not a sacrifice made in a moment of chaos or a wound taken protecting someone in a firefight, because Oroboros would not have accepted that and does not accept anything it did not understand itself to be given. It was decided in advance, over days, out loud, by four people arguing, and it was not the argument any of them expected to have, because more than one of them wanted it.

Whatever any of them managed to do for anyone else on Oroboros before then was never an ending for the place and never everything even for the person it was done for. Each of their capacities ran through the same thing that shaped everything else about them. Marcus's ran through protection, because that was the one thing about him Oroboros was never able to narrow down to nothing. Farro's ran through the door grace has always opened for her, moving with her because she was living. Whitfield's ran through structure — not individuals, necessarily, but the same instinct that held a struggling family together for over a decade, turned toward whatever on that planet could still be held together instead of ground down. And Ashgrove's was the most dangerous to earn, because it could only ever come from the same instinct to command that the rest of his time there taught him to fear in himself, which meant whatever good it eventually did anyone had to run through him redirecting that instinct rather than defeating it.

None of it was everything. Oroboros holds, afterward, essentially everything it held when they arrived: the endless front still moving, the Ascended still commanding what is left of their millions, the Undiminished still exactly what they always were, the First Light still patient and unkillable and fed by a living world that has not stopped making war just because three of its people found a way out.

What came back is not a victory. It is a small number of people who are no longer there, and a much larger number who are, and the knowledge — which none of the three find easy to live with — that they left behind uncountably more than they were ever able to reach.

Simon Riley and Gary Sanderson are among the ones who are no longer there. What Marcus did about that in his second year is the reason, and it was not clean, and he never described it to anyone.

What settles, in the end, is smaller and more human than anything about the world it happened in: whether the ones who came back came back as the people who arrived, or as something Oroboros spent four years trying to bend them into; what it cost each of them to choose, at the end, not to become that; and whether Elena Farro made it back to a five-year-old boy who had already lost one parent and had spent five months being told, gently and by people who believed it, that he had lost the other — five months for him, four years for her, which is its own kind of answer to the question of what any of it cost — or whether love, having refused to stop trying for four straight years in a place built specifically to wear it down, does not always get to win just because it never stopped.
